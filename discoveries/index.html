    Discoveries

    What we’ve found

    Most of what Project Scroll publishes was known to someone before us; we say where. These are the exceptions: facts found in the original records by Michael Urbanski for Project Scroll that we could not find in any published history, article or website. Each shows the record it came from, links to the story where it is set out with its source, and lists what we searched before calling it new.

    Kinds of finding. New record: a fact in no published account. Earliest record: the first appearance in print, earlier than any account gives. Correction: the record says otherwise than the accounts. Connection: two records that had not been put together. Identification: an unnamed source named. Map reading: a fact read from a map. Compilation: a list that did not exist. Myth tested: a story traced and found to have no early source.

    1. History of Cass County, Michigan (1882), p. 356. Internet Archive.

      Sister Lakes Connection

      The 1882 county history records a warning chalked in red on a peeled tree at the fork of the old road to “Dewey’s Lake”: “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.” No telling of the 1964 Dewey Lake Monster that we found mentions it.

      In The True Story of the Dewey Lake Monster: The Week the Valley Went Wild · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 23 October 2026

      What we searched

      Wikipedia’s Dewey Lake Monster article; Robert Atkinson’s survey of the 1964 papers (2017); Visit Southwest Michigan’s haunted-places list; the Observer’s lake-monster article; web searches for the red-chalk sign with Dewey Lake.

      Link to this finding

    2. Silver Creek Township plat, about 1928.

      Sister Lakes Map reading

      Read against the township plat, the warning pointed one way. Coming up the old track from Indian Lake to the south-east corner of Section 16, a traveler bore left, to the north-west, to reach Dewey’s Lake. The left-hand road, the one the warning sent “to the Devil,” was the road to Dewey’s Lake.

      In The True Story of the Dewey Lake Monster: The Week the Valley Went Wild · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 23 October 2026

      What we searched

      The 1882 history’s account of the fork (p. 356); the plat of Silver Creek Township of about 1928; every telling of the red-chalk sign and the Dewey Lake story we could find.

      Link to this finding

    3. Licking Valley RegisterCovington, Kentucky17 September 1847

      Vandalia Correction

      The raiders’ own home paper, the Covington Licking Valley Register of 17 September 1847, put their bail at “something over four thousand dollars.” Every later account we have found gives $2,600, the 1882 county history’s figure.

      In The Kentucky Raid of 1847 · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The county histories of 1875, 1882 and 1906; the House Divided project’s narrative of the 1847 stampedes; D. G. Chardavoyne’s article in The Court Legacy (2004); Wikipedia; the Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages; the Clio entry; the State Bar of Michigan’s milestone pages; web searches for the bail figure.

      Link to this finding

    4. Licking Valley RegisterCovington, Kentucky17 September 1847

      Vandalia Correction

      The same 1847 Kentucky account says the raiders’ baggage wagon, its wheels taken, “was then delivered up.” The earliest telling we know of the story that it was rolled into Birch Lake is in newspapers of 1936–37, and no account we have found sets the two side by side.

      In The Kentucky Raid of 1847 · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The county histories of 1875 and 1882, searched for “wagon”; the House Divided narrative; Wikipedia; the society’s pages; web searches for the wagon and Birch Lake.

      Link to this finding

    5. History of Cass County (1882), p. 251. Internet Archive.

      Vandalia Earliest record

      The earliest description we have found of the building now called the Bonine carriage house: in 1882, “exactly opposite his residence, which is on a corner,” “the finely-built storehouse,” with two rows of arbor vitae running from it. The history says nothing of fugitives there.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house and carriage house; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entries; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; web searches for Bonine with “storehouse” and “arbor vitae”.

      Link to this finding

    6. Siebert, The Underground Railroad (1898), Appendix E. Internet Archive.

      Vandalia Correction

      The Bonine named in Wilbur Siebert’s national directory of Underground Railroad workers (1898) is Isaac, the father: “Bogue, Steven. Bonine, Isaac. Shugart, Zachariah.” Modern accounts credit his son James E., and none we have found cites Siebert.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The same sources as for the storehouse, with web searches for Siebert with “Isaac Bonine”.

      Link to this finding

    7. Glover, Twentieth Century History of Cass County (1906), p. 386. Internet Archive.

      Vandalia Connection

      Glover’s history of 1906 places the meeting of the Anti-Slavery Friends of Cass County, with the Rev. Charles Osborn as pastor, “in a log building in the grove on the farm of the late James E. Bonine … known as the Elk Park.” Modern accounts say only that the Bonines were members.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The same sources as for the storehouse, with web searches for the Anti-Slavery Friends with Bonine and Elk Park.

      Link to this finding

    8. Michigan Farmer, 18 August 1885. Internet Archive.

      Vandalia Earliest record

      The earliest eyewitness account of the Bonine elk park, in the Michigan Farmer of 18 August 1885, says two elk “went to Victor Emanual, the King of Italy, … to grace the Royal Park,” with no warship, against the seven elk and “war vessel” of the 1882 county history. The same visitor describes a 40-acre park behind a ten-foot fence, ten elk and thirteen deer, the elk coming to the call “Come Bill!”, and a large fountain and flagstone walks before the house.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house, carriage house and Quaker timeline; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entry; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; Internet Archive full-text searches for Bonine with elk, Italy and “Victor Emmanuel”; Italian sources on the wapiti of La Mandria, none of which names Michigan.

      Link to this finding

    9. East Jordan News-Herald“A herd of 12 to 15 elk”8 October 1953

      Vandalia New record

      The Bonine Elk Park outlived its founder by half a century. Isaac A. Bonine wrote to the federal Biological Survey in 1907 that he had bred elk “for about thirty-five years”; a 1908 newspaper reports elk sold at $100 a head to parks in Detroit and Grand Rapids; the WPA’s 1941 guide to Michigan found “more than a score of American elk” there, sold “to zoos, parks, and private gardens, and sometimes to Elk lodges”; and in October 1953 a newspaper photograph shows 12 to 15 elk still on the park. That was the year the farm left the family: G. Elwood Bonine’s 1976 obituary says the Elk Park Farms were in the family “from 1837 to 1953.”

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house, carriage house and Quaker timeline; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entries; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; Internet Archive full-text searches for “Bonine Elk Park” and “Elk Park farm”; the Biological Survey bulletins; the WPA guide; the Friends of Silverbrook obituary transcriptions.

      Link to this finding

    10. Genealogy of the Sharpless FamilyPhiladelphia1887

      Vandalia New record

      The dates missing from modern accounts of the Bonines: James E. Bonine was born in Wayne County, Indiana, on 5 September 1816 and married Sarah Ann Bogue at Cassopolis on 8 August 1844; she was born in Preble County, Ohio, on 30 April 1825 and died at Vandalia on 17 September 1909. The Pioneer Society roll printed in 1906 gives James E. as coming to the county in 1841, a year before his father, not in 1843.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house, carriage house and Quaker timeline; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entry; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; the Sharpless (1887), Bogue (1944) and Hannum (1911) genealogies; Indiana Quaker meeting abstracts; Glover’s 1906 history, p. 352, checked against the page image.

      Link to this finding

    11. History of Cass CountyChicago1882, p. 258

      Vandalia New record

      James E. Bonine was superintendent of the Air Line railroad through Penn Township, giving “three years time, $6,000 right of way, and $6,000 cash,” by the 1882 county history. The line was the Michigan Air Line, opened through Vandalia in 1871. No modern account of the Bonine House we have read mentions it.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house, carriage house and Quaker timeline; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entry; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; web searches for Bonine with “Air Line” and railroad.

      Link to this finding

    12. The American Friend, vol. 4 (1897), p. 308. Internet Archive.

      Vandalia New record

      James E. Bonine, the builder of the Bonine House, died at his home in Niles on 18 February 1897, “in the eighty-first year of his age,” after an illness of four months, by his obituary in the Quaker weekly The American Friend. It says he built “principally one house for worship” and helped build three others. No modern account we have read gives the date; a 1906 Berrien County history gives 1898.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house, carriage house and Quaker timeline; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entries; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; Find a Grave (a bot check stopped us) and Geni (a title only); Internet Archive full-text searches for James E. Bonine with died, funeral and 1897; the Friends of Silverbrook obituaries; Glover’s necrology.

      Link to this finding

    13. Bulletin de la Société d’acclimatation (Paris, 1871), p. 500. Internet Archive.

      Vandalia Connection

      The Italian side of the Bonine elk story has a record of its own. In 1871 the naturalist of the King of Italy’s park of La Mandria reported that the king had sent the Count di Castiglione “to America to buy wapiti,” that the first shipment of 60 landed 47 alive, and that the American elk kept dying through 1873. No account of the Bonine sale we have read connects it with the king’s buying in America.

      In The Bonine House at Vandalia · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County’s pages on the house, carriage house and Quaker timeline; eight Leader articles of 2009–2021; the Clio entries; local radio and television features; Wikipedia; Italian accounts of La Mandria (Rivista Savej, Fondazione 1563, Wikipedia); Internet Archive full-text searches for wapiti with Mandria and Castiglione.

      Link to this finding

    14. Indiana American, Brookville, 27 Feb. 1846. Hoosier State Chronicles.

      E Clampus Vitus Earliest record

      The earliest dated mention of E Clampus Vitus yet found anywhere: a letter from Connersville, Indiana, printed in the Indiana American of Brookville on 27 February 1846, which speaks of “the ‘Eclampsus Vitus’ society, an order of which, we have in our town.” The order’s own histories give no dated record before 1847.

      In E Clampus Vitus: the complete record · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      Full-text searches for “Eclampsus Vitus”, “Eclampses Vitus” and “Clampus Vitus”; Hoosier State Chronicles; Internet Archive; Wikipedia’s articles on E Clampus Vitus and Ephraim Bee and their references; ecvinc.org and chapter websites; Then and Now (E Clampus Vitus, 1966); the Shumate list of early lodges as given by the Julia C. Bulette chapter; HMDB; the 1888 Biographical and Historical Record of Vermillion County, Indiana, which dates the order there to about 1873.

      Link to this finding

    15. Indiana State Sentinel, Indianapolis, 16 July 1846. Hoosier State Chronicles.

      E Clampus Vitus New record

      An Indianapolis lodge in July 1846: the Indiana State Sentinel notified “the members of Tent No. 43 of the order of Eclampees Vitus” to meet in their hall on 16 July, “attired in full regalia.” No history of the order mentions Indiana.

      In E Clampus Vitus: the complete record · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The searches listed for the Connersville letter, with web searches for the Sentinel and “Clampus”, and the Indiana State Library’s newspaper pages.

      Link to this finding

    16. Journal of the House of Representatives of Indiana, 1846–47. Internet Archive.

      E Clampus Vitus New record

      A lodge that asked a state legislature for a charter: House bill No. 242 in the Indiana House of Representatives, winter 1846–47, “to incorporate Jackson Lodge of the ancient order of Eclampses Vitus, of Centreville, Indiana,” was put off with a joke.

      In E Clampus Vitus: the complete record · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The searches listed for the Connersville letter, with searches for “Jackson Lodge” with Clampus and Centreville, and the Indiana State Library’s archives catalogue.

      Link to this finding

    17. 61 places · 1845–1905Texas to HawaiiThe register

      E Clampus Vitus Compilation

      A register of 61 places where E Clampus Vitus is on record before 1905, each with its earliest surviving record: the “complete list” of early lodges that the order’s historians wished for in 1966. The nearest earlier effort we know is Shumate’s list of about thirty places.

      In E Clampus Vitus: the complete record · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      Then and Now (1966), which names about fourteen places; the Shumate list as given by the Julia C. Bulette chapter; ecvinc.org and chapter websites; Wikipedia and its references; HMDB.

      Link to this finding

    18. San Joaquin RepublicanStockton, California14 April 1852

      E Clampus Vitus Earliest record

      The earliest dated California notice of the order yet found: the San Joaquin Republican of Stockton, 14 April 1852, calling members of the E. C. V. to their hall. The dates usually given are September 1852, or an undated record of 1851.

      In E Clampus Vitus: the complete record · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      Then and Now (1966); Wikipedia, which cites the Daily Alta California of 3 September 1852; the Sacramento Union of 28 September 1852; ecvinc.org and chapter websites; web searches for the Stockton notice.

      Link to this finding

    20. Carey & Lea, map of Michigan Territory, 1822. MSU Libraries.

      Kalamazoo Identification

      The unnamed “atlas of 1823” that first spelled the river “Kikalemazo” is, we believe, Carey & Lea’s American Atlas: its map of Michigan Territory, dated 1822 and reissued in the atlas of 1823, letters the river “Kikalemazo R.”

      In How Kalamazoo got its name · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Kalamazoo Public Library’s pages; Wikipedia’s “Etymology of Kalamazoo” and its source, Gannett’s Origin of Certain Place Names (1905); local news sites; web searches linking Carey & Lea and “Kikalemazo”.

      Link to this finding

    21. Durant, History of Kalamazoo County (1880). Internet Archive.

      Kalamazoo Myth tested

      The story that Titus Bronson was fined for stealing a cherry tree has no 19th-century source we can find. It is not in the Pioneer Collections of 1877, Durant’s county history of 1880 or the Compendium of 1906, though each tells of Bronson and the change of name.

      In How Kalamazoo got its name · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The three histories named; the Kalamazoo Public Library’s Bronson biography; the City of Kalamazoo’s page; Wikipedia; local news sites that repeat the story.

      Link to this finding

    22. Coolidge, Twentieth Century History of Berrien County (1906), p. 142. Internet Archive.

      Niles Earliest record

      The explanation of the name Niles, for Hezekiah Niles of the Baltimore Register, enters the county histories only in 1906, with Coolidge, seventy-seven years after the plat. The detailed history of 1880 gives no reason for the name.

      In How Niles got its name · found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026

      What we searched

      The Berrien County histories of 1880 and 1906; Wikipedia’s Niles article; the Niles History Center; web searches for when the explanation first appears in print.

      Link to this finding
