    • Breads · South Bend, Indiana (St. Joseph County), 1909 printing (first published 1901)

      Corn Bread

      Take 1 pint bolted corn meal, 1 pint wheat flour, 1 pint thick sour milk, 2 eggs, ½ cup sugar, butter the size of an egg. Stir all together, then add 1 teaspoonful soda stirred in a little milk. Bake in a moderate oven.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister Mell E. Wenger, South Bend, Ind.

      Making it today

      Mix 2 cups fine cornmeal, 2 cups flour, 2 cups buttermilk, 2 eggs, ½ cup sugar and ¼ cup melted butter. Stir 1 tsp baking soda into 2 Tbsp milk and add it last. Pour into a greased 9×13 pan and bake at 375°F for 25–30 minutes. 'Bolted' meal is sifted, finer corn meal; 'thick sour milk' is closest to buttermilk.

    • Breads · Walkerton, Indiana (St. Joseph County), 1909 printing (first published 1901)

      Corn Meal Doughnuts

      Take 2 cups of sifted corn meal, 1 cup of white flour, 1 tablespoonful of sugar, 1 teaspoonful of salt, 3 eggs, 1 cup of sour or buttermilk, and 1 teaspoonful of soda dissolved in the milk. Drop from a spoon into hot lard. Fry brown on both sides and serve warm with molasses.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister Mary Hilderbrand, Walkerton, Ind.

      Making it today

      Whisk 2 cups cornmeal, 1 cup flour, 1 Tbsp sugar and 1 tsp salt. Beat 3 eggs with 1 cup buttermilk and 1 tsp baking soda, and stir into the dry mix. Drop by tablespoons into oil or lard heated to 350°F and fry 2–3 minutes, turning, until brown. Drain and serve warm with molasses or sorghum. These are more like fritters than ring doughnuts.

      Breads · Dowagiac, Michigan (Cass County), 1909

      Graham Nut Bread

      One quart of lukewarm water, 1 quart graham flour, 1 compressed yeast cake, 1 cup English walnut meats (ground or broken in small pieces) 1 cup brown sugar, 1 teaspoon soda, ½ teaspoon salt, 2 quarts wheat flour. In the evening mix the water, graham flour and compressed yeast cake together, stirring until well mixed. In the morning put in walnut meats, sugar, soda, salt and white flour. Knead for 5 or 10 minutes and let rise. Mold into three loaves; let stand until light. Bake in a moderate oven. This may be molded twice which will make the grain of the bread finer.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. W. F. Judd, Dowagiac

      Making it today

      An overnight sponge bread. Evening: stir together 4 cups lukewarm water, 4 cups whole-wheat (graham) flour and one 0.6-oz cake of fresh yeast (or 2¼ tsp active dry). Cover and leave overnight. Morning: stir in 1 cup chopped walnuts, 1 cup brown sugar, 1 tsp baking soda, ½ tsp salt and about 8 cups all-purpose flour (add it gradually; you may not need it all). Knead 5–10 minutes, let rise until doubled (about 1 hour), shape into three loaves in greased 8×4 pans, let rise again until light, and bake at 350°F about 40–45 minutes. Halve everything for a more manageable batch. A 'compressed yeast cake' is the old fresh-yeast cake; 'soda' is baking soda.

      Celery Soup

      Cut a bunch of celery and boil it thoroughly one hour. Add one quart of chicken broth, one cup sweet cream, pepper and salt.

      From The Three Rivers Cook Book (reprint, with supplement), King's Daughters Society · Mrs. S. A. Walton

      Its story today: Kalamazoo grew celery for a century

      Making it today

      Chop one head of celery and simmer it in a little salted water about an hour, until very soft (blend it smooth if you like). Add 4 cups chicken broth and 1 cup heavy or light cream, heat through without boiling, and season with salt and pepper.

      Soups · Goshen, Indiana (Elkhart County), 1909 printing (first published 1901)

      Potato Soup

      Take 4 large potatoes, pare, and cut into small squares; boil in about 2 quarts of water until thoroughly done; season with salt, pepper, and butter the size of an egg. A little parsley added greatly improves it. Serve with crackers.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister John S. Swartz, Goshen, Ind.

      Making it today

      Peel and dice 4 large potatoes and simmer in 8 cups water (or use half broth) until very soft, about 25 minutes; mash some against the pot to thicken. Season with salt and pepper, stir in ¼ cup butter and a handful of chopped parsley, and serve with crackers.

      Soups · South Bend, Indiana (St. Joseph County), 1909 printing (first published 1901)

      Tomato Soup

      Place over the fire 1 quart of peeled tomatoes. Stew them soft, with a pinch of soda. Work through a sieve so no seeds remain. Set over the fire again, and add a little sugar to suit your taste. Then add a pint of good sweet milk with a large teaspoonful of flour mixed in it, stirring it constantly till it boils. Then add salt and pepper, and butter the size of a walnut. Serve hot over crackers. Canned tomatoes may be used.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister S. F. Sanger, South Bend, Ind.

      Making it today

      Simmer 4 cups peeled tomatoes (or a 28-oz can plus a little water) with a pinch of baking soda (it tames the acid so the milk will not curdle) until soft, about 15 minutes; press through a sieve or blend. Return to the pot, add sugar to taste, then whisk 1 heaping tsp flour into 2 cups milk and stir it in; bring just to a boil, stirring. Season with salt and pepper, add 1–2 Tbsp butter ('size of a walnut'), and serve over crackers.

    • Main dishes · St. Joseph, Michigan (Berrien County), 1909

      Escalloped Spaghetti

      Break the spaghetti into small pieces and cook tender by boiling hard in salted water about 15 minutes, after which drain. Butter a baking dish, cover the bottom of same with a layer of cracker crumbs, which have been rolled fine. Sprinkle this layer with pieces of butter, continue with a layer of spaghetti, then a layer of cooked tomatoes sprinkled with generous pieces of butter and salt and pepper. Continue in this manner until all the spaghetti is used, ending with pieces of butter covered with cracker crumbs. Bake in a moderate oven 45 minutes.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. Ada N. Platt, St. Joseph

      Making it today

      Break ½ lb spaghetti into short pieces and boil in salted water until just tender (about 10 minutes for modern pasta); drain. Butter a 2-quart dish and cover the bottom with fine cracker crumbs dotted with butter. Layer spaghetti, then stewed or canned tomatoes (about a 28-oz can in all), dotting each tomato layer with butter, salt and pepper; repeat, and finish with butter and crumbs. Bake at 350°F for 45 minutes.

    • Main dishes · Cassopolis, Michigan (Cass County), 1909

      Hamburg German Style

      Grind round beef in food chopper, add 1 egg, salt pepper and onion to suit the taste. Have ready bread stuffing made as you would for poultry. Butter gem pans, add a layer of steak then bread and lastly steak. When Baked turn out on individual plates and garnish with parsley. They retain their shape and are nice served at afternoon lunches.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Rebecca B. Woods, Cassopolis

      Making it today

      Mix 1 lb ground beef with 1 egg, salt, pepper and a little minced onion. Make about 2 cups of your usual bread stuffing (bread cubes, butter, onion, celery, sage, moistened with broth). Butter a 12-cup muffin tin; press a thin layer of beef in each cup, then a spoonful of stuffing, then another layer of beef. Bake at 375°F about 25 minutes, until the meat reaches 160°F. Turn out and garnish with parsley. 'Gem pans' are muffin tins.

      Main dishes · Kalamazoo, Michigan, 1906

      Planked White Fish

      Take a hardwood plank of well seasoned maple or oak about 1 1-2 feet long, 10 or 12 inches wide and 2 inches thick. Pour boiling water over it before using, until heated through. Split the fish open, bone, season well, lay upon the plank and bake. If the fish seems too dry, slices of salt pork may be placed upon the fish while baking. The fish should be served on the plank which may be garnished with rock salt or sprigs of parsley. The plank gives a peculiar delicious flavor which distinguishes it from fish baked in the ordinary way. Before serving cover fish with maitre de hotel sauce.

      From New Crumbs of Comfort: A Cook Book, compiled by the Ladies of St. Luke's Church · Mrs. Chas. A. Peck

      Making it today

      Soak an untreated oak, maple or cedar cooking plank, then heat it in a 400°F oven. Lay a boned, butterflied whitefish skin-side down on the hot plank, season, top with a few strips of bacon if you like, and bake at 400°F for 15 to 20 minutes, until it flakes. Serve on the plank with lemon-parsley butter (maître d'hôtel butter).

      Main dishes · Kalamazoo, Michigan, 1906

      Rabbit Fricassee

      Joint 2 carefully dressed rabbits, and place in a large dish of cold water with a handful of salt. Soak for 2 hours. Remove and rinse with fresh water, cover the bottom of a kettle with strips of salt pork (about ¼ of a lb.). When it is fried brown, slice on it a large onion and toss till light brown. Now add the jointed rabbit and stir till all are seared over; then add 1 qt. of boiling water, 1 tsp. salt and a pinch of red pepper. Place on the back of the range and simmer gently for 2 hours, or until perfectly tender. ... This is an excellent and economical dish in early winter when rabbits are in good condition.

      From New Crumbs of Comfort: A Cook Book, compiled by the Ladies of St. Luke's Church · Florence S. Wattles

      Making it today

      Brine two cut-up rabbits in salted water for 2 hours, then rinse. Brown 1/4 lb diced salt pork or bacon in a Dutch oven, add a sliced onion until golden, then sear the rabbit. Add 4 cups boiling water, 1 teaspoon salt and a pinch of cayenne, cover and simmer on low heat 1 1/2 to 2 hours until tender; thicken the broth for gravy and serve with currant jelly.

      Vegetables · Kalamazoo, Michigan, 1906

      Blue Grass Corn Pudding

      For quart pan size: 12 ears corn; split corn down the grain (do not cut grains off cobb), then scrape; mix well with corn; 3 eggs well beaten, 1 lump butter, size egg, ½ teaspoonful of salt, just a little sugar, pepper to taste. 1 pint sweet milk. In medium hot oven, cook 25 minutes (till top of pudding is well brown). Serve as vegetable.

      From New Crumbs of Comfort: A Cook Book, compiled by the Ladies of St. Luke's Church · Roger Hanson Peters

      Making it today

      Score down each row of kernels on 12 ears of fresh sweet corn and scrape the pulp and milk into a bowl (about 4 cups). Stir in 3 beaten eggs, 4 tablespoons melted butter ('size of an egg' is about 1/4 cup), 1/2 teaspoon salt, a pinch of sugar, pepper and 2 cups whole milk. Bake in a buttered 1 1/2- to 2-quart dish at 375°F for 25 to 35 minutes, until set and well browned on top.

      Vegetables · Marcellus, Michigan (Cass County), 1909

      Escalloped Parsnips

      Boil the parsnips tender in salted water with one medium sized red pepper. Drain off water and steam dry. Slice parsnips very fine. Put a layer of parsnips in a baking dish, dust over the layer rolled cracker crumbs and again layer of parsnips until baking dish is within 1 inch of being full. Put 1 teaspoon of brown sugar in 1 cup of sweet cream and pour over the parsnips. Bake 1 hour. Six medium sized parsnips will serve 8 to 10 people.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. Anna Walter, Marcellus, Chairman Civic and Town Improvement Com. M. S. F. W. C.

      Making it today

      Peel 6 medium parsnips and simmer them in salted water with one small dried or fresh red chili until tender, 15–20 minutes. Drain, let steam dry, and slice thin. Layer in a buttered 2-quart baking dish with crushed saltine crumbs between the layers, stopping an inch from the top. Stir 1 tsp brown sugar into 1 cup heavy cream and pour over. Bake at 350°F for about 1 hour, until bubbling and browned. Serves 8.

    • Vegetables · Three Rivers, Michigan (St. Joseph County), 1909

      Potato Entree

      Slice thin 3 medium raw potatoes into a baking dish, slice 1 medium sized onion. Put in a layer of the potatoes mixed with the onion, a few cracker crumbs, cover with cheese sliced thin. Repeat this process until the dish is full, then pour over all a little milk. Bake slowly one hour.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. L. L. Tyler, Three Rivers

      Making it today

      Thinly slice 3 medium potatoes and 1 onion. In a buttered 1½-quart dish, layer potatoes and onion, a sprinkle of cracker crumbs, salt and pepper, and thin slices of cheese (mild cheddar or Colby); repeat until full. Pour about ¾ cup milk over the top, cover, and bake at 325–350°F for 1 hour, uncovering for the last 15 minutes to brown.

      Vegetables · Dowagiac, Michigan (Cass County), 1909

      Stuffed Peppers

      Six young green peppers, 1½ cups minced meat of any kind, 1 cup bread crumbs, ¾ cup cream or stock, 1 tablespoon melted butter, 1 teaspoon minced parsley, ¼ teaspoon onion juice, ¼ teaspoon salt. Parboil the peppers for 5 minutes, then fill and put in a pan with ½ cup stock or ½ cup water and 2 tablespoons butter and bake about ¾ of an hour. Baste frequently with stock.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. W. F. Judd, Dowagiac

      Making it today

      Cut the tops from 6 green peppers and seed them; boil 5 minutes and drain. Mix 1½ cups finely chopped cooked meat (leftover roast, ham or chicken), 1 cup fresh bread crumbs, ¾ cup cream or broth, 1 Tbsp melted butter, 1 tsp chopped parsley, ¼ tsp grated onion with its juice and ¼ tsp salt. Fill the peppers, stand them in a baking dish with ½ cup broth and 2 Tbsp butter, and bake at 350°F about 45 minutes, basting now and then.

      Pies & cakes · Cassopolis, Michigan (Cass County), 1909

      Chocolate Cream Cake

      One cup sugar, ½ cup butter, ½ cup sweet milk, 2 eggs, 1½ cups flour, 1 teaspoon baking powder. Bake in 2 layers. Cream for above.— Two cups granulated sugar, ¾ cup sweet milk, piece of butter size of an egg. Boil 15 minutes. Then beat until stiff and spread on cake. Melt 2 squares of chocolate, 1 cup sugar, 1 egg, 3 tablespoons of milk. Cook and stir until thick, flavor with vanilla and spread on the cream.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Rebecca Woods, Cassopolis

      Making it today

      Cake: cream 1 cup sugar with ½ cup butter, beat in 2 eggs, then add 1½ cups flour sifted with 1 tsp baking powder alternately with ½ cup milk. Bake in two greased 8-inch round pans at 350°F for 20–25 minutes. White 'cream' filling: boil 2 cups sugar, ¾ cup milk and ¼ cup butter ('size of an egg') to the soft-ball stage (about 236°F), cool slightly, beat until thick and spread between and over the layers. Chocolate topping: cook 2 oz unsweetened chocolate, 1 cup sugar, 1 egg and 3 Tbsp milk over low heat, stirring, until thick (do not boil hard), add 1 tsp vanilla and spread over the white layer. A 'square' of chocolate is 1 oz.

      Cream Peach Pie

      Line a pie tin with rich paste, peel, halve and seed peaches enough to fill the pan, mix two tablespoons of flour with one cup of sugar and sprinkle over the peaches, fill the pan with thick sweet cream and bake until done. Canned peaches may be used or apples cut in small pieces.

      From The Three Rivers Cook Book (reprint, with supplement), King's Daughters Society · Emma Masser

      Its story today: The fruit belt and its great market

      Making it today

      Fit a 9-inch pie plate with pastry and fill it with peeled peach halves, cut side up. Mix 1 cup sugar with 2 tablespoons flour, sprinkle over the fruit, and pour in about 1 cup heavy cream. Bake at 425°F for 15 minutes, then 350°F for 30–40 minutes, until the cream is set and lightly browned; cool before slicing.

    • As printed, 1909

      Pies & cakes · Constantine, Michigan (St. Joseph County), 1909

      Fruit Cookies

      One cup half butter and half lard, 1½ cups light brown sugar, 3 eggs beaten separately, 1 full cup rich sour cream, 1 even teaspoon soda dissolved in cream, 2 teaspoons cinnamon, 1 teaspoon cloves, 2 teaspoons nutmeg, 1 cup seeded raisins chopped fine. Mix very soft.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. E. W. Keightly, Constantine

      Making it today

      Cream ½ cup butter and ½ cup lard (or all butter) with 1½ cups light brown sugar. Beat in 3 egg yolks, then 1 cup sour cream mixed with 1 tsp baking soda, the spices (2 tsp cinnamon, 1 tsp cloves, 2 tsp nutmeg) and 1 cup chopped raisins. Fold in the 3 whites, beaten stiff. Stir in flour (about 3 to 3½ cups) only until a soft dough forms. Drop by spoonfuls on greased sheets and bake at 375°F for 10–12 minutes. The book gives no flour amount; 'mix very soft' means just enough to hold a shape.

      Pies & cakes · Three Rivers, Michigan (St. Joseph County), about 1896

      Marlborough Pie

      Pare tart, mellow apples, quarter and take out seeds, stew in a little water until soft enough to put through a sieve. To twelve tablespoons of apple put same of sugar, six spoons melted butter, one-half pint of milk, the juice and rind of one lemon, some nutmeg. Bake in one crust, use whipped cream if you like it.

      From The Three Rivers Cook Book (reprint, with supplement), King's Daughters Society · Mrs. C. H. Harris

      Making it today

      Cook tart apples into a smooth sauce; measure 3/4 cup and mix with 3/4 cup sugar, 6 tablespoons melted butter, 1 cup milk, the zest and juice of a lemon and a grating of nutmeg (most cooks today also add 2–3 beaten eggs so the custard sets). Pour into an unbaked 9-inch crust and bake at 425°F for 10 minutes, then 350°F for 35–45 minutes until set. Top with whipped cream.

    • Pies & cakes · Paw Paw, Michigan (Van Buren County), 1909

      Sunshine Cake

      Whites of 7 fresh eggs, yolks of 5, 1 cup of granulated sugar, 1 cup flour, ¼ teaspoon of cream tartar and a pinch of salt. Sift sugar and flour 5 times. Beat yolks of eggs, set aside. Add salt to whites of eggs, beat about half, add cream tartar and beat very stiff. Stir in the sugar slowly, add beaten yolks and beat well. Fold flour into the eggs carefully, and flavor to taste. Put in a tube pan and in the oven at once. Bake 40 to 50 minutes. Icing for cake.— Yolk of 1 egg, 2 tablespoons of cream, thicken with XXXX powdered sugar.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. Fannie Parker Woodman, Paw Paw

      Making it today

      A golden sponge cake. Sift 1 cup sugar and 1 cup cake flour separately, several times each. Beat 5 yolks until thick and set aside. Beat 7 whites with a pinch of salt until foamy, add ¼ tsp cream of tartar and beat to stiff peaks. Beat in the sugar a spoonful at a time, then the yolks; gently fold in the flour and 1 tsp vanilla or lemon. Turn into an ungreased 10-inch tube pan and bake at once at 325°F for 40–50 minutes. Cool upside down. Icing: beat 1 yolk with 2 Tbsp cream and enough powdered sugar to spread (or skip the raw yolk and use a little more cream). 'XXXX' was the grade name for fine powdered sugar.

      Apple Fritters

      Two egg, well beaten, one-half teaspoon salt, one cup milk, two and one-half cups of flour, two tablespoons of sugar, two tablespoons baking powder, four apples thickly sliced; flavor with vanilla. Fry as you would fried cakes and serve hot with maple syrup or a lemon sauce.

      From The Three Rivers Cook Book (reprint, with supplement), King's Daughters Society · Mrs. F. H. Case

      Its story today: Apples and cider today: the fall orchards

      Making it today

      Whisk 2 eggs, 1 cup milk, 2 tablespoons sugar, 1/2 teaspoon salt and a little vanilla; stir in 2 1/2 cups flour and 1 tablespoon baking powder (the original's 2 tablespoons is a lot for modern double-acting powder). Fold in 4 sliced apples, drop by spoonfuls into oil heated to 350–365°F, and fry until golden, 3–4 minutes. Serve hot with real maple syrup.

      Black Pudding and Sauce

      Black pudding—½ cup butter, 1 cup sugar, 1 cup sour milk, 1 teaspoon soda, 1½ cups fruit, 4 eggs, 1 teacup of molasses, 2 cups of flour. Steam two hours. Sauce—½ cup butter, 1 cup sugar, 2 tablespoons flour. Rub thoroughly together. Add 1½ cups water and boil to a cream.

      From The Evening News (Benton Harbor), December 22, 1897 · Miss Belle Kidd, Benton Harbor

      Making it today

      A steamed molasses fruit pudding (no blood; 'black' is from the molasses). Pour into a greased 2-quart pudding basin, cover with foil and steam on a rack over simmering water for 2 hours. Sauce: rub ½ cup butter, 1 cup sugar and 2 Tbsp flour together, whisk in 1½ cups water and simmer, stirring, until creamy. A 'teacup' is about ¾ cup.

      Puddings & desserts · Kalamazoo, Michigan, 1906

      Boiled Huckleberry Pudding

      Measure ¾ pt. of sifted flour; add to it 1 teaspoon baking powder, ½ salt spoon of salt. Sift thoroughly together. Stir a well beaten egg into ½ pt. milk, and stir this gradually into flour, making a smooth batter. Then stir in 1½ cups of berries; turn into a buttered mould, and steam, in enough boiling water to partly cover the mould, for 1 hour. Serve with hard or liquid sauce.

      From New Crumbs of Comfort: A Cook Book, compiled by the Ladies of St. Luke's Church · Mrs. A. K. Edwards

      Its story today: Blueberry season on the calendar

      Making it today

      Sift 1 1/2 cups flour with 1 teaspoon baking powder and 1/8 teaspoon salt; beat 1 egg into 1 cup milk and stir in to make a smooth batter, then fold in 1 1/2 cups huckleberries or wild blueberries. Pour into a buttered 1 1/2-quart pudding mold or heatproof bowl, cover tightly with foil, and steam on a rack in a covered pot of simmering water (halfway up the mold) for 1 hour. Serve warm with hard sauce or a sweet sauce.

      Buttermilk Delda

      Let 2 quarts of buttermilk come to a boil, sweeten, add 4 tablespoonfuls of flour moistened with sweet milk, and stir till thick. To be eaten warm or cold.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister Sarah A. Crowl, Nappanee, Ind.

      Puddings & desserts · Sturgis, Michigan (St. Joseph County), 1909

      Cherry Pudding

      Butter size of an egg, 3 tablespoons sugar, 1 egg, 1 cup sweet milk, 2½ teaspoons baking powder. Use flour enough to make a batter as you would for any dumplings. Take small teacup of cherries (fresh or canned), drain off the juice, put in a pint bowl and pour the batter over. Set in a steamer and steam about 1 hour. Sauce. — Take 1 tablespoon melted butter, 2 tablespoons sugar, ½ tablespoon flour. Add a little cold water and enough cherry juice to make the sauce. Cook until thick. This recipe will serve 4 persons and is also excellent with huckleberries instead of cherries and served with sweet cream as a sauce.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. Elizabeth Grobhiser, Sturgis

      Making it today

      Cream ¼ cup butter with 3 Tbsp sugar, beat in 1 egg and 1 cup milk, then about 2 cups flour sifted with 2½ tsp baking powder — a thick dumpling batter. Put ¾ cup drained pitted cherries (or blueberries) in a buttered 1-quart heatproof bowl, pour the batter over, cover with foil and steam on a rack over simmering water about 1 hour. Sauce: cook 1 Tbsp melted butter, 2 Tbsp sugar and 1½ tsp flour with a splash of water and about ½ cup cherry juice, stirring, until thick. Serves 4. 'Butter size of an egg' is about ¼ cup; a 'teacup' is roughly ¾ cup. The book's 'pint bowl' is small for this much batter — use a quart bowl.

      Dessert (fruit with maple jelly)

      Put in sherbet glasses enough fruit (any kind or kinds desired, fresh or canned) with nuts to half fill glasses. Make a jelly by taking 1 cup maple syrup and 1 cup water. Thicken with cornstarch and pour over fruit, filling glasses about ¾ full. Cover with whipped cream and serve.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. Jas. P. Ryan, Bangor

      Making it today

Cool a little, pour over the fruit to fill the glasses about three-quarters, chill until set, and top with whipped cream.

      Puddings & desserts · Nappanee, Indiana (Elkhart County), 1909 printing (first published 1901)

      Fruit Potpie

      This can be made of canned or fresh fruit of any kind; if apples, pare as for pies; cherries should be pitted. For the dough sift together 1 quart of flour and 2 teaspoonfuls of baking powder, add lard size of a hulled walnut, pinch of salt, 1 pint of lukewarm water. Roll, cut in squares; butter the bottom of your cook kettle, put in some fruit, sprinkle sugar and a little flour over it, put in a bit of butter, then your dough, and so on until all is in; then put on stove and pour enough boiling water on it to cover nicely and not burn it. Boil 20 minutes or till done. Serve with sweetened milk and a little cream mixed.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister Sarah A. Crowl, Nappanee, Ind.

      Making it today

      Mix 4 cups flour, 2 tsp baking powder, a pinch of salt and 2 Tbsp lard or shortening; stir in about 1½ cups warm water to make a firm dough (add it gradually). Roll ⅛ inch thick and cut in 2-inch squares. Butter a Dutch oven and layer about 4 cups sliced apples or pitted cherries, sprinkling each layer with sugar, a little flour and dots of butter, then a layer of dough squares; repeat. Pour in boiling water just to cover, cover the pot and simmer about 20 minutes, until the dough is cooked through. Serve in bowls with sweetened milk and cream. In this Pennsylvania-Dutch style, potpie means boiled dough squares, not a pie with a crust.

      Puddings & desserts · Three Rivers, Michigan (St. Joseph County), 1909

      Graham Pudding with Foam Sauce

      Graham Pudding. One-half cup butter, 1 cup molasses, 2 eggs, 1 cup milk, 3 cups graham flour, raisins, spices and salt. Steam 3 hours. Foam Sauce. One cup sugar, ½ cup butter, 2 eggs. Beat the yolks of eggs, sugar and butter until smooth. Scald 1 pt. milk in a double boiler, and stir in the ggs, sugar and butter. When this thickens stir in lightly the whites of the eggs which have been beaten to froth. Flavor with vanilla.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Mrs. W. H. Burke, Three Rivers

      Making it today

      Pudding: melt ½ cup butter and mix with 1 cup molasses, 2 eggs and 1 cup milk. Stir in 3 cups whole-wheat flour, 1 tsp baking soda (the book names none; without it the pudding is very dense), ½ tsp salt, 1 tsp cinnamon, ½ tsp each cloves and nutmeg and 1 cup raisins. Pour into a greased 2-quart pudding basin, cover tightly with foil and steam on a rack in a covered pot of simmering water for 3 hours, topping up the water. Sauce: beat 2 yolks with 1 cup sugar and ½ cup butter. Heat 2 cups milk in a double boiler, stir in the yolk mixture and cook, stirring, until it coats a spoon (160°F). Fold in the 2 whites beaten to soft peaks and 1 tsp vanilla. Serve warm over the pudding.

    • Preserves & pickles · South Bend, Indiana, 1917

      Apple Butter With Cider

      It usually takes about equal quantities of sweet cider and peeled and sliced apples to make butter the right consistency. In other words, two gallons of sweet cider should be boiled down to one gallon, and two gallons of peeled and sliced apples should be added to it either uncooked or as apple sauce. Two of the essentials of making good apple butter are long, slow cooking (four to six hours) and constant stirring. If sugar is used, it should be added after the cooking of cider and apples is about two-thirds done. ... Apple butter is spiced according to taste, about a half teaspoonful each of ground cinnamon, cloves and allspice being used for each gallon.

      From South Bend News-Times ("Canning Lesson" column)

      Its story today: Apples and cider today: the fall orchards

      Making it today

      Boil 2 quarts fresh sweet cider down to 1 quart, add 2 quarts peeled, sliced apples, and cook low and slow, stirring often, for several hours (a slow cooker or a 300°F oven with the lid ajar works well) until thick enough that it doesn't separate. Add about 1/2 to 1 cup sugar two-thirds of the way through if you like, and a pinch each of cinnamon, cloves and allspice at the end. Refrigerate, or can following current USDA guidance.

      Orange Conserve

      Slice 3 oranges and 1 lemon through the rind, add ½ cup of water to each cup of fruit and cook ½ hour. The next day to each cup of pulp add 1½ cups sugar and cook ½ hour. This quantity fills 8 jelly glasses.

      From Michigan State Federation Cook Book (Michigan State Federation of Women's Clubs) · Cora Tullis Reed, Benton Harbor

      Making it today

      Slice 3 oranges and 1 lemon very thin, peel and all (remove seeds). Measure, add ½ cup water per cup of fruit, and simmer 30 minutes; let stand overnight. Next day measure again, add 1½ cups sugar per cup, and simmer, stirring often, about 30 minutes, until it reaches 220°F and sets on a cold plate. Ladle into hot sterilized half-pint jars and process 10 minutes in a boiling-water bath (the old 'jelly glasses' were sealed with wax). Makes about 8 small jars.

      Preserves & pickles · South Bend, Indiana (St. Joseph County), 1909 printing (first published 1901)

      Pickled Beets

      Wash and trim the tops from as many beets as desired; drop them into hot water and boil rapidly until they can easily be pierced with a broom straw; then peel and quarter or slice as preferred; have ready vinegar enough to cover them, place on the fire and heat thoroughly; add 3 tablespoonfuls of sugar to each quart of vinegar; salt and pepper to taste. Let come to a boil, then pour over the beets.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister Clara Kreighbaum, South Bend, Ind.

      Making it today

      Trim beets, leaving an inch of stem, and boil until a skewer slides in easily (30–45 minutes). Cool, slip off the skins, and slice or quarter. For each 4 cups cider vinegar add 3 Tbsp sugar plus salt and pepper; bring to a boil and pour over the beets in clean jars. Refrigerate and use within a few weeks (for shelf storage, follow a tested canning recipe). A 'broom straw' was the cook's cake tester.

      Chocolate

      Grape Juice

      10 lbs. Concord grapes, 2 qts. water. Put on stove, and cook until grapes burst open, then put in cheese cloth bags; let drain, and squeeze a little. Add to juice 2 coffeecups of sugar, and let boil 5 minutes; or until sugar is all dissolved. Put up in Mason cans.

      Simmer 10 lb stemmed Concord grapes with 8 cups water until the skins burst, about 10 minutes, then strain through a cheesecloth-lined colander or jelly bag, pressing lightly.       Take 2 cups of granulated sugar, ½ cup of milk, butter the size of a walnut, and Baker's chocolate to suit the taste. Boil until it hardens in water. Beat and put in buttered tins. Cut in squares.

      From The Inglenook Cook Book (Brethren Publishing House; recipes from Sisters of the Brethren Church) · Sister Gladie Swartz, Goshen, Ind.

      Making it today

      Popcorn Candy

      Taking care of the ice chest

      Keep the ice chest clean; wipe up anything spilled in it and, when necessary, wash it out with hot water, using two tablespoonfuls of washing soda to each gallon of water; rinse and dry thoroughly. ... Keep the ice compartment well filled. This is economy in the long run. Do not cover the ice with ice blankets, newspapers, or cloth. These prevent the ice melting, but make the ice chest less cool.

      Making it today

      The same idea works for a modern refrigerator or a camp cooler: keep it clean and full, and open the door as little as you can. A few tablespoons of baking soda in warm water is a gentler way to wash it out than washing soda.

    Old kitchen words

    The old cooks wrote for readers who already knew how. Here is what their words mean in a kitchen today.

    Saleratus
    An early baking soda (bicarbonate of potash or soda). Use baking soda.
    Butter the size of an egg
    About 1/4 cup (4 tablespoons). “The size of a walnut” is about 2 tablespoons.
    A gill
    Half a cup (4 fluid ounces).
    A coffeecup, a teacup
    A coffeecup is about 1 cup; a teacup a little less, about 3/4 cup.
    A salt spoon
    About 1/4 teaspoon.
    Rich paste
    Pie pastry made with plenty of butter or lard.
    A soft ball
    Sugar syrup cooked until a drop forms a soft ball in cold water: 235–240°F on a candy thermometer.
    A quick, moderate or slow oven
    Before oven dials: quick or hot is about 400–450°F, moderate about 350–375°F, slow about 300–325°F.
    Cassia buds
    The dried flower buds of the cassia (Chinese cinnamon) tree. Whole cloves or a cinnamon stick will do.
    Paraffine paper
    Waxed paper.
    Dripping pan
    A shallow roasting or baking pan.
    Maître d’hôtel sauce
    Melted butter with chopped parsley, lemon juice, salt and pepper.
    Sweet milk, sour milk
    Sweet milk is fresh milk. Sour milk was soured naturally; buttermilk is the nearest today.
    A peck, a bushel
    A peck is about 8 quarts (around 12 lb of apples or peaches); a bushel is four pecks.

    The recipes are from cookbooks and newspapers published before 1929, now in the public domain, quoted as printed; the notes on making them today are ours. Photographs of the dishes are credited on each one.
