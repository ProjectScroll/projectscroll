    Discovery and research

    How Kalamazoo got its name

    Everyone in Kalamazoo knows two stories: a footrace against a boiling pot, and a founder run out of town over a stolen cherry tree. We went back to the record, from a Jesuit’s route notes of about 1760 to the act of 1836, to see where each story really starts, where the letter L came from, and what the Potawatomi names for the place and the river say today.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 28 min read

    Kalamazoo, Mich. Ask anyone in Kalamazoo why the city is called Kalamazoo and you will hear about a footrace: a young man running to the river and back before a pot of water boiled away. Ask why it is not still called Bronson and you will hear about a stolen cherry tree. You will find both on the city’s own website. Neither one, it turns out, rests where people think it does.

    We went back to the paper: a Jesuit’s route notes of about 1760, a French map of 1744, the treaty of 1821, an atlas map of 1822, the act of the legislature that changed the name in 1836, the county’s first histories, and the linguists who have argued over the word for 170 years. Then we set beside them the names that the Potawatomi people use for the place and the river today. The record does not hand over a single tidy answer. It does show which stories are old, which are new, where the letter L came from, and which explanation has the oldest written support.

    How to read this page

    Every statement below is marked Documented (a source says so, and you can read it), Reading (our interpretation of the sources) or Open (not yet known).     At a glance

    The place
    Kalamazoo, Michigan: city, township, county and the river they are named for
    First name
    The Village of Bronson, platted by Titus Bronson and Stephen Richardson, 12 March 1831
    Renamed
    By act of the Michigan Legislature, approved 2 March 1836, effective 31 March 1836
    Oldest spelling
    “kikanamaso,” a “little” and a “great” river of that name, in the route notes of Father Pierre Potier, dated by his editor to the autumn of 1760
    With an L
    “Kekalamazoo” and “Ke-kal-i-ma-zoo,” both in the Treaty of Chicago, 1821; “Kikalemazo R.” on the Carey & Lea map of Michigan Territory, 1822
    The meanings
    Boiling pot (in print by 1867); otters under the water (Schoolcraft, 1855); “it smokes” (Verwyst, 1892; Gerard, 1911); mirage or reflecting river (undated, rejected by scholars)
    The names today
    Gzigmezé, describing a boiling (a Potawatomi language specialist on WMUK, 2018). Gun Lake Tribe: Gzigmezék for the village and city, Giknamezo zibé for the river
    Why it matters
    The race story is old, but its hero, “Fleet Foot,” first appears in 1922. The cherry tree has no source we can find before modern times. And the river’s Potawatomi name today is close to the oldest spelling on paper

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • The name came from the river, and the river’s name is old. A form of it, “kikanamaso,” is in a Jesuit’s route notes dated to about 1760, nearly seventy years before the first settler’s cabin.
    • The L arrived late. The oldest spellings have an N; the L first appears in the treaty of 1821. The Potawatomi language has no L sound.
    • The race story was in print by 1867, told as an anonymous “Indian” runner and a pot boiling on the fire at Toland’s Prairie. The hero’s name, “Fleet Foot,” his bride and the year 1810 first appear in a newspaper of 1922.
    • The village became Kalamazoo by an act of the state legislature, approved 2 March 1836. The county’s 1880 history credits three men, and probably a fourth, with getting it done, and gives no reason beyond “some good reason, we presume.”
    • The Potawatomi names in use today are Gzigmezé or Gzigmezék for the place and Giknamezo zibé for the river.

    What it does not show, yet

    • Any record of the cherry tree: no court record, no newspaper, nothing in the first county histories.
    • What the word meant to the people who first said it. Every published meaning is someone’s explanation, not a speaker’s testimony on record.
    • Which river was the “little” kikanamaso in the 1760s.

    Story or record? Play along

    Kalamazoo tells itself some good stories. Here are eight things people say about the name. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “Kalamazoo was first called Bronson.”

    True

    Titus Bronson and Stephen Richardson recorded the plat of the Village of Bronson on 12 March 1831. It was the county seat under that name until 1836 (Exhibit E).

    “The state legislature changed the name.”

    True

    “An act to alter the names of the township of Arcadia and the village of Bronson” was approved on 2 March 1836. From 31 March both were to be “known and called Kalamazoo.”

    “Bronson lost his town over a stolen cherry tree.”

    No record yet

    Today’s histories say so, but we could not find the cherry tree anywhere in the nineteenth-century record: not in Durant (1880), not in the 1906 Compendium, not in the county’s 1877 report. Durant, who wrote at length about the renaming, gives no reason at all.

    “The footrace story was made up in the twentieth century.”

    No, it is older

    The race to the river and the boiling pot were in print by 1867, in George Torrey’s history of the village, quoting A. J. Sheldon. What the twentieth century added was the romance.

    “The runner was called Fleet Foot, and he ran for his bride.”

    Not before 1922

    In the 1867 telling the runner has no name and there is no bride. “Fleet Foot,” the bride and the year 1810 first appear in the Kalamazoo Gazette of 20 August 1922.

    “The oldest spelling of the name has an L in it.”

    No

    The oldest form we can find, “kikanamaso,” in Father Potier’s route notes of about 1760, has an N. The L first appears in the Treaty of Chicago of 1821.

    “A famous explorer said the name was about otters.”

    True

    Henry Rowe Schoolcraft, in 1855: stones in the riverbed that looked like an otter swimming beneath the surface. In 1911 William Gerard called his explanations “etymological absurdities.”

    “Kalamazoo means ‘reflecting river.’”

    No early source

    The “mirage” or “reflecting river” meaning has no early source that we can find, and Vogel’s Indian Names in Michigan rejects it.

    Who’s who

    Match-e-be-nash-she-wish Potawatomi leader · village at the head of the river, 1821
    The 1821 Treaty of Chicago reserved a tract “at the village of Match-e-be-nash-she-wish, at the head of the Kekalamazoo river,” on ground that is now the city. The reserve was ceded in 1827. The Gun Lake Tribe, the Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians, carries his name today.
    Father Pierre Potier 1708–1781 · Jesuit missionary at Detroit
    Missionary to the Wyandot at the Assumption mission across the river from Detroit. His papers include route notes for travellers; the one that names the “kikanamaso” is dated by his modern editor, Robert Toupin, to the autumn of 1760.
    Titus Bronson 1788–1853 · founder of the village
    Connecticut-born pioneer, known in his day as “Potato Bronson.” He settled on the site in 1829, platted the Village of Bronson in 1831, and left after the name was changed. He died at Middlebury, Connecticut.
    Henry Rowe Schoolcraft 1793–1864 · explorer and Indian agent
    Travelled through Michigan in 1820 and 1821. In 1855 he published the explanation that the name describes stones in the riverbed that look like swimming otters.
    A. J. Sheldon and George Torrey early Kalamazoo residents and writers
    Torrey’s history of the village, printed in the directory of 1867, quotes Sheldon’s version of the race to the river: the oldest printing of that story we have found.
    Samuel W. Durant compiler, History of Kalamazoo County (1880)
    Reprinted Torrey’s account and the act of 1836, and wrote that the name came from a Potawatomi word meaning “the boiling-pot.”
    Father Chrysostom Verwyst 1841–1925 · Franciscan missionary and Ojibwe scholar
    In 1892 explained Kalamazoo as a corruption of the Ojibwe kikanamoso, “it smokes, or he is troubled with smoke.”
    William R. Gerard 1841–1914 · student of Algonquian languages

    The river and its places

    Every place in this story lies along one river. Durant, in 1880, puts the race on “Galesburg, or Toland’s Prairie,” east of the town.

    Exhibit A: the oldest spelling, about 1760

    DocumentedThe oldest form of the name we can find on paper is in the writings of Father Pierre Potier, the Jesuit at Detroit. Among his papers are itineraries: lists, stage by stage, of the rivers and portages along routes through the Great Lakes country. In them the word appears as two rivers, a “little” one and a “great” one: le petit and le grand kikanamaso. His editor, Robert Toupin, prints them in Les Écrits de Pierre Potier (1996), pages 144 to 151, and dates the itinerary to the autumn of 1760.

    ReadingSpelled with an N, not an L. That one letter matters a great deal, as we will see. The date is the editor’s dating of the notes, not a date written on them, and route notes of this kind could be copied from other travellers; so “about 1760” is the safest way to put it. Either way, the river had this name well before any American surveyor saw it.

    OpenWhy autumn 1760? Toupin ties the date to a journey of Potier’s, on page 144; we have seen that page only through a search of the borrowed book, and have not yet read his reasoning in full.

    OpenWhich river was the “little” kikanamaso? Two streams of the same name suggest a main river and a branch, or two neighbouring rivers. We have not yet seen Toupin’s notes on the passage. The book is on loan through the Internet Archive and in university libraries.

    Exhibit B: the French maps

    DocumentedThe French named the river differently. Jacques-Nicolas Bellin’s Carte des Lacs du Canada of 1744 marks the rivers on Lake Michigan’s eastern shore. Going north from the St. Joseph, the map shows the “Riv. Noire,” then “R. Marameg,” then “R. a la Barbue” and the “R. au Raisin,” and then the Grand River.

    ReadingRead from the St. Joseph northward, the order matches the real coast: the Black River, then the Kalamazoo, then the Grand. That puts “Marameg” where the Kalamazoo should be. The Kalamazoo Public Library also reports the river called by forms of “Marame” on maps of the early 1800s, and the historian Larry Massie gives “Meramec” or “Merame” for the French name. Marameg was also the name of a band of the Miami people; Vogel says the river’s name in the French period was Meramec, which he translates “catfish.” And the next river north on Bellin’s map, “à la Barbue,” is French for “of the catfish.”

    DocumentedThe same Handbook entry, though, writes the river as “Maramec (Kalamazoo) r.,” citing a French document of 1695 that places the “Miamis of Maramek” on it. What it rejects is the idea that the northern Marameg people lived there, not that the river was once called Maramec.

    OpenWhether “Marameg” on the 1744 map really is the Kalamazoo is not settled. The Handbook of American Indians (1907) thought the Marameg people lived far to the north, near Sault Ste. Marie, and called the Kalamazoo identification of their name “certainly erroneous.” Only a full survey of the French maps and journals will decide it.

    Exhibit C: the treaty of 1821

    DocumentedThe earliest American document we have found that names the river is the Treaty of Chicago, signed on 29 August 1821 by Lewis Cass and Solomon Sibley for the United States and by the Odawa, Ojibwe and Potawatomi. Its second article reserved, among other tracts, “One tract at the village of Match-e-be-nash-she-wish, at the head of the Kekalamazoo river.” A note after the signatures, fixing the size of that tract at three miles square, spells the river a second way: “Ke-kal-i-ma-zoo.”

    ReadingTwo spellings in one document, both with an L, both with the extra syllable in front: “Ke-.” The village of Match-e-be-nash-she-wish stood on the ground of the present city. The 1827 Treaty of St. Joseph took the reserve back, and the first settlers arrived two years later.

    Exhibit D: the map of 1822

    DocumentedThe Kalamazoo Public Library and others report that the river is spelled “Kikalemazo” on an atlas of 1823 (“five years before any white settlement,” in the library’s words; Titus Bronson came in 1829), without saying which atlas. Henry Carey and Isaac Lea’s American Atlas, published at Philadelphia in 1822 and reissued in 1823, includes a Geographical, Statistical, and Historical Map of Michigan Territory. Its river, rising in the interior and running west to Lake Michigan between the Grand and the St. Joseph, is lettered “Kikalemazo R.”

    ReadingWe think this is the atlas the library means. The Carey & Lea map is dated 1822, the atlas was reissued in 1823, and its spelling matches the library’s exactly.

    ReadingSo by the early 1820s the name had two forms on paper: a government spelling with “Ke-” and an L, and a map spelling that still kept the old “Kik-” at the front, close to Potier’s “kikanamaso.”

    ReadingThe library calls that atlas the first appearance of a word like today’s spelling. The treaty of 1821, with its “Kekalamazoo,” is a year older than the map and two years older than the 1823 issue.

    Exhibit E: the county, the village and the act of 1836

    DocumentedKalamazoo County was laid out by a territorial act of 29 October 1829 and organized on 30 July 1830, when its northern half became the township of Arcadia. Titus Bronson and Stephen H. Richardson recorded the first plat of the village on 12 March 1831 (Durant, page 214), and named it Bronson. The county seat was Bronson. The Pioneer Society of Michigan’s county list of 1877 puts it in one line: “County-seat, Kalamazoo,—first Bronson, then changed to Kalamazoo.”

    DocumentedThe change itself was made by the first legislature of the new State of Michigan, in “An act to alter the names of the township of Arcadia and the village of Bronson.” From 31 March 1836 the township and the village were both to be “known and called Kalamazoo.” It was approved on 2 March 1836, nearly eleven months before Congress admitted Michigan to the Union on 26 January 1837.

    DocumentedDurant’s History of Kalamazoo County (1880) tells it on page 220, under the heading “Changing Names.” In the winter of 1835–36, he writes, a movement was set on foot “for some good reason, we presume,” and “the change was brought about” mainly through “Messrs. Burdick, Sheldon, and Lyon,” probably with Anthony Cooley, a proprietor of the village. Bronson “felt that he had been deeply wronged,” stayed only a short time, and later “is said to have died in straitened circumstances.” Durant adds a footnote: some had said the act was passed by the territorial legislature, but the session laws show otherwise.

    ReadingDurant gives only surnames. Lyon is almost certainly Lucius Lyon, the surveyor who became one of Michigan’s first two senators, and Burdick almost certainly one of the Burdick brothers; Cyren Burdick ran the Kalamazoo House, the village’s first hotel. The town had more than one Sheldon. Durant himself, writing forty-four years later, did not know why they wanted the change, or would not say. He thought the new name “more musical and pleasing than Bronson,” but admitted it was hard to deny that the change had done the founder an injustice.

    The cherry tree

    DocumentedToday’s histories say that Bronson was fined for stealing a cherry tree. The city’s own page adds that his rivals then had the town renamed; the Kalamazoo Public Library’s biography says the fine fed the discontent against him.

    DocumentedWe could not find the cherry tree anywhere in the nineteenth-century record. It is not in Durant’s History of Kalamazoo County (1880), whose full text we searched; it is not in Fisher and Little’s Compendium of History and Biography of Kalamazoo County (1906); and it is not in the county’s report to the Pioneer Society (1877). Durant, who writes at length about Bronson and the renaming, gives no reason for it at all.

    ReadingThat does not make the story false. Justice-of-the-peace dockets of the 1830s seldom reached print, and a neighbour’s complaint over a cherry tree is exactly the sort of thing a small town remembers and a county history leaves out. But as things stand, the most famous fact about the renaming is the least documented one.

    OpenWhere did the cherry tree story come from? The likeliest places are the township justice dockets, the Kalamazoo Public Library’s subject file on Bronson, and Nick Kekic’s history of early Kalamazoo, A Fine Place for a City (1984). If you know the original record, please write to us.

    Exhibit F: the race to the river

    DocumentedThe oldest printing of the race story we have found is in George Torrey’s history of the village, in the Kalamazoo directory for 1867, as reprinted by Durant. Torrey quotes a letter from A. J. Sheldon, who said he “took great pains among the Indians” to learn what the word meant. On Toland’s Prairie, east of the town, a wager was made that a runner could not reach a point on the river and come back before a little pot of water on the fire boiled away. The race was run, and the river was named “where the water boils in the pot.” The name, Sheldon added, at first belonged only to that stretch of the river.

    DocumentedDurant himself gives the meaning as “the boiling-pot, or where the water boils like a pot” (page 56), and so does the county report in the first volume of the Pioneer Collections of Michigan (1877): the river’s name was “Ke-Kenamazoo (the boiling pot).”

    DocumentedIn the 1867 telling the runner has no name, there is no bride and there is no date. The Kalamazoo Public Library’s files show the story growing. In a Kalamazoo Gazette article of 20 August 1922 the runner is “Fleet Foot,” he runs to win his bride, and the year is 1810. About 1951 the painter Zoltan Sepeshy put the race in a mural for the Fidelity Federal Savings and Loan on South Burdick Street.

    ReadingSo the boiling pot is genuinely old in local tradition: older than any surviving explanation except Schoolcraft’s. The romance came later. Sheldon also credited Schoolcraft with the “boiling pot” meaning, but Schoolcraft’s own published explanation, as the next exhibit shows, is about otters.

    OpenWhere did Torrey’s 1867 directory and the Gazette of 10 March 1901, “Origin of the name Kalamazoo,” get the story? Neither is online. The library’s subject file holds both.

    Exhibit G: the scholars

    DocumentedSchoolcraft, 1855: otters. In the Summary Narrative of his 1820 expedition, published thirty-five years later, Henry Schoolcraft explained in a footnote that “Kalamazoo” contracted a phrase describing “the stones seen through the water in its bed” which, through the moving current, looked like an otter swimming beneath the surface. His original word was “Negikanamazoo,” from negik, an otter. He added a note that the letter L came in as Indian words passed into French.

    DocumentedVerwyst, 1892: smoke. Father Chrysostom Verwyst, who spent his life among Ojibwe speakers, called the name a “corruption of kikanamoso (it smokes, or he is troubled with smoke—e.g., in his wigwam),” pronounced kee-kah-nah-mo-zo.

    DocumentedGannett, 1902 and 1905: all of the above. The federal Origin of Certain Place Names in the United States gave “otter tail” on “one authority,” and listed “beautiful water,” “boiling water” and “stones like otters” besides, without choosing.

    DocumentedGerard, 1911: not otters. William R. Gerard, writing in the American Anthropologist, called Schoolcraft’s explanations “etymological absurdities” and showed why the otter word could not be built that way. He argued that the name was an old Ojibwe word, kikalâmozo, “he is inconvenienced by smoke in his lodge,” and guessed, admitting it was only a guess, that it had once been a person’s nickname.

    DocumentedVogel, 1986: none proven. Virgil Vogel’s Indian Names in Michigan treats “the boiling pot” as an alleged meaning, rejects the otters and the “mirage or reflecting river,” and suggests that the smoke explanations deserve Gerard’s label too. He thought it more credible that the name meant something like “it runs fast,” or that it was a mangled form of another river’s name, and he noted that forms with an L may come from the Miami language.

    ReadingThe “mirage” or “reflecting river” meaning, still repeated on many websites, has no early source that we can find. The earliest we know of is a modern place-names dictionary, and Vogel rejected it.

    DocumentedThe Kalamazoo Public Library also records a forest-fire legend, in which the name is translated “smothered.” It sits close to the smoke words of Verwyst and Gerard, though no early source for it has turned up.

    Exhibit H: the missing L

    DocumentedPotawatomi has no L sound. A Potawatomi language specialist made the point on WMUK in 2018: if “Kalamazoo” comes from a Potawatomi word, it is not a close copy. The linguistic descriptions of the language agree. In Ojibwe, too, the old Algonquian sound that became L in some languages became N.

    ReadingNow set the spellings side by side, in the order they were written:

    • c. 1760 kikanamaso (Potier)
    • 1821 Kekalamazoo; Ke-kal-i-ma-zoo (Treaty of Chicago)
    • 1822 Kikalemazo (Carey & Lea)
    • 1829–30 Kalamazoo (the county)
    • 1855 Negikanamazoo (Schoolcraft, explaining)
    • 1867 Kih-Kalamazoo (Torrey and Sheldon)
    • 1877 Ke-Kenamazoo (Pioneer Society)
    • 1892 kikanamoso (Verwyst)
    • 2026 Giknamezo zibé, the river (Gun Lake Tribe)

    ReadingThe oldest form and the tribal form of today both have an N in the middle. The L belongs to the years when American officials and mapmakers wrote the word down, and it may come from interpreters who spoke a language that had one, as Vogel and Gerard both suggested in different ways. The “Ke-” or “Kik-” at the front, lost in the English name, is in the oldest and in the newest forms alike.

    Exhibit I: the names today

    DocumentedPotawatomi has its own names for the place and the river. On WMUK in 2018, a Potawatomi language specialist gave Gzigmezé as the Potawatomi name for Kalamazoo and said it describes a boiling, adding that he was not sure of the word’s origins.

    DocumentedIn July 2026 the Gun Lake Tribe (the Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians) presented a talk in Kalamazoo under the title “Gzigmezék mine Giknamezo Zibé.” The event notice glosses Gzigmezék as the past Potawatomi village and present-day Kalamazoo, and Giknamezo zibé as the Kalamazoo River.

    ReadingTwo names, then: one for the place, which describes a boiling, and one for the river, which is very close to the oldest spelling on paper. Giknamezo and Potier’s kikanamaso share their consonants in the same order. Verwyst’s kikanamoso of 1892, the “it smokes” word, is close too. If that is right, the old river name and the old village name may both lie behind the English “Kalamazoo,” and the town’s favourite story, the boiling pot, may preserve the place name while the spelling preserves the river’s.

    OpenWhat Giknamezo means, and how Gzigmezé and Giknamezo are related, are questions for the people whose language it is. We have not asked them to confirm anything on this page, and nothing here should be taken as their view.

    What became of Titus Bronson

    DocumentedBronson left Kalamazoo soon after the change. The library’s biography follows him to Davenport, Iowa, where he lost what he had in a land swindle in 1842, the year his wife Sally died, and then to Illinois. He went home to Connecticut and died at Middlebury in January 1853. His gravestone there calls him “A Western Pioneer, Returned to Sleep with his Fathers.”

    ReadingDurant thought Kalamazoo had forgotten him; in 1880 not even a street bore his name, only the park. Today a hospital, the park and a boulevard do.

    The other Kalamazoos

    DocumentedKalamazoo has been the name of other places too. Larry Massie told WMUK that two other communities once called themselves Kalamazoo: one in Sheridan Township near Albion, and the village at the river’s mouth that became Saugatuck. Allegan County’s 1907 history also records a paper town, Kalamazoo Harbor, laid out near the mouth of the river.

    DocumentedThe painting at the top of this page, William Greason’s Bayou on the Kalamazoo River, Douglas (1934), was made near that mouth, at Douglas, across the river from Saugatuck.

    OpenWe have not yet found the Sheridan Township Kalamazoo in any county history, or Saugatuck’s early name in Allegan County’s.

    DocumentedThe name itself became famous for sounding like nowhere in particular: “from Timbuktu to Kalamazoo.” Carl Sandburg put it in a poem title, “The Sins of Kalamazoo” (1920), and Glenn Miller’s orchestra took “(I’ve Got a Gal in) Kalamazoo” to the top of the charts in 1942.

    A glossary

    Plat
    A surveyed map of a new village, dividing it into streets and lots, recorded with the county.
    Reserve
    Land held back from a treaty cession for a band or a person. The reserve at Match-e-be-nash-she-wish’s village lasted from 1821 to 1827.
    Zibé
    Potawatomi for “river,” as in Giknamezo zibé, the Kalamazoo River.
    Folk etymology
    An explanation of a word made up afterwards to fit how it sounds, rather than traced from what it once meant.
    Itinerary
    In Potier’s papers, a list of the stages of a journey, river by river and portage by portage.

    The poster

    The whole story on one page, for a classroom, a library wall or a refrigerator door: “How Kalamazoo Got Its Name,” from about 1760 to today, every date from the record, with the spellings in order along the foot. Letter size, landscape; it prints well larger.

    What we are not saying

    • Not that we know what Kalamazoo means. Nobody has a speaker’s explanation on record from the time the name was first written down. Every meaning on this page is somebody’s explanation.
    • Not that the race never happened, or that the cherry tree is false. We say only where each story first appears in print, so far as we can find.
    • Not that “Marameg” on Bellin’s map is certainly the Kalamazoo. The position fits; the identification is disputed.
    • Not that Potier walked the river in 1760. The date is his editor’s, and the notes may have been compiled from other travellers.
    • Not that we speak for any Potawatomi nation. We report the names as they have been published, and nothing more.

    If anyone can show the cherry tree in a record of the 1830s, or the race story in print before 1867, we will say so on this page, with thanks.

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. Each one says where the answer is most likely to be. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Where is the cherry tree? A fine for stealing a tree would be in a justice of the peace’s docket for Kalamazoo or Arcadia township, about 1832–36. The Kalamazoo Public Library’s subject files and the Western Michigan University archives are the places to ask.
    2. What else did Torrey’s 1867 directory say, beyond the passage Durant reprinted, and what did the Gazette print in 1901? Both are in the library’s file on the name.
    3. Which river was the little kikanamaso? Toupin’s edition of Potier, pages 144 to 151, and his notes.
    4. Was “Marameg” the Kalamazoo? The French maps after Bellin, and the journals of travellers on the lake shore, would settle it.
    5. What does Giknamezo mean? A question for the Potawatomi language programs, and theirs to answer.
    6. Who were “Burdick, Sheldon, and Lyon,” exactly, and why did they want the change? The legislative journals for February and March 1836 may show who brought the bill.
