    The Underground Railroad

    The Kentucky Raid of 1847

    Myth testedSeries · 2 of 2 ›Go see it19 min read

    In the summer of 1847 a party of Kentuckians came to the Quaker farms of Cass County to carry nine people back into slavery. Their neighbors stopped them at O’Dell’s mill, a hearing at Cassopolis set the nine free, and the lawsuits ran on for five years. We went back to the papers of 1847, the court files and the county histories.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 19 min read

    The short version

    In August 1847, a dozen or more men from northern Kentucky came to the Quaker farms of Cass County and seized nine people who had escaped from slavery. Several hundred neighbors stopped them near O’Dell’s mill, where Vandalia is now, and took everyone to Cassopolis. There a commissioner set the nine free, because no certified copy of Kentucky’s slave laws was offered in evidence.

    Fifty-two freedom seekers then left for Canada. The slaveholders sued the Quakers in federal court at Detroit; the jury disagreed in 1851, two men settled, and the last suits were dismissed in 1852. We went back to the papers of 1847 and the court files, and found details the later histories missed, including what the raiders’ own home paper said about their bail.

    • The raiders’ own home paper, the Covington Licking Valley Register of 17 September 1847, put their bail at “something over four thousand dollars.” Every later account we have found gives $2,600, the 1882 county history’s figure.

    Cassopolis, Mich. In the summer of 1847 a party of Kentuckians rode into Cass County to take back people who had escaped from slavery and were living and working on the Quaker farms around Young’s Prairie. In one night they seized nine. By the next morning the neighbors had caught up with them near O’Dell’s mill, at what is now Vandalia, and a crowd of several hundred walked raiders and captives together to Cassopolis. There a county commissioner heard the case and set the nine free, and that same day a party of fifty-two started north for Canada.

    The story is told every year in Cass County, and some of what is told has grown in the telling. We went back to what was written at the time: the Covington, Kentucky, newspaper that spoke for the raiders, the federal court papers, a freedom seeker’s own account, and the county histories of 1875, 1882 and 1906.

    At a glance

    When
    Most likely Friday, 13 August 1847, for the raid and the stand near O’Dell’s mill, and Monday, 16 August, for the hearing at Cassopolis (our reading of a letter written at Niles on 21 August)
    Where
    The Quaker farms of Penn and Calvin townships, Cass County, Michigan; O’Dell’s mill, now Vandalia; the courthouse town of Cassopolis
    Who came
    A dozen or more men from Kenton and Boone counties, Kentucky: “some 12 or 15,” said their home paper; “12 or 13,” said a Centreville paper; “sixteen,” said a Niles eyewitness
    Who was taken
    Nine people, seized at the farms of Josiah Osborn, Stephen Bogue, Zachariah Shugart and others
    What happened
    A commissioner, Ebenezer McIlvain, freed all nine: the Kentuckians had no certified copy of the laws that made them slaves
    Afterward
    Fifty-two freedom seekers left for Canada from Ishmael Lee’s farm; the slaveholders sued the Quakers in federal court at Detroit; the main suits ended in 1851–52
    Why it matters
    One of the grievances of the slave states in the years before the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • The raiders came from northern Kentucky. Their own paper, the Covington Licking Valley Register, called the runaways people “from this county” (Kenton), and others came from Boone County.
    • Nine people were seized, and several hundred neighbors stopped the raiders near O’Dell’s mill and took everyone to Cassopolis.
    • A commissioner freed the nine because no certified copy of Kentucky’s slave laws was offered in evidence. A Niles man who was there wrote that the commissioner also held that only a United States officer could certify ownership.
    • It was over within days. A letter written at Niles on Saturday, 21 August 1847, already tells of the seizure “on Friday morning last” and of the nine set free.
    • Fifty-two freedom seekers left for Canada from Ishmael Lee’s farm, in the charge of Zachariah Shugart.
    • The slaveholders sued in federal court. The jury disagreed in January 1851; two defendants settled, and the suits against Josiah Osborn and Stephen Bogue were dismissed in 1852.

    What it does not show, yet

    • The exact day, in so many words. No paper of 1847 that we have found prints it; the Niles letter of 21 August points to Friday, 13 August.
    • What the Niles Republican and the Detroit papers said. They survive on microfilm and in subscription archives, and we have not yet read them.
    • The sum Nicholson and Lee paid. The settlement paper names none.
    • Any record that Henry Clay or anyone in Congress spoke of the Cass County raid in 1850.

    Story or record? Play along

    Here are eight things people say about the raid. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “The raiders came from Bourbon County, Kentucky.”

    Not as the record shows

    The county histories say Bourbon, but the raiders’ own Covington paper wrote of runaways “from this county,” Kenton, and the freedom seekers’ accounts name Boone County.

    “A commissioner freed the nine because there was no copy of Kentucky’s slave laws.”

    True

    Rogers (1875, p. 141): McIlvain decided against the Kentuckians “on the ground that there was no certified copy of the statutes of Kentucky offered in evidence.” Their own paper complained of “a demand … to prove that slavery was tolerated by the Constitution of Kentucky!”

    “Hundreds of local people turned out to stop the raiders.”

    True

    The Kentucky paper itself said “a furious mob of several hundred.” The 1882 history says “at least three hundred.”

    “Henry Clay cited the Cass County raid in the Senate.”

    No record

    We have found no record of it. The Senate report behind the 1850 law speaks of the Marshall case, not Cass County.

    “The raiders’ wagon was rolled into Birch Lake.”

    No record before the 1930s

    The Kentucky paper of 17 September 1847 says the wagon’s wheels were taken, but that it “was then delivered up.” The earliest telling of the lake story we know of is in newspapers of 1936–37.

    “Fifty-two freedom seekers left for Canada together.”

    True, as printed

    Rogers (1875, p. 141): at Ishmael Lee’s farm “a party of fifty-two runaway slaves was made up, put in charge of Zachariah Shugert and started toward Canada.”

    “The Quakers won the federal case outright.”

    No

    The jury disagreed in January 1851. Two defendants then settled with the slaveholders’ lawyer, and the suits against Josiah Osborn and Stephen Bogue were dismissed in 1852.

    “A thousand freedom seekers passed through Cass County.”

    No count

    The “thousand” was Dr. Nathan Thomas’s estimate for his own station at Schoolcraft. The 1882 history guessed “probably not less than fifteen hundred” through Cass County, which is a guess, not a count.

    The Quaker line

    DocumentedCass County lay on what the county history of 1875 called the “Quaker line,” a chain of Quaker settlements reaching up from the Ohio River through Indiana, at which freedom seekers “were harbored, fed and directed on their way.” Rogers names two stations in the county, “kept by Stephen Bogue and Zachariah Shugert.”

    DocumentedIn the spring of 1847 two groups escaped from northern Kentucky. A Cincinnati paper of 28 April reported a “Grand Stampede” of twenty to twenty-five people from Kenton County, and a second party came from Boone County. One of them, Perry Sanford, told a Battle Creek paper in 1884: “Into that boat eleven of us crowded.” Many of them stopped on the Quaker farms of Young’s Prairie and stayed to work. Zachariah Shugart’s own account book lists arrivals that May.

    ReadingThey had reason to think they were far enough away. They were not. The slaveholders of Kenton and Boone counties found out where they were.

    The raid

    DocumentedAbout the first of August, Rogers says, “a party of thirteen men” arrived at Battle Creek, some of them posing as salesmen, and were turned away by Erastus Hussey and the townspeople there. In Cincinnati, the Quaker Levi Coffin got warning of the plan and sent a messenger to Young’s Prairie, but, he wrote, the raiders had “started several days earlier.”

    DocumentedThey came in the night, working in separate parties, to the farms of Josiah Osborn, Stephen Bogue, Zachariah Shugart and the East settlement. Perry Sanford remembered it: “About 4 o’clock, there came a knock at the door.” He fought and got away through a cornfield. Nine people were taken.

    DocumentedA man who saw the end of it wrote from Niles on Saturday, 21 August: “On Friday morning last, a gang of Kentuckians, to the number of sixteen, … commenced their diabolical work just before the break of day, after having divided themselves into companies of three and four each; being armed with carbines, pistols, dirks, &c.” His letter, signed J. I. Alexander, was printed in Ann Arbor’s antislavery paper, the Signal of Liberty, on 28 August, and was copied as far as New York.

    DocumentedBy morning the alarm had spread. “In the neighborhood of O’Dell’s mill,” Rogers wrote, the raiders “were speedily surrounded by an excited mob of citizens armed with rifles, shot-guns, straw cutters, axes, clubs and whatever other weapons chance threw in their way, who resolutely opposed their southward progress.”

    Documented“A parley was had,” Rogers wrote, “high words and threats exchanged, weapons drawn, and a bloody riot seemed imminent; but, fortunately, more moderate counsels prevailed.” The two sides agreed to go to Cassopolis and put the case to the law. The 1882 history describes the procession: “thirteen Kentuckians, their nine shackled captives and a crowd of at least three hundred citizens.”

    The hearing at Cassopolis

    DocumentedAt Cassopolis the Kentuckians were arrested on complaints of kidnapping and trespass, and bailed. Their lawyers sought to hold the nine as fugitives; the freedom seekers’ lawyers, Ezekiel S. Smith and James Sullivan, with James Brown of Niles, asked for a writ of habeas corpus. The county’s own commissioner was away, and the case was heard, “on the third day after the arrests,” by Ebenezer McIlvain, circuit court commissioner of Berrien County.

    DocumentedThe Niles letter tells the same days from the inside. The Kentuckians were charged with assault and battery and held to bail “in the sum of three thousand dollars, which they obtained by depositing $900 and their horses in the hands of some of the citizens.” Their trial was “deferred four days to enable them to send to Kalamazoo for counsel.” Meanwhile “two of the friends in Cassopolis came to Niles to obtain a writ of habeas corpus from E. McIlvaine Esq.,” and the writer rode back with them. The Kentuckians asked for time “to send to Indiana for witnesses,” and were refused.

    DocumentedMcIlvain decided “adversely to the Kentuckians, on the ground that there was no certified copy of the statutes of Kentucky offered in evidence showing the legal existence of slavery in that State.” The nine were free. The Niles letter gives a second ground: that McIlvain, “being a State officer, had no jurisdiction in the case—that no one but a United States officer could give a certificate of ownership.” The two accounts do not contradict each other.

    Documented“Immediately upon the decision,” Rogers goes on, the freed people “were taken in charge by their friends and hurried to the farm of Ishmael Lee, where a party of fifty-two runaway slaves was made up, put in charge of Zachariah Shugert and started toward Canada.” The 1882 history says that Dr. Nathan Thomas and his wife fed the party at Schoolcraft on the way north.

    What Kentucky read

    DocumentedThe Covington Licking Valley Register told its readers about it on 3 September 1847, under the heading “INFAMOUS!” A party “of some 12 or 15 gentlemen,” it said, had gone to reclaim “some 20 or 30 Slaves, from this county,” and been met by “a furious mob of several hundred abolitionists.”

    DocumentedTwo weeks later the paper printed a “fuller account.” It called McIlvain an “unprincipled scoundrel” who had “no jurisdiction of the case, he being only a county officer, and resided in another county,” and it raged at “a demand of our citizens to prove that slavery was tolerated by the Constitution of Kentucky!”

    New findingThe same account gives two details the later histories do not. It says the raiders’ bail came to “something over four thousand dollars,” where the 1882 history, and every account since that we have found, says $2,600; the Niles letter, written within days, says three thousand. And it says the raiders’ baggage wagon, seized by “the demons in the neighborhood” and its wheels taken, “was then delivered up.” The earliest telling we know of the story that the wagon was rolled into Birch Lake, cited by the House Divided project, is in newspapers of 1936–37.

    Five years in court

    DocumentedThe slaveholders could not take the nine back, so they went after the people who had helped them. Under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 they sued for the value of the freedom seekers in the federal court at Detroit: Thornton Timberlake against Josiah Osborn and others, and companion suits against Stephen Bogue, Zachariah Shugart, David T. Nicholson, Ishmael Lee, Ebenezer McIlvain and their neighbors. Abner Pratt of Marshall acted for the Kentuckians; Jacob M. Howard of Detroit defended the Quakers.

    DocumentedThe county histories date the filing to February 1848; the court dockets, as historians have read them, to January 1849. A Cassopolis doctor who had watched the Kentuckians in the village gave a deposition in April 1850: “I don’t know that I saw any of them use violence at all.” The case was tried before Judge Ross Wilkins over the winter of 1850–51, and in January 1851, says the 1882 history, “it was concluded, the jury disagreeing.”

    DocumentedOn 3 December 1851, at Cassopolis, David T. Nicholson and Ishmael Lee signed a paper with the slaveholders’ lawyers: the suit had been “this day settled,” it would be discontinued and “not again to be instituted” against them, and “no judgement fo[r] costs” would be entered for either side. The paper names no sum. The suits against Josiah Osborn and Stephen Bogue were dismissed in February 1852.

    ReadingThe amount is told several ways, and three different things have run together. Rogers (1875) says Nicholson paid “$2,755.” The 1882 history says he paid “$1,000 to clear himself and Ishmael Lee,” and that “the total costs of the case, which amounted to about $3,000, were borne by the several defendants”: that $3,000 is the whole defense’s costs, not the settlement. The legal historian D. G. Chardavoyne (2004) gives $1,000 plus costs “estimated at $300 to $500,” and says his figure comes from a letter Nicholson wrote to the Covington Journal in February 1852; that is where the “about $1,300” of later accounts comes from. We have not yet read Nicholson’s letter.

    DocumentedThe court files themselves, six suits from Graves, Timberlake and Scott, are digitized and free to read in the National Archives catalog: Timberlake v. Osborn alone runs to 67 pages. Among them is a case no history we know of mentions: United States v. Jonathan Crews, for perjury, against a witness in the Timberlake trial.

    Where the nine went

    DocumentedThe suits name the people the Kentuckians claimed. Thornton Timberlake claimed Jonathan, Nancy, Mary, Robert and Gabriel; John L. Graves claimed Joseph, Cena and Benjamin; Charles Scott claimed Lewis. Perry Sanford, telling the story in 1884, named among those taken Joe Sandford, “his wife and daughter,” and the daughter of the man later known as William Casey.

    DocumentedOne of them told his own story. In Canada West in the 1850s, Joseph Sanford gave his narrative to the Boston writer Benjamin Drew: “There were thirteen of us started away in company.” It breaks off in Cincinnati, and the next narrative in the book, by John Hatfield, who hid Sanford and his wife there, finishes it: “they were afterwards all captured in Michigan: but they got off before a judge, and were then sent over the line into Canada.”

    ReadingLibrary and family research carries several of them further, and we have not yet checked it against the census ourselves. Gabriel took the name James Johnson in Canada and farmed at Colchester, Ontario, where he died in 1915; the Amherstburg Freedom Museum tells his family’s story. The Ughbanks family is traced to Colchester as well, and Joseph and Cena Sanford to Anderdon, near Amherstburg. The family of Lewis Gardner, who became William Casey, is placed by one source in Battle Creek and by another in Canada. Perry Sanford, who got away through the cornfield, lived on in Battle Creek and died there in 1905.

    OpenThe old histories say nine were taken, but their own farm-by-farm counts, and the names in the suits, come to ten. Which nine stood before McIlvain is not yet settled.

    Did it change the law?

    DocumentedIn March 1847, before the Cass County raid, Kentucky’s legislature had already asked Congress for stronger laws to reclaim fugitives, after a rescue at Marshall, Michigan. The Senate committee’s report of May 1848 rests on the Marshall case and does not mention Cass County.

    ReadingThe 1882 county history is the first we have found to tie the Cass raid to the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, and even it is careful: the raid, “with the Van Zant case in Ohio, it had a strong bearing upon the passage of the fugitive slave law of 1850 …” It is fair to call the raid one of the grievances behind the 1850 law. The record we have read does not show that it caused it.

    OpenWe have found no record that Henry Clay, or anyone in Congress, named the Cass County raid in the debates of 1850. If you know of one, please tell us.

    How the story is told today

    DocumentedThe fullest modern retelling is Cindy Yawkey’s The Kentucky Slave Raid of 1847, Cass County Michigan, written and illustrated by the head docent of the Bonine House and published by the Underground Railroad Society of Cass County about 2019. It goes farm by farm, with her own drawings and a hand-drawn map of the prairie, and it is the version most visitors to Vandalia will meet. The society’s narrated presentation Out of Bondage: The Perry Sanford Story takes its title from Sanford’s own 1884 interview.

    DocumentedMuch of it comes straight from the county histories, and checks out against them: the spy who called himself Carpenter and posed as an abolitionist from Worcester County; the Rev. Mr. Stevens taking a baby from a cabin and making it cry until its mother came out of hiding; Perry Sanford’s knock at the door at four in the morning; the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 as the law the Kentuckians stood on.

    ReadingHer book dates the raid to “Tuesday, August 17, 1847.” The arithmetic of the old histories points elsewhere. Rogers puts the hearing “on the third day after the arrests,” and the 1882 history says the commissioner “heard the case on Monday.” And a letter dated at Niles on Saturday, 21 August 1847, already reports the nine set free, so the Monday hearing can only have been 16 August, and the raid, three days earlier, Friday the 13th: neither the Tuesday of her book nor the Friday the 20th of most accounts online.

    OpenA few details we have not yet found in any record: that twelve-year-old Angeline Osborn hid a mother and daughter in her father’s house, and was later made to testify against him; that forty freedom seekers, not Rogers’s fifty-two, went north from Ishmael Lee’s farm; and that the slaveholders’ suit was first brought in 1850, where the histories and the dockets give 1848 or 1849. Her sources may hold them, and we would like to see them.

    DocumentedHer picture book They Have My Shoes, I Have My Freedom, which won a Michigan State History Award in 2016, carries a fictional freedom seeker named Lucinda from Georgia to the Bonine carriage house. We write about it in our story The Bonine House at Vandalia.

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. What day was the raid? Modern accounts give 16, 17 or 20 August 1847. J. I. Alexander’s letter, dated Niles, 21 August 1847, and printed in the Signal of Liberty of 28 August, says the Kentuckians struck “on Friday morning last” and that McIlvain had already freed the nine. With Rogers’s “third day after the arrests” and the 1882 history’s “Monday,” that points to Friday, 13 August for the raid and Monday, 16 August for the hearing. The Cassopolis Advocate’s own account, reprinted in the Covington Licking Valley Register of 15 October 1847, may settle it; we have not yet seen it.
    2. What did the Niles and Detroit papers say? The Niles Republican of 21 and 28 August 1847 survives on microfilm at the Cass District Library’s Local History Branch in Cassopolis and at the Library of Michigan; the Detroit Free Press of 1847 is in subscription archives. Both should be read page by page.
    3. How much did Nicholson and Lee pay? The settlement paper of 3 December 1851 names no sum. The figure most often given, $1,000 plus $300 to $500 in costs, rests on Nicholson’s own letter to the Covington Journal of February 1852, which we have not yet read.
    4. Which nine, and where did they go? The suits name ten people claimed; the histories say nine were taken. Joseph Sanford’s own account and the narrative beside it in Drew’s The Refugee (1856) put him and his wife in Canada after the hearing. The later lives traced by library and family research are still to be checked in the census.
