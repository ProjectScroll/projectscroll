    The lakes

    Stone Lake, the lake that keeps secrets

    Go see it9 min read

    No stream runs into Stone Lake, and none runs out. The village of Cassopolis was laid out on its shore in 1831, the county history of 1882 called it the most peculiar lake in the county, and people once believed it rose and fell with Lake Superior. Here is what the record says, and the seven mysteries it still keeps.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 9 min read

    Cassopolis, Mich. Stone Lake lies at the foot of Broadway in Cassopolis, close enough to the courthouse that the village was laid out around its shore. It is not large, and it is not famous. But it has puzzled people for a long time. No stream runs into it, and none runs out. Its water was soft when its neighbor’s was hard. And in the 1880s, by the county history’s account, many people in Cass County believed it was fed by an underground river from Lake Superior.

    We went back to the county histories of 1882 and 1906 and the county atlas of 1872 to see what the record says about the lake, and what it still keeps to itself.

    At a glance

    The place
    Stone Lake, on the south side of the village of Cassopolis, the seat of Cass County, Michigan
    The name
    From the boulders: its shores “were originally very thickly strewn with stone, in the form of bowlders” (1882)
    Size
    188 acres, in the county history’s table of 1882
    The water
    “No visible inlet or outlet; its water is very fine and very soft,” while Diamond Lake’s, half a mile away, “is hard” (1882)
    The village
    Platted in 1831 beside the lake; the plat was “broken on the southwest corner by the lake” (1906)
    Drinking water
    From 1891 the village water works pumped from “the depths of Stone lake” (1906)
    Why it matters
    The lake shaped where the village grew, and it still keeps seven open questions

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • The lake is named for its stones: boulders the glaciers left on its shores.
    • It has no visible inlet or outlet, and its water was “very fine and very soft” in 1882, when Diamond Lake’s was hard.
    • People believed in a river from Lake Superior. The 1882 history reports the belief that the lake was fed by “the great subterranean stream” from Superior, and rose and fell with it.
    • The village was laid out on its shore, and for years drew its drinking water from it.

    What it does not show, yet

    • Whether the lake still rises and falls when its neighbors don’t. Only people on the shore can tell us.
    • Whether its water is still soft. A few dollars of test strips would answer it.
    • Where the icehouse stood that, around 1899, supplied ice to the Diamond Lake cottages.

    Story or record? Play along

    Here are eight things people say about Stone Lake. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “The 1882 county history called Stone Lake the county’s most peculiar lake.”

    True

    “The largest is Diamond Lake … and the most peculiar is Stone Lake.” History of Cass County (1882), p. 11.

    “In 1882 Stone Lake’s water was hard, like Diamond Lake’s.”

    No

    Its water was “very fine and very soft”; Diamond Lake’s, “only half a mile distant, is hard.” 1882, p. 11.

    “Stone Lake is named for the boulders on its shores.”

    True

    Its shores “were originally very thickly strewn with stone, in the form of bowlders.” 1882, p. 11.

    “An underground river from Lake Superior feeds Stone Lake.”

    No record

    The 1882 history says only that it was “supposed by many people.” No study has ever shown it.

    “Cassopolis once drank Stone Lake water.”

    True

    From 1891 the water works pumped “from the depths of Stone lake, where the water is crystal pure and ice cold, and free from lime, or ‘soft.’” Glover (1906), p. 152.

    “The lake took a corner out of the village plat.”

    True

    The 1831 plat was a rectangle “broken on the southwest corner by the lake.” Glover (1906), p. 149.

    “The first manufacturing business in Cassopolis stood on Stone Lake.”

    True, as printed

    In 1833, Glover says, the Silvers brothers “put up a distillery on the banks of Stone lake, the first manufacturing institution of Cassopolis.”

    “Stone Lake is the largest lake in Cass County.”

    No

    In the 1882 table it is 188 acres; Diamond Lake, half a mile away, is 1,083.

    A shore of stones

    DocumentedThe 1882 county history explains the name plainly. Of all the county’s lakes, “the most peculiar is Stone Lake — so named from the fact that its shores were originally very thickly strewn with stone, in the form of bowlders.”

    ReadingThe boulders came with the glaciers, dragged down from the north and dropped when the ice melted, as they were all across this county. Most of them are gone from the shore today.

    OpenWhere did they go? If you know of a building in Cassopolis made of Stone Lake stone, we would like to hear about it.

    No stream in, no stream out

    Documented“This lake has no visible inlet or outlet; its water is very fine and very soft. That of Diamond Lake, only half a mile distant, is hard.” The same history adds: “There is known to be a chain of soft-water lakes extending for a considerable distance across the country from north to south.”

    DocumentedThe village put that soft water to use. In 1891 Cassopolis built water works, and, Glover wrote in 1906, “the water is pumped into the mains from the depths of Stone lake, where the water is crystal pure and ice cold, and free from lime, or ‘soft.’”

    OpenIs it still soft? Water-hardness test strips cost a few dollars at a hardware or pool store. Dip one at a public spot on Stone Lake and one on Diamond Lake, and send us the numbers. You could settle a question that goes back to 1882.

    The river from Lake Superior

    Documented“It is supposed by many people,” the 1882 history says, “that Stone Lake is one of the surface spots of the great subterranean stream by which Lake Superior is believed to discharge its waters into the Gulf of Mexico; and, it is averred in support of this theory, that the rise and fall in Stone Lake corresponds closely with that of the ‘shining’ big sea water.”

    ReadingThe “shining big sea water” is Longfellow’s, from The Song of Hiawatha (1855): “By the shining Big-Sea-Water.” The history does not name him, but its readers would have known the line.

    OpenNeighbors on the shore may be the only people who can answer this one: does Stone Lake rise and fall when the other lakes around it don’t?

    The village on the shore

    DocumentedWhen the county seat was fixed at Cassopolis in 1831, the proprietors laid out their village beside the lake. Glover, in 1906, describes the original plat: a rectangle of 119½ by 191 rods, “broken on the southwest corner by the lake.” “The lake,” he adds, “occupied the principal natural position in influencing the location of residence and business enterprises at the early period.”

    DocumentedThe first manufacturing in the village was on its banks: in 1833, Glover says, Jacob, Abiel and Benjamin F. Silvers “put up a distillery on the banks of Stone lake.” By 1882 there was a bowl factory on the shore, and the road to Niles in early days, the 1882 history says, “led through the woods on the high ground west of Stone Lake.”

    DocumentedThe county atlas of 1872, whose plan of Cassopolis heads this page, shows the lake at the foot of the village, with Broadway, Disbrow and the public square laid out beside it.

    Ice in winter

    DocumentedBefore refrigerators, the lake had a winter crop. The Diamond Lake Association’s history, drawn from Lois Webster Welch’s A Diamond Sparkles (1970), records under 1899: “Ice deliveries were made from the Stone Lake Icehouse.” We have not yet found the newspaper of the time.

    ReadingSo for a while the iceboxes at the Diamond Lake cottages most likely ran on Stone Lake water, and the two lakes that never shared their water did, a block at a time.

    OpenWhere did the icehouse stand, and who ran it? Do you have a photograph of an ice harvest, or of a summer day on Stone Lake? Tell us, and tell us where it came from.

    Seven secrets still kept

    Stone Lake has its own treasure hunt, on Facebook, with seven mysteries open. Answers count when they come with the page they came from: a book, a map, a deed, a newspaper. We will not give the answers here, but here are the questions.

    1. Who was the settler who gave away forty acres of lakefront for the village?
    2. Where is the 1831 parchment deed today?
    3. Where does Stone Lake’s water really come from?
    4. Where did the boulders on the shore go?
    5. What powered the flour mill on a lake with no stream, and where did it stand?
    6. Who was the doctor who dug into a mound in this county in 1878? (Not on this lake.)
    7. What became of the thigh bone, and of everything else from that grave?

    Cracked so far: none. When someone solves one, we will name them here.

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Does the lake still rise and fall on its own? Readings from the shore over a year would show it.
    2. Is the water still soft? Test strips, or the village’s own water reports, would tell.
    3. Where did the icehouse stand? A plat book, a tax roll or a photograph might show it.
    4. Where is the 1831 plat, the one Glover said had copies “still extant”?
