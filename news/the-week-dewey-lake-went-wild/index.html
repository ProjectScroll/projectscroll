    Creature scare, 1964

    Monster Town, U.S.A.: The Week Dewey Lake Went Wild

    7 min read

    In June 1964, berry pickers near Dewey Lake said they saw something huge and hairy in the dark. Within days the back roads jammed, armed hunters walked the fields, and Chicago papers called Sister Lakes “Monster Town, U.S.A.” The scare was real. The monster never turned up. And the road to Dewey Lake had a warning on it long before.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 23 October 2026 · 7 min read

    Sister Lakes, Mich. In June 1964, in the middle of strawberry season, workers in the berry fields near Dewey Lake said they had seen something big and hairy at the edge of the dark. The sheriff’s office came out. It found nothing.

    Within two days that nothing was on the front page of The Detroit News. Cars jammed the back roads, men walked the fields at night with guns and flashlights, pickers stayed home, and Chicago papers called Sister Lakes “Monster Town, U.S.A.” Within a week it was over. We went back to what the papers printed, and to an older warning on the same road, written in red chalk on a tree.

    How to read this page

    Every statement below is marked Documented (a source says so, and you can read it), Reading (our interpretation of the sources) or Open (not yet known); a New finding is something we found in the record that we could not find in any earlier telling. Every source is listed at the foot of the page.

    At a glance

    What
    A reported hairy, man-like “monster,” said to stand far taller than a man
    Where
    The farms and fields near Dewey Lake, in the Sister Lakes country west of Dowagiac, Silver Creek Township, Cass County, Michigan
    When
    First reported on 9 June 1964; the scare had faded within about a week
    Coverage
    The front page of The Detroit News on 11 and 12 June 1964; the South Bend Tribune; the Chicago papers
    Result
    Searches found no creature
    Our verdict
    The scare was real and drew national notice. There is no evidence of any creature.

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • It was front-page news in Detroit. The Detroit News of 11 June 1964 ran “Frankenstein Snarl of Traffic Loosed by ‘Monster’ Hunt” on page one.
    • The berry pickers stayed out of the fields. The next day’s front page: “‘Monster’ Scare Idles Pickers.”
    • Searches found nothing. By 12 June the South Bend Tribune called the monster “nonexistent.”
    • The road to Dewey Lake had a warning on it long before. The county history of 1882 tells of a tree by the road, marked in red chalk: “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.”

    What it does not show, yet

    • What, if anything, people actually saw.
    • How tall the first witnesses said it was. Modern tellings give nine feet, or ten feet and 500 pounds; we have not found those figures in the 1964 papers.
    • Who led the search. Later accounts name two different sheriffs, and we have not yet seen a 1964 page that settles it.

    Story or record? Play along

    Here are five things people say about the Dewey Lake Monster. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way.

    “The monster scare was front-page news in Detroit.”

    True

    The Detroit News put it on page one on 11 and 12 June 1964.

    “Police caught, or photographed, the creature.”

    False

    The searches found nothing, and by 12 June the South Bend Tribune called the monster “nonexistent.”

    “Berry pickers stayed out of the fields.”

    True

    “‘Monster’ Scare Idles Pickers,” said The Detroit News of 12 June 1964. It was strawberry season.

    “It stood ten feet tall and weighed 500 pounds.”

    No record

    Those figures come from later tellings. We have not found them in the 1964 papers.

    “Dewey Lake is named for a pioneer.”

    True

    Henry Dewey, who moved to Silver Creek for the land near “the lake which bears his name,” as the county history of 1882 puts it (p. 356).

    A thing by the fields

    DocumentedAccording to Robert Atkinson’s survey of the 1964 papers, deputies were called to a farm near Dewey Lake on 9 June after seasonal workers reported a large creature. Over the next three days more people said they had seen it, including a daytime report on 11 June, and footprints were reported on 12 June.

    DocumentedThe headline in The Detroit News of 12 June explains why the farms cared: “‘Monster’ Scare Idles Pickers,” with “Workers Afraid To Go Into Fields.” It was strawberry season.

    ReadingThat detail matters. A monster that keeps the pickers home is not just a ghost story; it costs a farmer his crop. The Chicago Tribune of 13 June put it plainly, as Atkinson lists it: “Monster Jabs Berry Farms in Pocketbook.”

    The hunt

    DocumentedThe Detroit News of 11 June ran the story across the top of page one: “Frankenstein Snarl of Traffic Loosed by ‘Monster’ Hunt,” with a map inside.

    DocumentedPolice searched and found nothing, while sightseers and hunters poured in. Local businesses sold “monster burgers,” “monster getaway gas” and monster hunting kits.

    DocumentedAtkinson lists a Chicago’s American story of 15 June: “Itchy Gun Fingers Blast Away at Pony.”

    ReadingThe headline speaks for itself. That week, the real danger in the fields after dark was armed amateurs.

    What the experts said

    DocumentedTwo University of Michigan zoologists were asked what it could be. As Atkinson quotes the 1964 press, Dr. William H. Burt thought of a bear: “When they get up on their hind legs … they might be five or six feet.” Professor Frank Eggelton doubted it, “Bears don’t stand on their hind feet too often,” and floated an escaped gorilla.

    DocumentedBy 12 June the South Bend Tribune’s headline read “Dewey Lake ‘Monster Is Nonexistent.’” By 14 June it was “‘Hairy Monster’ Scare Lessens”; nobody, the paper said, “seems frightened anymore.” On 18 June the Dowagiac Daily News ran “Policeman Says ‘No Monster’” (as Atkinson lists it).

    DocumentedIn 1983 the Tribune’s “Action Line” column looked back and suggested the monster had been dreamed up by strawberry pickers who had been drinking.

    The road with a warning on it

    DocumentedDewey Lake takes its name from a pioneer. The county history of 1882 says Henry Dewey moved to Silver Creek because “the land in the vicinity of the lake which bears his name possessed many attractions, among others its proximity to the lake, which at the time was alive with fish.”

    DocumentedOn the same page is an older fright. Timothy Treat brought his family from Aurora, New York, in the autumn of 1837 and lived two years at Ontwa before moving to Silver Creek. Coming in from Indian Lake, they found that “at the southeast corner of Section 16 the road forked, one branch leading to Dewey’s Lake, the other to their future home.” Someone had gone ahead of them, “evidently … not favorably impressed with the country,” and had peeled the bark from a tree and written on it in red chalk:

    “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.”

    ReadingThe book does not say which branch was the left one. Read from the page, the Treats would have reached the fork about 1839. Either way, the road to Dewey Lake had a frightening reputation more than a century before the monster.

    New findingWe have found no modern telling of the Dewey Lake Monster that mentions the red-chalk sign on the road to “Dewey’s Lake,” or that names the lake’s pioneer from the 1882 history.

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Who led the search? Later accounts name Sheriff Robert Dool; a dealer’s note names Paul Parrish. The Dowagiac Daily News of 11 June 1964, “Daily News Goes On ‘Monster’ Hunt With Cass County Sheriff’s Men,” should settle it.
    2. How tall did the first witnesses say it was? The papers of 10 to 12 June should give the first descriptions, before the story grew.
    3. Was there a reward? Later tellings speak of $1,000 for its capture. Who offered it, if anyone?
    4. Which branch was the left? Where the old track forked at Section 16, and which way led to Dewey’s Lake, can perhaps be read from the early plat maps of Silver Creek Township.
