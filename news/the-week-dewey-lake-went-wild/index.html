    The True Story of the Dewey Lake Monster: The Week the Valley Went Wild

    Discovery · 5 new findings ›57 min read

    Everyone around Sister Lakes knows the story of the 1964 monster. Most of what they know is wrong. It did not begin with berry pickers, and it did not begin in 1964: more than a hundred and twenty-five years earlier, someone peeled the bark from a tree at a fork in the road to Dewey’s Lake and wrote a warning on it in red chalk. This is the real story, and it is stranger than the legend.

    The short version

    The legend says some berry pickers saw a ten-foot monster in 1964 and the sheriff found nothing. The record says more. People near Dewey Lake, west of Dowagiac, had been seeing a large, hairy thing on two legs since 1962. On 9 June 1964 it was seen between a barn and a shed on a farm south of the lake: about nine feet, five hundred pounds, red eyes, a cry like a baby. Two days later three girls saw it cross a road in broad daylight, and one fainted. For a week the Sister Lakes country went wild: armed hunters from three states, a $1,000 reward, “Monster Burgers,” and migrant families sleeping locked in their cars.

    The papers called it nonexistent. But the story is far older than 1964. The county’s oldest books hold older frights: a blood-drinking “monster” cat, a serpent in neighboring Indian Lake, and a warning chalked in red on a tree at a fork in the road. Read against the old township map, that warning, “Turn to the left and go to the Devil,” pointed down the road to Dewey’s Lake. And the reports from this valley have never quite stopped.

    Sister Lakes, Mich. You have probably heard the story. In 1964 some berry pickers near Dowagiac saw a hairy monster, ten feet tall. The papers had a laugh, the sheriff found nothing, and that was the end of it.

    Almost none of that is true.

    It was not one sighting. People had been seeing the thing, and keeping quiet about it, for two years before anyone called the police. It was not ten feet tall, not at first: the first accounts on the wire said nine, and a girl who saw it in broad daylight said seven, and it grew with every newspaper that printed it. It did not begin with pickers in a strawberry field. It began after dark at a farmhouse south of Dewey Lake, between a barn and a shed, where a woman said the thing had been coming around for summers, and had once chased her into her own house. And nobody in 1964 called it Bigfoot. That word was only six years old, coined by a California newspaper in 1958. In Sister Lakes they called it the monster, or the creature, or the thing.

    The papers decided within a week that there was nothing there. But the story did not begin in 1964, and the strangest parts of it were never in the papers. They are in the county’s own oldest books. More than a hundred and twenty-five years before the berry pickers, someone peeled the bark from a tree on the road to “Dewey’s Lake” and wrote a warning on it in red chalk. An 1882 history called a thing in these woods “the monster” and said it drank a colt’s blood. In 1898 the lake next door had a serpent of its own. And the reports kept coming, from the swamps and creeks and woods of this valley, long after everyone agreed there was nothing out there.

    This is the real story, as the record tells it. Every statement on this page says where it comes from. Some of it is stranger than the legend.

    Our verdictThe scare was real, and it drew national notice. No one has ever produced evidence of the creature. The record is older, and stranger, than the legend.

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • It was front-page news in Detroit. The Detroit News of 11 June 1964 ran “Frankenstein Snarl of Traffic Loosed by ‘Monster’ Hunt” on page one.
    • The berry pickers stayed out of the fields. The next day’s front page: “‘Monster’ Scare Idles Pickers.”
    • The sheriff’s men led the hunt. Sheriff Robert Dool sent his undersheriff, Ernest Kraus, and deputies with deer rifles into the lakes country (United Press, 11 June 1964).
    • Searches found nothing. By 12 June the South Bend Tribune called the monster “nonexistent.”
    • The road had a warning on it long before. The county history of 1882 tells of a tree by the road to Dewey’s Lake, marked in red chalk: “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.”
    • It was not the first thing in the dark. The same old books tell of a blood-drinking “monster” cat in Volinia Township, and papers of 1898 told of a serpent in Indian Lake, next door to Dewey.

    What it has never shown

    • A body, a bone, a clear photograph, or a cast that held up. The one plaster cast of the first night came out of the sand soft and uncertain.
    • The Browns’ own account as first printed. The wire’s nine feet and 500 pounds are Mrs. Utrup’s; the Browns’ words survive only as Robert Atkinson gathered them from the 1964 papers.
    • Any agreement on who stood with Gordon Brown that first night.

    Prologue: a warning on the road, the 1830s

    DocumentedPicture the country before the farms. Oak and hickory to the horizon, tamarack swamps in the low ground, and one narrow track winding north-east from Indian Lake “around fallen trees and swamps,” as the county history of 1882 describes it. At the corner of Section 16 the track divided. One branch led to what would become the Treat family’s farm. The other ran toward a small lake that a pioneer named Dewey would give his name to.

    DocumentedSomeone had gone that way before the settlers. At the fork he stopped, stripped the bark from a tree, and on the bare wood he wrote in red chalk: “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.” The 1882 book says only that he was a “disconsolate emigrant,” and that he was gone by the time the Treat family came through, about 1839.

    DocumentedThose were the same years a hairy wild thing was reported in the papers on the other side of the lake country. Late in 1839 a story from Michigan City, some forty-five miles to the south-west on Lake Michigan, told of a creature about four feet tall, covered in light brown hair, a very fast runner and a strong swimmer, and fond of the water. It went into the papers as far away as Gettysburg, where the writer thought it would be “an act of Humanity” for the young men to turn out and capture it.

    ReadingNobody wrote down which road the warning meant, or why. But read the old township map and the left-hand road went to Dewey’s Lake. A hundred and twenty-five years later, at the end of that road, something stood between a barn and a shed and let two brothers with a flashlight walk right past it. This is how it happened, and how far back it goes.

    Before the headlines

    DocumentedThe thing at Dewey Lake did not arrive in June 1964. According to Robert Atkinson’s survey of the 1964 papers, people had been reporting a large, hairy creature on two legs since 1962, among the cherry orchards, the berry patches and the swampland south of the lake. Most of them kept quiet. In June the sheriff’s deputies told the press the sightings had come on and off for nearly two years.

    DocumentedErnest Kraus, the undersheriff, explained the silence to the wire on 12 June: “People have been reluctant to call because it’s a fantastic story and they’re afraid of ridicule.”

    “People have been reluctant to call because it’s a fantastic story and they’re afraid of ridicule.”

    Undersheriff Ernest Kraus, to the United Press, 12 June 1964

    Tuesday night, 9 June

    Tuesday9 June 1964No moon. New moon tomorrow.After dark · the Utrup farm, south of Dewey Lake

    DocumentedThe call came from the farm of Mrs. Evelyn Utrup, south of Dewey Lake in Silver Creek Township. Seasonal workers had driven onto the farm after dark: Gordon Brown, who was twenty-five, his wife Mary, who was seventeen, his brother Randall, and a girl from a neighboring place. Gordon Brown worked on the farm next door, which belonged to Mrs. Utrup’s brother-in-law, Harold Utrup, as the National Observer told it that month. Between the barn and a shed, they said, they saw something large. Not every account agrees on who stood beside Gordon Brown in the dark: the National Observer said only “two companions,” and a 2022 book names a fellow farmhand, Joseph Smith. The papers could count the monster’s pounds more easily than the people who saw it.

    DocumentedThe Brown brothers went after it with a flashlight, about three hundred yards, as Robert Atkinson reconstructs it from the 1964 papers. Then they understood that the tree they had just passed was not a tree. It was the thing, standing still, letting them go by. They ran.

    DocumentedWhat they told the deputies, as Atkinson gathers it, matched the description the whole country would repeat: about nine feet tall, some five hundred pounds, a black, leathery face, and eyes that glowed red. It made a sound, they said, like a baby crying, or a goose honking.

    DocumentedMrs. Utrup said she had never actually seen it. But she had heard it, often, outside, in the summers. Once, she said, it had chased her into the house, and its stamping had shaken the ground and the house with it. To the sheriff, as the National Observer told it, she spoke of “great thumping feet” rushing at her when she went into the back yard to see what a noise was; she ran back in without seeing what made them. (The wire called her Mrs. John Utrup and the Observer Mrs. Evelyn Utrup; in the custom of the day, that was almost certainly one woman, Evelyn, the wife of John.)

    ReadingRead that line again: often, in the summers. Whatever was out there, by her account, had been coming around for a long time before anyone picked up a telephone.

    DocumentedIn the earliest wire story we have read on the page, the United Press report printed in Mount Pleasant’s Isabella County Daily Times-News on 11 June, the nine feet and five hundred pounds are hers. “Mrs. John Utrup of nearby Dewey Lakes, Mich., told sheriff’s deputies the beast chased her into her home Tuesday night. She said she was saved when her dogs barked and drove the monster away. It was nine feet tall, she said, and weighed 500 pounds.” The next day’s wire added the face: it was “covered with hair except for a black leathery human-like face.”

    ReadingThe two accounts do not quite agree. The wire has the chase on Tuesday night and gives her a height and a weight; the Observer has her never seeing it at all, only hearing it. Both cannot be her exact words. What both agree on is the sound: something big, outside, in the dark, close enough to drive her indoors.

    ReadingSo the woman in the St. Joseph Herald-Press headline of 10 June, “Monster Chases Woman!,” was almost certainly Mrs. Utrup. And the description the country repeated seems to have reached the wire through her, not only through the Browns.

    DocumentedShe telephoned the sheriff, Robert Dool. He sent his undersheriff, Ernest “Ernie” Kraus, with a posse of deputies into the lakes country that night. They carried deer rifles, the United Press wire reported, and they meant to stake out the fields.

    DocumentedThe deputies found a track: three and three-quarter inches across the heel, six inches wide across the middle. They poured plaster into it. The soil was sand, and the cast came out soft and uncertain.

    What the witnesses heard

    • A baby crying (the wire, 11 June)
    • Noises “like a tamed goose” (the wire, 11 June)
    • A goose honking (the first witnesses, as Atkinson gathers them)
    • “Great thumping feet,” rushing at a woman in her own back yard (National Observer)
    • Stamping that shook the ground, and the house with it (Mrs. Utrup)
    • The dogs, barking, until it went away (the wire, 11 June)

    “Descriptions varied according to the teller,” the wire said on 15 June. “Some said it cried like a baby, others that it quacked like a duck. But all agreed it was big.”

    Wednesday, 10 June

    Wednesday10 June 1964New moon. A black night.A sign goes up · the back roads fill with guns

    DocumentedBy morning someone had put up a sign, MONSTER, with an arrow pointing the curious toward the Utrup farm. The deputies took it down. It did not matter. A radio newscaster in Benton Harbor told his listeners, wrongly, that the Cass County authorities wanted help hunting the creature, and the St. Joseph Herald-Press ran the story under “Monster Chases Woman!”

    DocumentedThat night they came. Men drove the back roads with shotguns, rifles, tire irons and knives, with license plates from Indiana, Illinois and as far away as Ohio. The deputies spent the night directing traffic and sending would-be posses home. Officers came over from Van Buren County to help. At one stop they took a loaded magnum revolver from three boys from the Detroit area; at another, baseball bats from seven more; at another, tire irons and clubs from a caravan of eight carloads of teenagers. “I’d feel safer if it was just me and the monster,” one deputy, Andy Chavous, said. The crowd did not thin until about three in the morning. (A United Press story printed on Saturday the 13th tells of the same eight carloads, and the same line.)

    “I’d feel safer if it was just me and the monster.”

    Deputy Andy Chavous, on the night the hunters came

    Thursday morning, 11 June

    Thursday11 June 1964A sliver of moon, down soon after the sun.9:15 a.m., broad daylight · Swisher Street and Town Hall Road

    DocumentedAt a quarter past nine in broad daylight, three girls were walking near Swisher Street and Town Hall Road: Joyce Smith, thirteen, and two sisters, Gail Clayton, thirteen, and Patsy Clayton, twelve. Something crossed the road in front of them. It walked on two legs. The next day’s wire put it more starkly: the “Monster” “suddenly reared up alongside the road.”

    DocumentedJoyce Smith fainted. The Clayton sisters brought her round, and the three of them ran to the nearest house to call the police. Dowagiac’s police chief, Richard Wild, passed the call to the sheriff. Two sheriff’s cars came, and a conservation officer, and a State Police plane was called for. They found nothing.

    DocumentedPatsy Clayton had the best look. It was seven feet tall, she said, with a black face, and it was “definitely not human.” Joyce Smith said it looked like a bear, but it walked on its hind legs. Patsy, the wire said, “got a good look at the creature before it ducked into the woods.”

    “Definitely not human.”

    Patsy Clayton, twelve, who saw it cross the road at 9:15 in the morning

    DocumentedAnd they were not the only ones that Thursday. “Three more persons Thursday reported seeing the creature,” the wire said the next morning. It did not give their names.

    DocumentedKraus, the undersheriff, told the wire that day that a gorilla had not been ruled out: “We have had no reports of any circus gorillas.” By the next day the deputies thought it most likely a bear that had wandered in.

    DocumentedThat day’s Detroit News put it across the top of page one: “Frankenstein Snarl of Traffic Loosed by ‘Monster’ Hunt.”

    DocumentedBy Sunday the 14th, a wire story printed as far away as Muncie, Indiana, had the creature at about ten feet and more than five hundred pounds (the Muncie Star Press of that day, as the Notebook of Ghosts gives it).

    ReadingNine feet on the wire, seven from the girl who saw it best, then ten. The monster grew in print, a long way from the farm.

    ReadingThe road names are older than the monster. On the Silver Creek Township plat of about 1928, “S. Swisher” holds 160 acres in Section 8, the section that runs down from the south shore of Dewey Lake. Swisher Street very likely carries that family’s name.

    Friday, 12 June

    Friday12 June 1964A thin crescent, setting early.Tracks in the woods · a drawing on a front page · pickers locked in their cars

    DocumentedRobert Walker of Dowagiac came forward with what he called “big ape tracks” in the woods near Dewey Lake. He said he had seen the creature himself the summer before, and had not wanted to say so. He knew its nightly rounds, he said, and the commotion had driven it off: “It hasn’t showed up for two nights: it shows he is scared.” The deputies who looked at his tracks thought they looked more like a dog’s.

    DocumentedIn Chicago, Chicago’s American put the monster on its front page. Its reporter, Michael McGovern, had asked Mary Brown to draw what she saw, in his notebook, balanced on an oilcan. The paper ran her drawing under the words “Have YOU Seen THIS Monster?” and quoted her husband: “It was an extreee giant looking thing, a whale of a joker.”

    DocumentedThe Detroit News had a different worry: “‘Monster’ Scare Idles Pickers,” with “Workers Afraid To Go Into Fields.” Among the migrant families who had come north for the berry harvest, a rumor had spread that the monster had eaten four people. Some families packed up and left. Some of those who stayed, the wire reported on 12 June, “slept overnight in their cars with the windows up and the doors locked.”

    “It may be a joke to some people, but it’s no joke to us.”

    A fruit farmer, to the United Press, 12 June 1964

    ReadingIt is easy to laugh at the monster. It is harder to laugh at a family sleeping in a car with the doors locked, a long way from home, in a country where something was said to walk the fields at night.

    Monster Town, U.S.A.

    DocumentedSister Lakes did what a resort town does with a crowd. The taverns poured “Monster Brew.” Reid’s Drive-In put a stuffed monster out front to sell its “Monster Burger.” Harvey’s East variety store sold a “Monster Hunting Kit” for $7.95: a baseball bat, a wooden mallet, an arrow, a net and a flashlight (other papers add a squirt gun and a stake). Gas stations sold “getaway gas.” The radio station at St. Joseph, WSJM, played monster songs between its live reports, and a Dowagiac theater booked The Horror of Party Beach and The Curse of the Living Corpse. A group calling itself the Monster Boosters promoted the whole thing; the Sister Lakes Chamber of Commerce said it had nothing to do with it.

    DocumentedThen came the money. Jack Hadley, a beer distributor, offered $1,000 for the monster taken alive by five o’clock on 22 June. (The United Press wire, as printed in Mount Pleasant and in Traverse City on 13 June, spells him Hadley both times and calls him “a local beer distributor”; one later account has Handly.) The sheriff, Robert Dool, was already swamped with calls from around the world, and he had seen the crowds. “It’s getting mighty dangerous,” he said, “with 3,000 strangers prowling about at night with guns.” He persuaded Hadley to withdraw the offer, and by Monday the 15th it was gone.

    DocumentedThere was gunfire only once. A hunter saw a black shape in the dark, fired twice and missed. It was a Shetland pony. Chicago’s American had the headline: “Itchy Gun Fingers Blast Away at Pony.”

    DocumentedNot everyone was amused. “They call us ‘Beast Town’ now in Detroit,” a Dowagiac Daily News columnist wrote. “Gives us a rather uncouth image doesn’t it.” The Chicago papers, by then, were calling the place “Monster Town, U.S.A.”

    DocumentedThe name stuck where it was given. On Saturday, 13 June, the Detroit Free Press ran it across a banner: “‘Beast Town’ Booms: Get Your Monster Kit (7.95).”

    DocumentedOn Saturday and Sunday, 13 and 14 June, there were no more sightings. The hunters went back to work. The summer people went back to the water.

    DocumentedOther towns noticed what a monster could do for business. Two months later the Saline Reporter, a weekly south of Ann Arbor, confessed to envy: Dowagiac, it wrote on 19 August, had “capitalized on a monster before we could get around to installing a Loch Ness … type in the mill pond, thus drawing scads of tourists.”

    What the experts said

    DocumentedTwo University of Michigan zoologists were asked what it could be. As Atkinson quotes the 1964 press, Dr. William H. Burt thought of a bear: “When they get up on their hind legs … they might be five or six feet.” Professor Frank E. Eggleton doubted it, “Bears don’t stand on their hind feet too often,” and suspected an escaped circus gorilla. No zoo or circus reported an animal missing.

    DocumentedEggleton had his own doubt. If it was true the thing had been roaming the area for two years, he told the wire, it could not be a gorilla, since “they couldn’t survive that long in this climate out-of-doors.”

    ReadingSo the experts had a bear that would not stand up and a gorilla that could not have lived through two Michigan winters. Neither answer fit.

    DocumentedThe county officials liked the bear. Dick and Dorothy Grabemeyer, who farmed by the swampland near Dewey Lake, said they had seen bears there before. But the Brown brothers were hunters, and they insisted it was no bear; and the witnesses who saw it said it walked on two legs.

    DocumentedThere was a catch in the bear. Michigan’s black bears live mostly in the north. The Department of Natural Resources says their range in the Lower Peninsula has only spread south toward Grand Rapids since about 2000, and when a black bear wandered into rural Cass County in May 2022, it made the news.

    ReadingSo a bear in the Sister Lakes country in 1964 was not impossible. But it would have been almost as unusual as the monster, and a bear that walked upright across a road in front of three girls, in broad daylight, more unusual still.

    DocumentedThe Grabemeyers’ name is on the old map too. The township plat of about 1928 shows “F. W. Grabemeyer” on 119 acres in Section 6, in the north-west corner of the township, a mile or so west of Dewey Lake.

    ReadingAnd the red eyes? Many animals have a mirror at the back of the eye that throws a flashlight beam straight back: deer, raccoons, dogs, bears. Human eyes do not. The first night’s witnesses spoke of red eyes. Whatever was out there, it was looking back at them.

    DocumentedBy 12 June the South Bend Tribune’s headline read “Dewey Lake ‘Monster Is Nonexistent.’” By 14 June it was “‘Hairy Monster’ Scare Lessens”; nobody, the paper said, “seems frightened anymore.” On 18 June the Dowagiac Daily News ran “Policeman Says ‘No Monster.’”

    DocumentedIn 1983 the Tribune’s “Action Line” column looked back and suggested the monster had been dreamed up by strawberry pickers who had been drinking.

    ReadingNonexistent, said the paper. Nobody frightened anymore. It is the kind of thing people say in daylight.

    Why people see what they see

    The skeptic’s case deserves a fair hearing. Every one of these is real, and every one was at work in June 1964.

    • Eyes that glow. Deer, raccoons, dogs and bears have a mirror at the back of the eye that throws a flashlight straight back. Human eyes do not. Red eyes in the dark mean an animal is looking at you, not what kind.
    • The tree that moves. At dusk the brain fills in shapes it half sees. A stump, a fence post, a man in a coat becomes a figure, and a figure becomes a face.
    • Sounds that carry. A red fox’s scream sounds like a woman or a child. Geese honk at night. Owls shriek. Over still water, all of it travels.
    • A story that grows. Seven feet from the girl who saw it best, in daylight; nine on the wire; ten in Muncie; ten again on I-96. The farther the story travelled, the bigger the monster got.
    • Expectation. Once the radio said there was a monster, every shape at the edge of every field was the monster. That is how a hunter in the dark ends up firing twice at a Shetland pony.
    • Pranks. A youth in a fur coat at the Saranac overpass. Eight boys in ponchos on each other’s shoulders. The hoaxes were real, too.

    And yet. None of that explains two summers of silence before anyone said a word, or three girls in broad daylight, or a sheriff’s plaster cast poured into the sand. The skeptic’s case explains a great deal. It does not quite explain everything.

    The copycats

    DocumentedWithin a week the monster had imitators. Early on Wednesday, 17 June, some eighty-five miles north-east, near Ionia, two brothers, Wayne Ritter, nineteen, and Lloyd Ritter, seventeen, of Clare, driving from Lansing to Grand Haven at four in the morning, reported a ten-foot creature “with long brown hair” crossing Interstate 96. If it was the same monster, the wire noted, it had “grown a foot” since Thursday.

    DocumentedThey had stopped and called the Kent County sheriff’s department; Sergeant Sid Frazer took the call, and the State Police post at Ionia took the case. Two troopers, Charles Bush and Bob Tubbs, drove the brothers back to the spot. They found no tracks. “It was foggy,” Bush said, “and could have been an impression enhanced by an illuminated road sign. It could have been a deer.”

    DocumentedThe next night, something came out onto the same road. Corporal Richard Abbott of the State Police was driving home to East Lansing with his tracking dog, Jocko, when a figure, “apparently a youth clad in” fur, jumped onto I-96 near the Saranac overpass, in front of the car ahead of him. Abbott slowed, thinking it was a deer; then he jumped out and grabbed for it, and missed. He and Jocko went after it until a downpour washed out the tracks. Nobody ever learned who was inside the fur. It left behind a piece of fur, “apparently from a coat or wrap,” and a rubber glove. “This kid could have gotten run over,” a trooper at the Ionia post said. Before dawn the same post arrested eight would-be monsters on M-21, two miles west of town: boys under big ponchos, one standing on another’s shoulders. They were brought in, “sternly lectured,” and handed over to their parents.

    DocumentedTheir home paper gave them the front page. Under “Did They See The Monster?” the Clare Sentinel of 18 June described the thing as the Ritters told it: “about ten feet tall and covered with long brown hair, ‘like a gorilla,’” upright, running on muscular hind legs. By daylight there was nothing. “The outer limits of the twilight zone,” the paper wrote, “had again become a modern, paved expressway and not a foot track or sign of the Monster could be found.”

    DocumentedWhen the Sentinel looked back over 1964 on the last day of the year, the Ritters made the list, between a forest fire and the Jaycees’ Young Man of the Year: “June came and with it a little bit of madness.”

    DocumentedBy August the scare had found a new animal. “Now Sheridan Has Its Own ‘Monster,’” the United Press reported on 10 August, from Montcalm County: a lion, “like the type you see in a circus.” Mr. and Mrs. Lyle North, south of Sheridan on M-66, said they saw it standing on a limb in a tree; their dogs chased it, and it roared and fled into the swamps. A ten-year-old boy in Bushnell Township was in trouble for telling his family he had seen a lion, until it walked into the yard and his mother saw it too. Conservation officers said lions were not native to Montcalm County. The sheriff was not excited. No paper we have found ever reported the lion caught.

    ReadingAnd then one line in that Sheridan story that could have come straight from Dewey Lake two months before: other rural residents said they had seen the animal too, “but don’t talk about it for fear of ridicule.” It was Ernie Kraus’s explanation, almost word for word, from the other end of the state. Whatever people see in the dark, the fear of being laughed at keeps more of it quiet than the dark does.

    DocumentedThe next summer the Benton Harbor News-Palladium reported, on 17 August 1965, that the monster which “caused a flurry in this area last summer” had “apparently packed his bags” for Monroe, on Lake Erie. There, on the night of Friday the 13th, Mrs. George Owens, thirty-eight, and her daughter Christine Van Acker, seventeen, said that a thing seven feet tall and some four hundred pounds, with hair “like quills,” came at their car, reached in at the open window and struck the girl’s head against the door. She was left with a black eye, and the Detroit Free Press put her picture on its front page under “‘The Thing’ Haunts Monroe: Monster Days Are Here Again.” Sixteen more sightings followed that summer, and search parties went out. The two women passed a lie-detector test given for a radio show and failed one given by the police, who called it a hoax. They kept to their story.

    ReadingEvery hoax made the next report easier to dismiss. That is the trouble with a hoax: it does not only fool people. It teaches them to stop listening.

    Back to the fork in the road

    DocumentedDewey Lake takes its name from a pioneer. The county history of 1882 says Henry Dewey moved to Silver Creek because “the land in the vicinity of the lake which bears his name possessed many attractions, among others its proximity to the lake, which at the time was alive with fish.”

    DocumentedOn the same page is an older fright. Timothy Treat brought his family from Aurora, New York, in the autumn of 1837, lived two years at Ontwa, and then moved on to Silver Creek. They came in from the south. At Indian Lake they found a track running north-east, “winding around fallen trees and swamps.” And “at the southeast corner of Section 16 the road forked, one branch leading to Dewey’s Lake, the other to their future home.”

    DocumentedSomeone had been there before them. “Some disconsolate emigrant had preceded them,” the book says, “and evidently was not favorably impressed with the country to which they were going.” He had stripped the bark from a tree, and on the bare wood he had written in red chalk:

    “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.”

    ReadingThe book never says which branch was the left one. But the map can. Dewey Lake lies across Sections 4, 5, 8 and 9 of Silver Creek Township, north-west of Section 16. Indian Lake lies in Sections 30 and 31, in the south-west corner. A traveler coming up the track from Indian Lake toward the north-east, and reaching the south-east corner of Section 16, would have to bear left, to the north-west, to reach Dewey’s Lake.

    New findingRead that way, the warning was not a joke about the whole country. It pointed one way. Turn to the left, it said, and go to the Devil. The left-hand road went to Dewey’s Lake.

    ReadingThe Treats came to the fork about 1839, so the warning was already there, and its writer gone. The family took the other branch, and the book moves on.

    DocumentedSo where did the other branch go? The family’s own genealogy answers it. The Treat Family (1893) says Timothy Treat, born in Vermont in 1786, a veteran of the War of 1812, left Aurora, New York, for Michigan, and “in the fall of 1837” settled in Silver Creek township, Cass County, where he lived until 1863. His eldest son was Sullivan Treat, born in Aurora in 1811, who “settled in Silver Creek, Cass Co., Mich., on a heavily timbered farm, which he cleared, enduring all the privations incident to pioneer life.” Father and son both ended their days at Elk Grove, California; Sullivan went west across the Plains in 1859.

    ReadingSullivan Treat’s land patent of May 1839 is for the south-east quarter of the north-east quarter of Section 12, on the township’s east side. From the fork at the corner of Section 16, Section 12 lies to the north-east, and Dewey’s Lake to the north-west. The Treats took the right-hand road, and the warning pointed the other way.

    New findingWe have found no modern telling of the Dewey Lake Monster that mentions the red-chalk sign on the road to “Dewey’s Lake,” or that names the lake’s pioneer from the 1882 history.

    Older things in the dark

    The monster of 1964 was not the first thing the people of this country saw at the edge of the trees. The old county books keep a few others.

    DocumentedThe cat on the leaning tree. In Volinia Township, north-east of Dowagiac, in the early days, families who let their fire go out sent a child to a neighbor’s to borrow coals. The 1882 history tells what one boy saw: “Reuben F. Gard was sent on such an errand one frosty morning to the house of George Newton, and when there remarked that he saw an immense cat, crouched on a tree that leaned over the path he passed along.”

    DocumentedThe book goes on: “A panther at once suggested itself, and a search showed where the monster had killed a colt belonging to D. C. Squires, and sucked its blood. It was a narrow escape.” (History of Cass County, 1882, p. 285.)

    DocumentedThe cries on the road home. In Porter Township, the same book says, Mr. and Mrs. Rinehart, coming home from church in Newberg, “were followed for several miles by a panther, who encircled them while emitting his blood-curdling cries,” until it left them near Birch Lake (p. 303).

    DocumentedNext door, the panthers were public business. When Van Buren County’s supervisors met for their first annual meeting, in the schoolhouse at Paw Paw on 13 October 1837, the only business besides the bills and the taxes was a bounty: five dollars a head “on each wolf and each panther killed in the county.” The meeting’s minutes were reprinted in the Paw Paw Courier-Northerner ninety years later.

    ReadingThose were real animals: the cougar was native to Michigan until it was wiped out in the early 1900s. But notice the word the old historian reached for. In 1882, in plain print, it was already “the monster.”

    DocumentedThe serpent next door. In October 1898 the state-news columns carried a short item: “Residents of the vicinity of Indian lake, near Dowagiac, have been terrorized of late by a huge sea serpent in the lake” (Isabella County Enterprise, 14 October). Another paper added that “parties are out gunning for the monster.” Two weeks later it “proved to be an otter and a muskalonge” (Evart Review, 28 October). In July 1899, under the heading “Cass County’s Sea Serpent,” it was back.

    DocumentedWhich Indian Lake? Michigan has more than one. But every one of the papers says “near Dowagiac,” and that is Cass County’s Indian Lake, in Silver Creek Township: the lake where the Treats picked up the track toward the fork, a few miles from Dewey.     Something that learned to keep its distance

    Drive the back roads of this valley at dusk and you will see how much of it is still dark. Between the towns lie river bottoms and tamarack swamps, wet woods along the creeks, woodlots no one has cut in a lifetime, and lakes whose far shores show no lights at all. A few miles from Dewey Lake, the Dowagiac Woods alone keeps 384 acres of forest, the Michigan Nature Association’s largest sanctuary in the Lower Peninsula.

    ReadingCountry like that keeps its secrets. A deer can live a whole life a hundred yards from a road and never be seen. So can a fox, a bobcat, a mink. And the stories people tell about this country all share a shape: something seen at the edge of the light, at the edge of the trees, watching. Something that does not come closer. Something that, if it is there at all, has learned to stay away.

    DocumentedPeople have another name for the Dewey Lake Monster now. The Wikipedia article on it calls it the “Sister Lakes Sasquatch.” Another word for Sasquatch is Bigfoot.

    DocumentedAnd it has not been reported only once. In the database of the Bigfoot Field Researchers Organization, a group that collects such reports, Michigan has 227 listings, three of them in Cass County. One comes from near Dowagiac, on 27 July 2001, at about half past two in the morning. Two friends, roused by a frightened goose and the sound of snapping branches, took their flashlights outside and found, they said, a large, hairy, ape-like figure crouched in a ditch about thirty feet away. For some ten minutes it froze whenever the lights were on it and stirred when they went off. When the batteries began to fade, one of them ran in for the car keys, to use the headlights. When he came back, it was gone.

    DocumentedWiden the circle and the count climbs. The same database lists 227 reports in Michigan, 82 in Indiana, 305 in Illinois and 335 in Ohio: 949 in the four Great Lakes states closest to this valley. Around this valley alone it lists three in Cass County, three in Berrien, three in Kalamazoo, one in St. Joseph County, Indiana, one in Elkhart County and two in La Porte.

    DocumentedAcross the country, the same group says it has received nearly 75,000 reports since the 1990s, and counts some 5,500 of them as credible. The state-by-state counts in its database add up to more than 5,700.

    ReadingNone of that is proof of anything. A report is a story someone tells; it is not a body, or a bone, or a photograph that holds up. But it is worth knowing that the witnesses of 1964 were not the last people in this valley to come in from the dark with that look on their faces. Thirty-seven years later, a few miles away, two more did. And somewhere out past the last porch light, in the swamps and the river bottoms and the long dark woods, the valley is keeping its counsel.

    Other footsteps in the valley

    The Bigfoot Field Researchers Organization’s database is only one collection of stories, and nobody checks them all. But read the reports it holds for the counties around this valley one after another, as we did, and something begins to show: the same kind of night, the same kind of place, the same dog that will not go on.

    DocumentedNear Union, Cass County, the late 1960s. A few years after Dewey Lake, a summer-camp riding instructor was out on a trail near the camp in the evening, in the hardwoods near U.S. 12, toward the St. Joseph River, when her horse spooked and bolted. Something large and pale kept pace with them through the trees, running alongside, about as tall as she was in the saddle. She let the horse run. The report, given years later by her nephew, says she was badly frightened and rarely spoke of it again.

    DocumentedNear Kalamazoo, the 1970s. A girl of about seven was running across a school playground at the edge of the pines when she ran straight into something. It was very tall, holding a pine branch aside, with skin she remembered as “tawny reddish” and deep chocolate-brown hair. It let the branch go and walked away. Nine or ten years later, on a winter night at her family’s house, she heard growling and heavy steps outside and saw a large shape look around the corner. In the morning there were big footprints in the snow. “I physically ran into a Sasquatch,” she told the investigators.

    DocumentedStevensville, Berrien County, a winter night about 1974. In a house near the wooded dunes and a cemetery, a twelve-year-old was babysitting four younger brothers when a sound came from right outside the living-room window: a “howling scream that sounded like something dying,” which turned, as it went on, into a gurgling, low, laugh-like chuckle. The child waited half an hour, then went out with a flashlight. There was fresh snow on the ground. There were no tracks. And the family dog was gone.

    DocumentedNear Stillwell, La Porte County, Indiana, October 1987. A man camping alone at dusk, just outside the old Kingsbury ordnance plant, heard rustling and grunting close by. He shouted that he had a gun. A stump landed eight feet from him; then a log, three feet away. He ran. Behind him he heard footsteps on the railroad tracks, about a second apart, and at a dip in the trail he saw something eight or nine feet tall, which screamed: “the most God forsaking scream,” he wrote, “that it would make your blood curdle.” When he came back with two friends three hours later, the tent was torn down. The stump and the log were where they fell.

    DocumentedNear Fulton, Kalamazoo County, spring 1989. Two friends camped by a creek in a swampy wood heard something come up a deer trail, stop about thirty yards off, and scream while it tore at a tree. One loaded a shotgun. The other walked toward it and talked to it, and after a few minutes it went away. The first man says his family had been hearing the thing in those woods since the 1960s. They called it the “Monkey Beast.”

    DocumentedNear Buchanan, February 1993. On a cloudy Saturday morning a hunter went looking for his German shepherd at a beaver pond among the orchards. What he took at first for a clump of dark bushes resolved into a shoulder, an arm and a head. He put it at seven to eight feet, “very muscular.” It turned, took two steps and was gone into the brush. His dog would not go near the place afterward.

    DocumentedSouth Bend, November 2000. At dawn on a foggy Saturday, two thirteen-year-olds hiking in the woods by a creek off Kern Road found pieces of wood scattered around a set of footprints. Then they heard a noise, and one of them saw a hairy, ape-like figure, about six foot eight, no more than twenty yards away. They ran. The boy said afterward that he had read about the Sister Lakes Monster in a library book.

    DocumentedNear Dowagiac, July 2001. The goose, the snapping branches, the thing in the ditch that froze in the flashlight beam, told above.

    DocumentedNear Plainwell, May 2002. North up the Kalamazoo, four people reported a sighting together, at night.

    DocumentedNear Pullman, Allegan County, June 2007. At two in the morning a man and his girlfriend woke to two screams from the woods by a creek-fed swamp, eight or ten seconds apart, from two different places. Their dog went wild and afterward would not go outside. “It sounded like two ladies being killed,” he said. A few nights later, after he shouted into the dark, he heard something run off into the trees on two feet.

    DocumentedOsceola, Indiana, May 2008. At half past three on a Sunday morning, by the swamps and marsh along Baugo Creek, a man heard a howl he had never heard before and could not name. His dog, which went after anything that moved outside, ran out the door, turned straight around and came back in without a sound. It “sent chills up my spine,” he wrote.

    DocumentedMill Creek, La Porte County, Indiana, October 2008. A bowhunter twenty feet up a tree stand at last light watched two nervous does burst out of the brush behind him. Then came a smell like an open septic tank. A rotten log about two feet long struck the base of his tree; something at least the size of a man crashed off through the pawpaw. Forty-five seconds later a deep, rising scream came from the swamp at the far end of the woods. He stayed in the stand for an hour before he dared climb down.

    DocumentedThe Paw Paw Lake country, June 2012. At dusk, in the woods between Lake Michigan and Paw Paw Lake, a man walking his dog heard what sounded like a large stick break. The dog stared into the trees, “the fur on the back of his neck … standing straight up.” In the morning there was a print in the soil, he said, five inches longer than his own shoe, sunk deep where his own weight left no mark. The investigator noted that the photograph was poorly defined, and could have been made by a person.

    ReadingNow look at them together. Almost every one is at the edge of water: a lake, a river, a creek, a beaver pond, a swamp. Almost every one is at the edge of the dark: dusk, night, the early hours. Again and again the first witness is not a person but a dog, or a horse, or a goose. Three times, something threw wood, or left it scattered, as if to say it was there. And again and again, the thing does not come closer. It watches, or it paces, or it turns and takes two steps and is gone.

    ReadingThere are plain explanations for every one of them, and they deserve to be said. A red fox’s scream can sound like a woman, or a child. A bear standing up is taller than a man, and a man in camouflage in a swamp can look like a bush. A goose and a dog will panic at a raccoon. People who have heard a story tend to see it. And a report is not a body, or a bone, or a photograph that holds up; none of these has been proved.

    ReadingBut none of them has been ruled out, either. Each one is a story somebody told, from somewhere in this valley, about a night they never forgot.

    The legend that would not die

    DocumentedThe monster outlived the week that made it. The sellers of 1964 had their Monster Burgers and their getaway gas. Sister Lakes Brewing Company has since poured a “Dewey Lake Monster,” a double IPA.

    DocumentedIt never quite stopped being seen, either. In 2016 a Kalamazoo television station, WWMT, closed its “Michigan Monsters” series at Dewey Lake and counted nearly thirty sightings over the years, from Dowagiac as far as Decatur, some with cars said to have been smashed or turned over. Wendy Alexander, who grew up in Cass County hearing the stories and kept track of new ones, was open-minded but plain about what it would take to settle it: “an actual specimen.”

    DocumentedIn July 2019, at the Dewey Lake boat parade, the Dowagiac Daily News met a family whose son dressed as a sasquatch every year, to keep up, as his mother put it, “the tradition of the Dewey Lake Monster.”

    DocumentedIn October 2020 the Lost Cryptids Conservatory, a Michigan group founded in 2011 by Blake Duescher, held a Dewey Lake Monster Conference at the brewery. A few dozen people came, believers and skeptics both; there were plaster casts of footprints on the tables, and Duescher’s team had walked the woods the day before. What struck him most was how the creature was said to behave: “the level of aggression and harassment is kind of unparalleled.” That same month Southwestern Michigan College in Dowagiac released a radio play, Terror on Dewey Lake. The brewery has since hosted “Bigfoot and Brews,” a whole convention in the monster’s honor.

    ReadingThat is how a legend works. The papers said it was nonexistent, and the sheriff called off the reward, and the pickers went back into the fields. But the name stayed. Sixty years on, people who never heard of the 1964 headlines still know there is something called the Dewey Lake Monster, and still, now and then, slow the car on the back roads at dusk and look into the trees.

    Story or record? Play along

    Now that you have read the record, try these. Here are ten things people say about the Dewey Lake Monster and the country around it. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “The monster scare was front-page news in Detroit.”

    True

    The Detroit News put it on page one on 11 and 12 June 1964.

    “Police caught, or photographed, the creature.”

    False

    The searches found nothing, and by 12 June the South Bend Tribune called the monster “nonexistent.”

    “Berry pickers refused to go into the fields.”

    True

    “‘Monster’ Scare Idles Pickers,” said The Detroit News of 12 June 1964, with “Workers Afraid To Go Into Fields.” It was strawberry season.

    “The first witnesses said it stood about nine feet tall.”

    True, as reported

    About nine feet and 500 pounds is what the first witnesses told the deputies, as Robert Atkinson gathers the 1964 reports. A twelve-year-old girl who saw it on 11 June said seven feet. On 14 June a wire story printed in Indiana had it at ten feet and more than 500 pounds; the monster grew in the telling.

    “In 1964 people called it Bigfoot.”

    Not in the papers

    The 1964 headlines called it the monster, the creature or the thing. The word Bigfoot had been coined only six years before, by a California newspaper in 1958. The Sister Lakes Sasquatch is a later name.

    “It was said to cry like a baby.”

    True, as told

    The story told in the Sister Lakes country today, as Visit Southwest Michigan tells it: a large, hairy creature with glowing red eyes, and “it cried, like a baby.”

    “There was a $1,000 reward for it.”

    True

    A local beer distributor offered $1,000 for the monster taken alive by 22 June, then withdrew it at the sheriff’s request when the calls poured in (as Atkinson reports).

    “Someone once warned travelers off the road to Dewey Lake.”

    True

    The county history of 1882 (p. 356): on a peeled tree at the fork, in red chalk, “Turn to the left and go to the Devil.”

    “The Dewey Lake Monster has been called a Sasquatch.”

    True

    The Wikipedia article on it gives the name “Sister Lakes Sasquatch.” And in 2001, near Dowagiac, two friends reported a large, hairy, ape-like figure.

    “An old county book calls a creature here a “monster.””

    True

    The 1882 history, telling of a great cat in Volinia Township: “the monster had killed a colt … and sucked its blood” (p. 285).

    If you go out there

    After dark at the Sister Lakes

    Stay on the public roads, and leave the farms, the fields and the private shores alone. Turn the engine off. Wait. Listen for the frogs to stop, the way they do when something moves. And if, out past the last porch light, you hear what sounds like a baby crying in the trees, you will know, at least, that you are not the first.
