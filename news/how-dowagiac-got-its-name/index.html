    How Dowagiac got its name

    Series · 2 of 7 ›Go see it7 min read

    Dowagiac was a river before it was a town. The railroad made the village in 1848 and the village took the creek’s name; the county history of 1875 says the word means “fishing-water,” and a newspaper of 2023 says “foraging ground.” We went back to the record.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 7 min read

    Dowagiac, Mich. Dowagiac is the rare valley town that did not grow slowly from a cabin and a mill. It was made almost at once, in 1848, when the Michigan Central Railroad came through and two men laid out a village beside the depot. The name was already waiting for it: the Dowagiac was the creek, and had been in print for at least ten years.

    What the word means is another matter. We went back to Michigan’s first gazetteer of 1838, the county histories of 1875, 1882 and 1906, and what is said about the name today.

    At a glance

    The place
    Dowagiac, Michigan: a city in Cass County, on Dowagiac Creek and the Michigan Central line
    The creek first
    “Dowagiake river” in Blois’s Gazetteer of 1838, ten years before the town
    The plat
    Recorded 16 February 1848, beside the planned depot grounds
    Founders
    Nicholas Cheeseborough, the railroad’s right-of-way agent, and Jacob Beeson of Niles
    Before
    Venice, a “paper town” laid out nearby in 1836 that was never built
    The meaning
    “Fishing-water,” says the county history of 1875; “foraging ground,” say writers today; no early source for either
    Why it matters
    The town is the railroad’s; the name is the creek’s

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • The name belonged to the creek first. Blois’s state gazetteer of 1838 lists the “Dowagiake river” among the county’s water-courses.
    • The village was made by the railroad. Its plat was recorded on 16 February 1848, and “Front street runs parallel to the track.”
    • A paper town, Venice, had been laid out nearby in 1836 and never built.
    • The first post office was kept in a boarding house for railroad hands, the Cataract House.

    What it does not show, yet

    • Who first called the new village Dowagiac.
    • What the word meant to the people who first said it. The county history’s “fishing-water” gives no source.
    • An earlier spelling than Dowagiake, on the maps of the 1820s and 1830s.

    Story or record? Play along

    Here are eight things people say about the name. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “Dowagiac was a creek’s name before it was a town’s.”

    True

    Blois’s Gazetteer of the State of Michigan (1838) lists the “Dowagiake river” in Cass County, ten years before the town was platted.

    “The Michigan Central Railroad chose the name Dowagiac.”

    No record

    Rogers calls the village “Born of the Michigan Central Railroad,” but no source says who picked the name. It was already the creek’s.

    “The village plat was recorded in 1848.”

    True

    Glover, 1906: the original plat “was recorded in the register’s office February 16, 1848.”

    “A town called Venice was laid out near the site twelve years earlier.”

    True

    Rogers calls Venice, laid out in August 1836, a “paper town … known only by name and record at the County Seat.”

    “The 1875 county history says Dowagiac means ‘fishing-water.’”

    True

    Rogers: “The name Dowagiac is of Indian origin, and signifies fishing-water.” He gives no source for it.

    “The nineteenth-century histories say the name means ‘foraging ground.’”

    No

    The 1875 and 1882 histories both say “fishing water.” “Foraging ground” appears in writing of our own century.

    “Dowagiac had a long pioneer history before the railroad came.”

    No

    Rogers, 1875: “Dowagiac had little or no pioneer history.”

    “The town’s first post office was kept in a railroad boarding house.”

    True

    Rogers: the Cataract House, built as a boarding house for railroad hands. “In it the Postoffice was first kept.”

    The creek

    DocumentedThe oldest form of the name we have found is in John T. Blois’s Gazetteer of the State of Michigan (1838). Among the water-courses of Cass County it lists “Christiana river, Corbett’s river, Dowagiake river, Putnam’s creek,” and it says Putnam’s creek discharges “into the Dowagiake river.”

    DocumentedThe low ground around the creek bore the name too. “In 1838,” Rogers writes, “the first road across the Dowagiac Swamp was partially built, and completed the next year.”

    OpenThe hand-coloured maps of the territory from the 1820s and 1830s almost certainly letter the creek too. We have not yet read their lettering.

    Venice, the town that never was

    DocumentedBefore Dowagiac there was Venice, on paper. Rogers: “The ancient town of Venice, situated in that part of the Township, where Dowagiac now stands, was a ‘paper town’ laid out by Orlando Crane, in August 1836, and known only by name and record at the County Seat.” Glover, in 1906, found “a plat of the village of Venice, filed for record August 6, 1836,” and places it diagonally across the township corner from the later town.

    ReadingVenice was one of many paper towns of the land boom of 1836, platted for sale and never built. It shares nothing with Dowagiac but the ground, or the ground next door.

    A town born of the railroad

    DocumentedRogers tells it plainly. In 1847 Nicholas Cheeseborough (Glover spells it Cheesebrough) “was employed by the Michigan Central Railroad Company to procure the right of way from Kalamazoo to Niles. As soon as the location of the depot sites was determined at headquarters, he associated with himself Jacob Beeson, of Niles, and together they purchased (in Mr. Beeson’s name) the northeastern eighty acres in Pokagon Township and soon after had it platted in village lots.” They gave the railroad its right of way and “spacious depot grounds, a portion of which was set aside as a public park.”

    DocumentedGlover adds that the eighty acres, in the northeast corner of Pokagon township, were bought of Patrick Hamilton, and that the original plat of Dowagiac “was recorded in the register’s office February 16, 1848.” The railroad was not yet running; trains reached Niles that October. “In the words of the plat, ‘Front street runs parallel to the track of the Michigan Central railroad.’”

    DocumentedThe first post office, Rogers says, was kept in the Cataract House, a boarding house built for the railroad hands. Postal listings date the Dowagiac office from 1848. The first sermon in the village was preached in July 1848. “Dowagiac,” Rogers sums up, “had little or no pioneer history.”

    ReadingNobody records who decided to call the new village Dowagiac. The creek and the swamp already had the name, and the depot sat in the creek country; the name was the obvious one.

    Fishing-water, or foraging ground?

    DocumentedRogers, 1875: “The name Dowagiac is of Indian origin, and signifies fishing-water.” The 1882 history agrees: “The name of this stream is of Indian origin, and its meaning is ‘fishing water.’” Neither says who told them.

    DocumentedSome writers today give “foraging ground.” A Dowagiac newspaper series for the city’s 175th anniversary, in 2023, gives the source word as Ndowagayuk and the meaning as “foraging ground,” reflecting the area’s game, fruit, grain and herbs.

    ReadingBoth meanings are explanations, not records. The nineteenth-century one came from settlers’ hearsay; the modern one is a reading from the language, and may well be the better. What the word meant to the people who first named the creek is not written down anywhere we have found.

    The name on paper

    Play the names in order, from the paper town to the city.

    • 1836 Venice (the paper town nearby)
    • 1838 Dowagiake river (Blois’s gazetteer)
    • 1848 Dowagiac (the plat, 16 February)
    • 1848 Dowagiac (the post office)
    • 1875 Dowagiac (Rogers)
    • 2023 Ndowagayuk (a modern reading)

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Who named the village Dowagiac? The recorded plat of 16 February 1848, at the Cass County register of deeds, may say who signed it and under what name.
    2. How do the early maps spell the creek? The territorial maps of the 1820s and 1830s are the next place to look.
    3. Where did “fishing-water” come from? Rogers names no informant.
