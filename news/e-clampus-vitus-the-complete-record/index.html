    E Clampus Vitus

    E Clampus Vitus: the complete record

    Every lodge of the order on record before 1905, from Indiana in 1846 to Sierraville in 1904, each with its earliest source; the words and customs of the old order; the revival of 1931 and the chapters today; the myths set beside the record; and the founding documents in full.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 49 min read

    New finding What’s new here

    • The earliest dated mention of E Clampus Vitus yet found anywhere: a letter from Connersville, Indiana, printed in the Indiana American of Brookville on 27 February 1846, which speaks of “the ‘Eclampsus Vitus’ society, an order of which, we have in our town.” The order’s own histories give no dated record before 1847.

      Found by Michael Urbanski, Project Scroll, 22 October 2026.

    The whole order, on paper. In 1966 the historians of E Clampus Vitus closed their history with a wish: that “some day someone will attempt to research and compile a complete list of the number and location of the lodges formed during this earlier period.” This page is that list, as far as the surviving record allows, and everything else we have been able to find about the order from its first appearance in print in 1846 to its fifty-one chapters today.

    It began with one Michigan county history and a lodge at Cassopolis in 1846. That story has its own page. This one is the whole record: every place the order is known to have met before 1905, with the earliest surviving record for each and a link to it; the words and customs of the old order, dated by when they first appear; the claims that are often repeated, set beside what the records show; the founding documents in full; and the books and archives where the rest lies.

    ReadingRead down the dates and the shape of the story is plain. The order was in print in Indiana in 1846, in Pennsylvania in 1847, in Missouri and Illinois in 1849, in Virginia, Texas and Georgia by 1852, and in California from the spring of 1852. In California it spread from Stockton and the southern mines in 1852 and 1853, boomed in 1855 and 1856, when Sacramento initiated “nearly five hundred persons … within three weeks,” and reached the northern camps by the end of the decade. It was still meeting in the 1880s and was initiating candidates at Sierraville in 1904.

    Before California, 1845–1853

    DocumentedThe order’s first appearance in print is a joke in a newspaper letter from Connersville, Indiana, in February 1846. By July it had “Tents” at Indianapolis numbered at least to 43; by that winter a Centreville lodge was asking the Indiana legislature for a charter; and by the spring of 1846 a lodge was meeting at Cassopolis, Michigan, with four charter members whose names survive. The whole Indiana and Michigan record, with the House Journal pages, is in the Cassopolis case file.

    DocumentedIn 1847 a travelling agent for a patent cutting-box came to Bedford, Pennsylvania, “from the west,” and founded a lodge that met in the grand jury room by the light of one tallow candle. Judge William M. Hall, a member, wrote it all down in 1890, down to the hailing sign, the oath and the names of the officers; his chapter is transcribed in full below. In 1849 a lodge at Metropolis, Illinois, kept a record book as “Division No. 110,” and the ritual was printed at the office of the Bowling Green Journal in Missouri.

    DocumentedBy March 1852 the order had reached Alexandria, Virginia, where a correspondent of the Gazette “exposed” it as a hand in a vice. A travelling card of “San Antonio Sanhedrim No. 21,” Texas, is dated 30 May 1852, and a lodge room at Columbus, Georgia, was described in December 1852. Ephraim Bee’s town of West Union is first tied to the order in the Weston Herald of November 1853, known only from a quotation of 1931; by 1867 the Wheeling Intelligencer could call Bee “the recognized American head” of the order.

    Be initiated: Bedford, 1847

    No ritual of the 1840s survives, but one member wrote down exactly what happened to a candidate. Step inside.

    From Judge William M. Hall’s own account of the lodge at Bedford, Pennsylvania, in 1847 (Reminiscences and Sketches, 1890, pp. 121–127). Every quoted word is his.

    1. The door

      Your friend leads you to the courthouse. A sentinel armed with a musket stands in the entry. He raps three times; the door opens an inch, and a stern voice demands: Who is there and what is wanted? By virtue of what authority do you venture into the sacred precincts of the ancient and venerable order of the Ecclampsis Vitis?

    2. The room

      Two inside sentinels with bayoneted muskets take your arms and march you in. It is the grand jury room. The only light is a short piece of tallow candle standing on the table by a spot of its own grease; it serves only to make darkness visible and show the eyes of the circle of members.

    3. The patriarch

      Behind a long table the grand worthy patriarch rises and puts on a hat made of brown paper. The origin of the order, he tells you, was lost in the mist of antiquity. It will nurse you when sick and bury you when dead, and its benefits come without money and without price. How, you will learn in the royal arch degree.

    4. The oath

      You raise your hand and swear to keep the secrets, or else your heart may be cut from your living body and be burned, palpitating, before your eyes.

    5. The first sign

      Place your chin in your hand … Now wave your hand up gracefully in this way three times. And why? That, says the grand worthy, is to keep the oats from falling out.

    6. The grand hailing sign

      Hands at your temples, the fingers upward, like a mule’s ears. Move them backward and forward three times. Now repeat after him: When — shall — we — three — meet — again?

    7. Even now

      Even now, cries the grand worthy, and unrolls before you a large picture of two mules. The room, mute as mice until now, breaks into uproarious shouts of laughter. Some stand dazed; some get mad; in a few minutes everyone is ready to initiate somebody else.

    8. Satisfactory

      There is a torch-light procession behind the Bedford band, the regalia a muslin sash with a star cut out of the heavy purple colored paper that loaf sugar used to come in. It lasts some eight or ten weeks, until Dr. Keyser is initiated, gets furiously mad, and gives the game away. But we had fun — lots of it — while it lasted.

    California, 1852–1904

    DocumentedThe first California notice yet found was printed in the San Joaquin Republican at Stockton on 14 April 1852, calling “THE MEMBERS of the E. C. V.” to their hall on the corner of El Dorado and Centre streets. By July the paper called the order “recently established in this city,” and its lodge, David Lodge No. 1, gave money to the Carson Valley road fund. In San Francisco the order met in the District Court room that summer and gave $100 to the firemen. At Murphys in February 1853 it gave $150 toward a school, and met in a room over Buffum’s saloon “built expressly for the purpose.”

    DocumentedThe great years were 1855 and 1856. Lodges, “Sanhedrims” and “encampments” opened at Sacramento, Marysville, Weaverville, Nevada City, Shasta, Downieville, Yreka, San Andreas, Knight’s Ferry, Spanish Flat and Placerville. Members included lawyers and legislators: David S. Terry was Noble Grand Humbug at Stockton in 1855. The ritual was sold by mail at five dollars a set. Brigham Young, the Shasta Courier reported, “has thundered an anathema” against the order.

    DocumentedThe fun had a long tail. Lodges opened at Susanville in 1860, Napa in 1862, Bear Valley and Coulterville in 1867, Colusa in 1878 and Cedarville in 1879, and in 1884 the Feather River Bulletin reported that at Sierraville “the Royal Platrix has sounded the hewgag.” But the lodges were thinning. The Calaveras lodge “only slumbers,” a Stockton paper wrote in 1871, its properties lost to fire; at Oroville by 1892 the order was “a thing of the past”; and at Marysville in 1899 “the paraphernalia has been stored away.”

    DocumentedSteamboat Jake. The order’s best-loved story comes from Yreka in 1856. Jacob Ehrenbacher paid fifty dollars to join the Odd Fellows, the Masons and the “Eclampus Vitus” at a single sitting; his chanted response “Timbo” became “Steambo,” and the rest is in Harry Wells’s History of Siskiyou County (1881), the earliest source.

    The revival, 1931

    DocumentedIn the spring of 1930, by the order’s own history, the idea came to Carl I. Wheat and Leon O. Whitsell; with George Ezra Dane they revived E Clampus Vitus at a luncheon at the Clift Hotel in San Francisco in 1931, with some ten men present. The first chapter was Yerba Buena No. 1. The link to the old order was Adam Lee Moore, an old member of Balaam Lodge at Sierra City, who “assisted and furnished much information on the ritual and general procedure in vogue during the mining days” (Credo quia absurdum, 1949) and was installed as Clampatriarch.

    DocumentedThe revived order printed its own books, The Enigmatical Book of Vitus (1934), The Curious Book of Clampus (1935) and others, and played its most famous joke in 1936: the brass plate “found” near San Francisco Bay and hailed as Sir Francis Drake’s was a Clamper in-joke, as Edward Von der Porten and colleagues showed in 2002. Marysville’s 1915 incorporation of the old order passed to the revivers.

    The Clampers today

    DocumentedE Clampus Vitus recognises 51 chapters in 11 western states (October 2026), from Yerba Buena No. 1 to the Doc Maynard Chapter No. 54-40 in Washington, chartered in 2006 as the 42nd. Its Grand Council meets each spring at Sonora. It publishes no total of members. The Historical Marker Database lists 1,876 entries in its E Clampus Vitus series (22 October 2026), counting markers the order shares with others; a 2025 study found its hand in at least 149 California Historical Landmark plaques, more than any other group. The red shirts, the hewgag and the cry of “Satisfactory!” carry on.

    Myth or record? Play along

    Every old order gathers stories about itself, and the Clampers have always enjoyed theirs. Here are the twelve most often repeated. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the records show.

    “Founded in 1845 by Ephraim Bee.”

    No record

    No document of 1845 mentions the order. The earliest dated record anywhere is the Connersville letter of February 1846. Bee first appears in connection with the order in 1853, in a Weston paper known only from a 1931 quotation, and then in the Wheeling Intelligencer of 1867. Bee’s own biographer, Boyd Stutler, wrote in 1931 that the ritual was “perhaps” worked out “as early as 1850.” The year 1845 first appears in G. Ezra Dane’s essay of 1938, reasoned from the date of Caleb Cushing’s return from China. Nothing yet found contradicts a founder in the 1840s; nothing yet found names one.

    “Bee got his commission from the Emperor of China, through Caleb Cushing.”

    An old joke

    It is a joke, and an old one. By February 1867 the Wheeling Intelligencer was already reporting that Cushing “is said to be the man who brought the secrets of the order to this country, and communicated them to Mr. Bee,” and that Bee performed the mysteries in Chinese. No commission has ever been produced.

    “The order began in California in 1849 or 1850.”

    No

    The order came to California; it did not begin there. It was on record in Indiana in 1846, in Pennsylvania in 1847 (brought “from the west”), in Missouri and Illinois in 1849, and in Virginia by March 1852. The earliest California record yet found is the Stockton notice of April 1852. The Hangtown lodge of 1850 rests on a newspaper reminiscence of 1896.

    “Joe Zumwalt brought it to California.”

    Remembered, not recorded

    The story was told in his lifetime: in 1898 a Gridley correspondent wrote that J. H. Zumwalt of Mokelumne Hill “enjoys the distinction of being the man who brought the ritual of the Clampers to this State.” The account of how he bought it at Bowling Green, Missouri, in 1849 was printed in 1896. No record of 1849 to 1851 has been found to confirm it.

    “Mokelumne Hill Lodge No. 1001 was the first, in 1851.”

    Partly

    A lodge was at work at Mokelumne Hill by March 1853. The number 1001 is first in print in 1867 and the date 1851 in 1896. Meanwhile Stockton called its own lodge “David Lodge No. 1” and was named in 1853 as “the grand centre and focus of the societies.” Both are probably true in their way: the order’s numbers were part of the joke. Even the plaques disagree: Mokelumne Hill’s (2010) puts Zumwalt’s first chapter there in 1851; Downieville’s (1987) says he founded the first chapter at Downieville.

    “The old order died out in the 1880s.”

    No

    It dwindled, but it never quite died. Sierraville’s Moses Lodge was initiating in 1884 and still in 1904; Marysville revived its lodge in 1888, initiated Lord Sholto Douglas in 1896, and incorporated the order in 1915. An old Sierra City Clamper, Adam Lee Moore, helped revive it in 1931.

    “Steamboat Jake was initiated in 1852, for $98.50.”

    Later retelling

    The earliest source, Harry Wells’s History of Siskiyou County (1881), sets the story at Yreka in 1856. The victim was Jacob Ehrenbacher, who paid fifty dollars to join the Odd Fellows, the Masons and the “Eclampus Vitus” all at once. The date 1852 and the fee of $98.50 come from a 1936 retelling.

    “Mark Twain was a Clamper.”

    No record

    No record of his membership has been found in the California newspapers searched for this page, or in the order’s own histories of 1949 and 1966. The Mark Twain Project dates his hearing of the jumping-frog story to Angel’s Camp in February 1865, from Ben Coon, with no mention of the order. Today’s Samuel L. Clemens Chapter No. 1856 at Winnemucca honours him; a name is not a membership.

    “It was a parody of the Odd Fellows and the Masons.”

    Of all of them

    The earliest descriptions say so in general terms: “in broadest burlesque of legitimate secret organizations” (Cassopolis, written 1875) and “a burlesque on all secret societies” (Bedford, written 1890). Its titles borrowed from several: Bedford’s “worthy patriarch” and “scribe” were the officers of the Sons of Temperance, Indiana’s “Tents” were the Rechabites’, and the “Noble Grand” in Noble Grand Humbug is the Odd Fellows’.

    “Drake’s Plate of Brass was a Clamper joke.”

    True

    The brass plate “found” in 1936 and hailed as Sir Francis Drake’s was made as an in-joke by members of the revived order, aimed at the historian Herbert Bolton. The case was laid out by Edward Von der Porten and colleagues in California History in 2002.

    “The name means something.”

    Nobody knows

    No one has explained it. In March 1852 an Alexandria writer claimed to have discovered the secret: “E. Clampus Vitas, is dog Latin, and means ‘squeezing the life out of you.’” The candidate’s hand, he said, went into a vice. That was a joke too.

    “The Staff of Relief and the Poor Blind Candidate are gold-rush terms.”

    Not found before 1931

    Neither has been found in any nineteenth-century source searched for this page. Both appear in the writings of the revived order. The older words are real: the Noble Grand Humbug from 1852, “Satisfactory” from 1853, the Hall of Comparative Ovations from 1857, the Hewgag from 1884.

    “the first Secret Society, of which there is any record”Howard S. Rogers, History of Cass County, from 1825 to 1875 (Cassopolis, 1875), pp. 253–254 · written by James M. Shepard · the whole passage

    The first Secret Society, of which there is any record, was a Lodge of the “Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus,” which was instituted in the spring of 1846, with Dr. E. J. Bonine, Laban Harter, J. P. Osborn and Dr. L. Osborn as charter members.

    The Order was in broadest burlesque of legitimate secret organizations and was afterward merged in the “Sons of Malta,” which died from exposure (by Frank Leslie) in the next decade.

    The candidates for admission were bound fast, blindfolded and dragged into the hall by halters. They were placed in the most ludicrous positions and required to pledge themselves to performances and courses of conduct which, by a cunningly devised double entendre in the wording of the pledges, were either impossible or eminently ridiculous.

    A peculiarity of human nature, which renders the victim of a “sell” restless and unhappy until he has inveigled others into the same meshes, insured the rapid growth and financial prosperity of this monstrous hoax. Numerous neophites were found to assuage the grief and soothe the wounded pride of the earlier victims.

    A grand ball was given by the Lodge in the winter of 1846–7, at the Union Hotel, at which over two hundred badges of the “Ancient and Honorable Order” were displayed, and that, too, by men who stood among the highest in popular esteem and respectability.

    The (dis) Order collapsed in 1847, partly from lack of raw material and partly from a growing satiety amounting to disgust on the part of the better portion of the members, but it was successfully resurrected in 1860, under the alias of “The Brothers of Charity.”

    The second edition, although enlarged and improved, was of “few days and full of trouble” to all except the charter (?) members.

    The Ancient and Venerable Order of Ecclampsis VitisWilliam M. Hall, Reminiscences and Sketches, Historical and Biographical (Harrisburg, 1890), pp. 121–127 · Bedford, Pennsylvania, 1847 · the whole chapter

    In 1847, while I was reading law, there came to Bedford, from the west, a traveling agent for a patented invention of a cutting-box. He instituted a new secret society called the Ecclampsis Vitis. It was in truth a burlesque on all secret societies—an exaggerated travesty, full of fun and very enjoyable for the younger members. The secret of the thing was wonderfully well kept for many weeks, and the society flourished and had grown to large proportions, with numerous candidates for initiation, at the time of the dénouement, which resulted in its downfall.

    The society was constituted with a worthy patriarch and two past grand worthy patriarchs, with out-door sentinels and in-door sentinels, and divers other officers. The ceremony of initiation was formal, solemn and imposing. The place of meeting was the grand jury room of the court house, quite a large room, now divided by a partition into two rooms, one of which is used as the sheriff’s office and the other by the recorder of deeds.

    Behind a long table sat the grand worthy patriarch, Joe Mann, as homely a specimen of humanity as you could find in a week’s travel, who was then a student-at-law. He was supported on either side by the past grand worthies, John Ottinger and Ben Cromwell. The only light in the room was a short piece of tallow candle, which stood in the center of the table in front of the worthy patriarch, without a candle-stick, supported in an upright position by a spot of its own grease, dropped on the table for that purpose. The dim light only served to make darkness visible and show the eyes of the circle of members who sat around on chairs and benches.

    The candidate for initiation was conducted by the friend who had proposed him, to the sentinel, who stood, armed with a musket, in the entry on the outside of the door, to whom he made known that he had a candidate duly voted on and now present for induction into the ancient and venerable order of Ecclampsis Vitis; whereupon the outside sentinel rapped thrice upon the door, which was opened an inch or so, and a stern voice from within demanded, “Who is there and what is wanted? By virtue of what authority do you venture into the sacred precincts of the ancient and venerable order of the Ecclampsis Vitis?” When informed in extended language, formal and precise, of the name of the candidate and of the member who vouched for him, the door was opened and the candidate turned over, in the dim light, to the charge of two stalwart inside sentinels, armed with bayoneted muskets, each of whom took him by an arm and marched him up in front of the grand worthy patriarch, whom one of them informed, in stilted language, “Most worthy patriarch of the ancient and venerable order of Ecclampsis Vitis, we present here to you, for initiation into the mysteries and benefits of our most wonderful, and benevolent, and exalted order, Mr. Blank, who is vouched for as being an intelligent, upright and virtuous citizen, free from bodily infirmity, by our most worthy Brother Blank, whose name, having been duly proposed, was voted on, and no single black ball having been cast against him, was duly accepted, and is now present for initiation into the signs and mysteries of our beloved, benevolent, and truly charitable order.”

    Whereupon the worthy patriarch arose and put on a hat made of brown paper, imposing-looking in the gloom of the room and the fright of the candidate, and in a short address informed the candidate that the origin of the order was lost in the mist of antiquity. That it embraced people of all nations—go where he would, he would find members who would welcome him with charitable hearts and hands to all social privileges. That he would be taken care of in sickness, and if he died he would be buried at the expense of the order. That the society was far superior to other secret societies which made greater pretensions—that it was older, wider spread and particularly that it excelled them in the fact that its benefits were extended without requiring of the members any fees or charges—that it conferred its benefits without money and without price. How it did this was a mystery that he could not then learn, but that hereafter, as he advanced to the royal arch degree, he would know more. That secrecy was essential to the existence and welfare of the society, and the members were all bound by a solemn oath, which would now be administered to him by the grand worthy scribe.

    He was then sworn by the uplifted hand, “In the presence of the Great Creator of the universe, from whose all-seeing eye nothing can be hid, you do solemnly pronounce and declare that you will faithfully keep the secrets of the ancient and venerable order of the Ecclampsis Vitis, and you do solemnly agree that if you violate this oath, your heart may be cut from your living body and be burned, palpitating, before your eyes, and your body be disembowled, and quartered, and burned, and the ashes thrown to the four winds of heaven,” etc.

    The oath being taken, the candidate was again brought before the grand worthy, who said to him, “I will now initiate you into the signs of the order. Wherever you go the broad world around, you will be recognized by these signs by the brethren of the order, and be welcomed with joy. Place your chin in your hand, in this way, inserting the end of your chin between your thumb and forefinger and extending the hand held level in front of the chin.” When the candidate had done this, he proceeded, “Now wave your hand up gracefully in this way three times, thus” (showing him, and the candidate doing it). “That,” proceeded the grand worthy, “is to keep the oats from falling out.”

    This was the first intimation of the burlesque except the paper cap, but, in the novelty of the situation and the grave and solemn-looking surroundings and in the dim light, the candidate would fail to notice the intimation of the oats, as he had previously failed to notice the tallow dip and the paper cap. The G. W. P. would then proceed: “I will now teach you the grand hailing sign. Put your hands in this way, as I do.” He would then put an extended hand on each side of his head, with his thumbs against his temples and the fingers upward, like a mule’s ears, and required the candidate to do the same. “Now move them backward and forward three times, thus.” Further addressing the candidate, “You will now repeat after me these words: When—repeat, sir! When—shall—we—three—meet—again?” This being done, raising his hand solemnly, the grand worthy would cry with a loud voice, “Even now,” and would unroll in front of the new brother a large picture of two mules, which he was left to look upon, and the initiation was over, amid the uproarious shouts of laughter of the assembled throng, who up to this time had been as mute as mice. Some would stand dazed, some got mad; but in a few minutes all would get over it and be ready to enjoy the fun of initiating somebody else.

    Well, we had a torch-light procession all over town and a speech. Jacques W. Johnson, a young lawyer, delivered an oration on the order in the court house. Judge Black was there to hear it, and old Mr. Russell and all the beauty and the chivalry of the village. Johnson’s oration was in manuscript, and Judge Black borrowed it and read it through with a great deal of pleasure, he said. He couldn’t understand, however, how the society existed without levying contributions on its members; he couldn’t possibly see or comprehend where the funds came from. But that was a mystery only to be learned by initiation into the royal arch degree, and the judge did not seem willing to go that far.

    John Ottinger and Ben Cromwell were made past grand worthies because of their ability to sit and look wise with grave faces. Nothing would stir the facial expression of either into animation except the immediate prospect of a drop of old rye.

    One of the laughable features of the meetings was to call upon P. G. W. Ottinger for a story. During the intervals of initiation, a brother would rise and gravely move that P. G. W. Ottinger now favor the lodge with the narration of some little incident or story from his extensive repertory. This being voted, in response, Ottinger told always the same story, utterly oblivious that he had ever told it before. It became a matter of absorbing interest to see how often he would, with no glimmer of recollection that he had previously told it, retell the story, and as long as the institution survived, some eight or ten weeks, Ottinger repeated, with a grave face, the same little story, and wound up with a peculiar laugh, which contorted his face but gave forth no sound, except a rumble, as if a laugh was rolling around somewhere in the cavities of his capacious abdominal development.

    Cromwell rode at the head of the torch-light procession, as chief marshal, on a gray horse. The regalia was a strip of muslin about two yards long and four inches wide, which passed over the right shoulder and was fastened in a knot at the waist on the left side, ornamented with a star cut out of the heavy purple colored paper that loaf sugar used to come in. For the officers the muslin was blue. The torches were balls of candle wick soaked in turpentine and fastened with wire upon upright sticks. The Bedford band headed the procession.

    Samuel Shuck was chairman of the committee on regalia, and John H. Filler of the committee on torch lights.

    Dr. Keyser was the first man to betray the order. He had been peculiarly anxious for initiation. His name was pending a good while before he was voted in. Somehow we feared he might divulge it, and hesitated to trust him. His anxiety to join sprang from an idea that the order was going to be a great power socially and politically—just the thing for a rising young doctor to belong to.

    But Keyser, when initiated, got furiously mad, and would not be placated, and denounced the order as a burlesque. We talked some of drowning him, but didn’t do it. Our fun was done for, and the ancient and venerable order of Ecclampsis Vitis passed away forever, so far as the village of Bedford was concerned. But we had fun—lots of it—while it lasted.
