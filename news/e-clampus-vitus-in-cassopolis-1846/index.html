    Discovery

    A lodge of E Clampus Vitus, in Cassopolis, in 1846

    Two county histories record a lodge of the “Ancient and Honorable Order of Eclampsus Vitus” at Cassopolis, Michigan, in the spring of 1846: four charter members named, a ball of two hundred badges, and a date earlier than any lodge of the order we have been able to find in print. Here is the whole record, what it does and does not prove, and the questions still open.

    Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 14 min read

    Cassopolis, Mich. In 1875 the first history of Cass County, Michigan, printed a short account of “the first Secret Society, of which there is any record” in the county: a lodge of the “Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus,” instituted at Cassopolis in the spring of 1846. Seven years later a second county history printed it again. Neither book was written for the order now known as E Clampus Vitus, and so far as we can find, no historian of that order has ever cited either one.

    The record

    This is the passage as Alfred Mathews printed it in 1882, page 174, under the heading “The Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus.” A footnote credits it to “James M. Shepard, in Roger’s ‘History of Cass County.’” It is reproduced in full; the page photographs are below.

    “The first secret society of which there is any record was a lodge of the Ancient and Honorable Order of Eclampsus Vitus, which was instituted in the spring of 1846 with Dr. E. J. Bonine, Laban Harter, J. P. Osborn and Dr. L. Osborn as charter members.

    “The order was in broadest burlesque of legitimate secret organizations, and was afterward merged in the ‘Sons of Malta,’ which died from exposure (by Frank Leslie) in the next decade.

    “The candidates for admission were bound fast, blindfolded and dragged into the hall by halters. They were placed in the most ludicrous positions and required to pledge themselves to performances and courses of conduct which by a cunningly devised double entendre in the wording of the pledges were either impossible or eminently ridiculous.

    “A peculiarity of human nature which renders the victim of a ‘sell’ restless and unhappy until he has inveigled others into the same meshes, insured the rapid growth and financial prosperity of this monstrous hoax. Numerous Neophites were found to assuage the grief and soothe the wounded pride of the earlier victims.

    “A grand ball was given by the lodge in the winter of 1846–47, at the Union Hotel, at which over two hundred badges of the ‘Ancient and Honorable Order’ were displayed, and that, too, by men who stood the highest in popular esteem and respectability.

    “The (dis)order collapsed in 1847, partly from lack of raw material and partly from a growing satiety amounting to disgust on the part of the better portion of the members, but it was successfully resurrected in 1860 under the alias of the ‘Brothers of Charity.’

    “The second edition, although enlarged and improved, was of ‘few days and full of trouble’ to all except the charter members.”

    The original, in Howard S. Rogers’s History of Cass County, from 1825 to 1875 (Cassopolis, 1875), pages 253 and 254, differs in small ways that matter. Rogers prints the order’s name as “the ‘Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus,’” the respectable men as those who “stood among the highest,” and the last line as “to all except the charter (?) members.” The question mark is in the 1875 text; Mathews dropped it. The phrase “of few days, and full of trouble” is from the Book of Job (14:1), an old funeral text.

    Who wrote it

    Mathews names the author: James M. Shepard. He did not see the lodge. Shepard was a small child in 1846 and came to Cassopolis only on 3 September 1868, as the village dentist; he later owned the county’s newspaper, the Cassopolis Vigilant (sole proprietor from 1878), and was elected to the Michigan Senate in 1878 (Glover, 1906, pp. 556–558). When he wrote, in about 1875, all three of the founders we can trace were still living in or near the county. The account is a written record of local memory, set down about thirty years after the events, and it should be read as that, not as a minute book.

    The four founders

    DocumentedDr. Evan J. Bonine. Born at Richmond, Wayne County, Indiana, on 10 September 1821, of Quaker family; read medicine at Centreville, Indiana, and graduated from the Ohio Medical College at Cincinnati in 1843; married Eveline Beall in 1844 and practised at Cassopolis. He was twenty-four in the spring of 1846. In 1849 he went overland to California with his brother-in-law Laban Harter, and returned in 1851 to settle at Vandalia. He was surgeon of the 2nd Michigan Infantry in the Civil War, sat in the Michigan House for Cass County (1853) and Berrien County (1865–68, 1873) and in the Senate (1869–70), and was mayor and postmaster of Niles, where he died in December 1892. (Michigan Legislative Biography; Portrait and Biographical Record of Berrien and Cass Counties, 1893, quoting the Niles Daily Star.)

    DocumentedLaban Harter. Of the four he is the least known. In the county histories of Cass and of Berrien he appears only on this charter. Two later records place him: an “L. Harter, Treasurer,” of the Niles Horse-Thief Association, organized in May 1853 (Ellis and Johnson, History of Berrien and Van Buren Counties, 1880), and “the old Loban Harter residence” at Niles, where Bonine’s son Dr. Fred N. Bonine was born on 21 October 1863 (Niles Daily Star, obituary of Dr. Fred N. Bonine, 23 August 1941). Bonine’s 1892 obituary calls him Bonine’s brother-in-law. OpenThrough whom they were brothers-in-law, and Harter’s own dates, are not yet known.

    DocumentedJ. P. Osborn: Jordan P. Osborn. Born 6 August 1823, a son of the Quaker minister Charles Osborn; named by Mathews as “Jordan P., who is a resident of Cassopolis,” and later a village trustee and, in 1876, President of the Village of Cassopolis. He was twenty-two in the spring of 1846. His elder brother Parker B. Osborn, born 1821, kept a harness shop at Cassopolis but removed to Wilmington, Ohio; the charter’s initials, J. P., point to Jordan.

    DocumentedDr. L. Osborn: Dr. Leander Osborn, of Vandalia. Born 27 December 1825, a son of Josiah Osborn and grandson of Charles Osborn (Mathews, 1882). He began reading medicine with Dr. Bonine in 1847 and was afterward his partner for two years. He was twenty in the spring of 1846 and not yet a doctor: the title in the 1875 account is the one he held when it was written.

    ReadingSo the lodge’s four were young men of one small circle: a young doctor, his brother-in-law, and two Osborns, one of them his future student. Two of the four, Bonine and Harter, would be in California within three years.

    The room

    DocumentedThe Union Hotel was the frame tavern built by Eber Root, one of the first two men licensed to keep a tavern in Cassopolis, on 24 January 1832 (Rogers, 1875). It stood on the west side of Broadway at the public square (Glover, 1906). The 1860 wall map of Cass, Van Buren and Berrien counties prints one word on that block: HOTEL. Six years after the ball, on 12 June 1852, the county’s Masons held their first meeting “in the Union Hotel” (Mathews, 1882, p. 174): the kind of order the burlesque was made to mock, organizing under the same roof. The corner is still there; the hotel is not, and there is no marker. What became of the building is the subject of Case 2.

    What became of the lodge

    DocumentedBy the account’s own words, the lodge collapsed in 1847 and was “resurrected in 1860 under the alias of the ‘Brothers of Charity,’” and the order “was afterward merged in the ‘Sons of Malta,’ which died from exposure (by Frank Leslie) in the next decade.”

    ReadingThat merger needs care. No lodge of the Sons of Malta is dated before 1854 (a New Orleans lodge, November 1854), so the lodge of 1846, gone by 1847, cannot have merged into it. The revival of 1860 could have: the Sons of Malta were then at their height, and the exposure of their ritual in Frank Leslie’s Illustrated Newspaper early in 1860 is generally reckoned to have finished them. The account’s “in the next decade” is loose, but nothing in it is impossible.

    Where it fits in the order’s history

    E Clampus Vitus is known today as the historical society of the old West, the “Clampers” whose plaques mark forgotten places across California and beyond. Its own history runs like this, and every date below is the order’s or its historians’, not ours.

    DocumentedThe founding legend. The order traces itself to Ephraim Bee, innkeeper and blacksmith of West Union, in what is now West Virginia. The marker at West Union, put up in 2003, says that by legend Bee received his commission “around 1845.” No document from those years has been produced to date it. Jennifer Wilt of the Doddridge County Heritage Guild, working from the county’s court books and the period press, writes that “shortly after 1850” Bee proclaimed himself Grand Hototote, and that she has found newspaper references to the order “as early as 1853.” An 1896 account in the Parkersburg State Journal has its writer meeting Bee in the fall of 1847, and finding, when they met again “some years afterward,” that Bee “had concocted the celebrated secret order” in the meantime.

    DocumentedThe eastern lodges. The order’s historian Hugh Shumate, as published by the Julia C. Bulette Chapter, writes: “There is documentation of lodges in Bedford Pennsylvania, (1847); Metropolis, Illinois (1849)” and Bowling Green, Missouri (1849); a lodge at Dahlonega, Georgia, is evidenced from a California newspaper of 7 March 1853.

    DocumentedCalifornia. The order’s own 1966 history, Then and Now, says it “is generally accepted” that Joe Zumwalt brought the ritual to Hangtown (Placerville) in 1849, where a lodge “didn’t last,” and that he “did establish the first lodge of ECV by 1851” at Mokelumne Hill. Shumate dates the Hangtown attempt to 1850. The same book tells how Steamboat Jake, a merchant from Yreka, was brought “bound and blindfolded” before the “Hall of Comparative Ovations” in 1852.

    DocumentedThe gap the order’s own history leaves. Then and Now says that travelling salesmen were taken into the order, “with or without authority from Bee,” and that by 1849 “the East and Middle West were dotted with Clamper Lodges.” It names none. Its closing postscript hopes that “some day someone will attempt to research and compile a complete list of the number and location of the lodges formed during this earlier period.”

    ReadingRogers names one. A county seat in the Middle West, with a town, a season, a building, four names and a count of badges: Cassopolis, spring 1846. On the evidence we have been able to find, that is the earliest firmly dated lodge of the order in the surviving record, earlier than the order’s own documented dates in both the state of its founding and the state of its fame. We set it out as the first line of the list the order asked for in 1966.

    The spelling

    Both Michigan histories spell the name “Eclampsus,” with an S. The order’s historian writes that Carl Wheat chose the present spelling, “Clampus,” in 1931, when the order was revived in San Francisco. The 2003 marker in Ephraim Bee’s own town still reads “E Clampsus Vitus.” The Michigan books are not misspelling the name; they spell it as it was spelled before 1931.

    A timeline

    • 24 Jan 1832 Eber Root licensed to keep a tavern at Cassopolis; he builds the frame house that becomes the Union Hotel.
    • c. 1845 The order’s legend: Ephraim Bee’s commission. (Undated in any surviving record.)
    • Spring 1846 The Cassopolis lodge is instituted: Bonine, Harter, J. P. Osborn and L. Osborn.
    • Winter 1846–47 The grand ball at the Union Hotel: “over two hundred badges.”
    • 1847 The lodge collapses. Bedford, Pennsylvania: the earliest dated eastern lodge. Leander Osborn begins reading medicine with Bonine.
    • 1847 (fall) The 1896 Parkersburg account’s first meeting with Bee.
    • 1849 Bonine and Harter go overland to California. Metropolis and Bowling Green lodges. Hangtown attempt (1966 history).
    • 1850 Hangtown attempt (Shumate). “Shortly after 1850”: Bee proclaims himself Grand Hototote (Wilt).
    • 1851 Mokelumne Hill: the first lasting lodge in California. Bonine returns to Michigan.
    • 12 Jun 1852 The Masons’ first meeting at Cassopolis, in the Union Hotel.
    • 1853 Earliest newspaper references to the order (Wilt).
    • 1854 Earliest dated lodge of the Sons of Malta.
    • 1860 The Cassopolis lodge revived as “The Brothers of Charity.” Frank Leslie exposes the Sons of Malta.
    • 1868 James M. Shepard comes to Cassopolis.
    • 1875 Rogers prints Shepard’s account.
    • 1882 Mathews prints it again, naming Shepard.
    • 1931 The order revived in San Francisco; Carl Wheat fixes the spelling “Clampus.”
    • 1966 Then and Now asks for a list of the early lodges.

    What we are not saying

    • Not that the order was founded at Cassopolis. The account says a lodge of the order was “instituted” there. Rogers uses the same word on the same page for the Odd Fellows lodge instituted at Cassopolis in February 1847 under a dispensation from a Grand Master: a local lodge of an order that already existed.
    • Not that the evidence is like for like. The Cassopolis account is local memory written down about thirty years later by a man who was not there. Wilt’s dates come from contemporary court books and newspapers. Ours is a claim about the surviving record, not about what may have happened unrecorded.
    • Not that the question is closed. Two histories of the order have not yet been read for this: Lois Rather, Men Will Be Boys: The Story of E Clampus Vitus (Oakland, 1980), and Seth Slopes, E Clampus Vitus: Now and Then (1979). Either could hold an earlier eastern lodge. Until they are read, the claim is provisional.
    • Not that Bonine or Harter carried the order west. The dates line up; no document connects them to any California lodge.

    If anyone can show a lodge of the order dated earlier than the spring of 1846, with a place and names, we will say so on this page, with thanks and with their name.

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. Each one says where the answer is most likely to be. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Is there an earlier lodge? Rather (1980) and Slopes (1979), by interlibrary loan; and the order’s papers in the Holt-Atherton Special Collections, University of the Pacific, Stockton.
    2. Who told Shepard? He came in 1868 and wrote in about 1875, when Bonine and both Osborns were still living. His own question mark after “charter” suggests he was not sure the lodge ever had one.
    3. Did anyone write it down in 1846? Cassopolis had two papers that year, The Literary News, a small sheet of gossip and humour, and the Cass County Advocate. Any surviving issue could carry a notice of the ball.
    4. Who was Laban Harter? His dates, his trade, and how he was Bonine’s brother-in-law. The Cass County marriage records begin in 1837; the 1850 census of Berrien County; Silverbrook Cemetery at Niles.
    5. Did Bonine or Harter meet the order in California? Bonine’s typescript “Journey to California” is listed at the Fort St. Joseph Museum, Niles, with a copy in the Merrill J. Mattes Library of the Oregon-California Trails Association. Has anyone seen their names in any early Clamper record?
    6. Does any badge survive? Two hundred were worn at one ball. Who made them, and of what, is not recorded.
    7. What was “The Brothers of Charity” of 1860, and was there ever a Sons of Malta lodge at Cassopolis?
    8. What do the Bedford lodge’s records hold? If the 1847 Pennsylvania lodge has a charter list or a building, it belongs beside Cassopolis.
