    A lodge of E Clampus Vitus, in Cassopolis, in 1846

    Two county histories record a lodge of the “Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus” at Cassopolis in the spring of 1846: four charter members named, a ball of two hundred badges, and a date earlier than any lodge of the order yet found in print.

    Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 4 min read

    Cassopolis, Mich. “The first Secret Society, of which there is any record, was a Lodge of the ‘Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus,’ which was instituted in the spring of 1846, with Dr. E. J. Bonine, Laban Harter, J. P. Osborn and Dr. L. Osborn as charter members.” So wrote Howard S. Rogers in his History of Cass County, from 1825 to 1875, published at Cassopolis in 1875, on page 253.

    What the lodge was. “The Order was in broadest burlesque of legitimate secret organizations,” Rogers goes on. “The candidates for admission were bound fast, blindfolded and dragged into the hall by halters,” and made to pledge themselves to things that, by a double meaning in the wording, “were either impossible or eminently ridiculous.” In the winter of 1846 and 1847 the lodge gave a grand ball at the Union Hotel, “at which over two hundred badges of the ‘Ancient and Honorable Order’ were displayed, and that, too, by men who stood among the highest in popular esteem and respectability.”

    Who founded it. Young men. Dr. Evan J. Bonine, born in Richmond, Indiana, on 10 September 1821, had graduated from the Ohio Medical College in 1843 and was practising at Cassopolis. In the spring of 1846 he was twenty-four. In 1849 he and his brother-in-law Laban Harter, another of the four, set out overland together for the California gold fields; Bonine came home in 1851, served as a Civil War surgeon, sat in both houses of the Michigan legislature, and was mayor of Niles. Of the two Osborns, the books say little more than their names.

    What became of it. “The (dis) Order collapsed in 1847,” Rogers writes on page 254, “partly from lack of raw material and partly from a growing satiety amounting to disgust on the part of the better portion of the members, but it was successfully resurrected in 1860, under the alias of ‘The Brothers of Charity.’” He adds that it was “afterward merged in the ‘Sons of Malta,’ which died from exposure (by Frank Leslie) in the next decade.”

    That last line needs care, and we have given it some. No lodge of the Sons of Malta is dated before 1854, so the lodge of 1846, gone by 1847, cannot have merged into it. The revival of 1860 could have: the Sons of Malta were then at their height, and the exposure of their ritual in Frank Leslie’s paper, early in 1860, is generally reckoned to have finished them.

    Told twice. Seven years later Alfred Mathews reprinted the passage almost word for word in his History of Cass County, Michigan (1882), page 174, with a footnote naming its author: James M. Shepard, a Cassopolis man, writing in Rogers’s history. On the same page Mathews records that the county’s Masons held their first meeting “June 12, 1852, in the Union Hotel”: the solemn order meeting under the roof where the burlesque had held its ball.

    The room. The Union Hotel stood at the northwest corner of Broadway and State Street, facing the courthouse square. The 1860 wall map of the county prints one word on that block: HOTEL. The corner is still there. The hotel is not, and there is no marker.

    Why it matters. E Clampus Vitus is known today as the historical society of the old West, the “Clampers” whose plaques stand across California and beyond. The order traces itself to Ephraim Bee of West Union, in what is now West Virginia; the marker there, put up in 2003, says that by legend Bee received his commission “around 1845.” No document from those years has been produced to date it. An 1896 account in the Parkersburg State Journal has a traveller meeting Bee in the fall of 1847 and finding, when they met again “some years afterward,” that Bee “had concocted” the order in the meantime. In California, the order’s own 1966 history, Then and Now, puts a first attempt at Hangtown in 1849 and a lasting lodge at Mokelumne Hill by 1851.

    The same history says that travelling salesmen carried the order from town to town until “by 1849 the East and Middle West were dotted with Clamper Lodges,” yet it names none of them, and closes by hoping that “some day someone will attempt to research and compile a complete list” of those early lodges. Rogers names one. A county seat in the Middle West, a few miles off the Detroit to Chicago road, with four names and a season: spring 1846.

    So far as we have been able to find, that is the earliest dated lodge of the order anywhere in print. If you know of an earlier one, with a date, a place and names, we want to hear about it, and we will say so here.

    Two more threads, still open. Both Michigan books spell the name “Eclampsus,” with an S, as the West Union marker still does. And two of the four founders reached the California diggings in 1849, the very years the order first appears there. Nobody, so far as we know, has ever looked for Bonine or Harter in a Clamper record. Have you seen their names, or Cassopolis, anywhere in the order’s lore?
