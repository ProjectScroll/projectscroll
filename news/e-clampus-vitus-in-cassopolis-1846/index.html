    E Clampus Vitus

    A lodge of E Clampus Vitus, in Cassopolis, in 1846

    From the archiveSeries · 1 of 2 ›37 min read

    Two county histories record a lodge of the “Ancient and Honorable Order of Eclampsus Vitus” at Cassopolis, Michigan, in the spring of 1846: four charter members named, a ball of two hundred badges, and the earliest lodge of the order yet found with its members named. Newspapers of the same year show the order already at work across the state line in Indiana. Here is the whole record, what it does and does not prove, and the questions still open.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 37 min read

    Cassopolis, Mich. Picture a winter night in the county seat of a frontier county not yet twenty years old. The frame tavern on the square is lit up. Inside, more than two hundred people are wearing the badges of a secret society with a name few of them could have spelled the same way twice: the “Ancient and Honorable Order of Eclampsus Vitus.” It is the winter of 1846–47, and the Cassopolis lodge is less than a year old.

    In 1875 the first history of Cass County, Michigan, printed a short account of “the first Secret Society, of which there is any record” in the county: a lodge of the “Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus,” instituted at Cassopolis in the spring of 1846. Seven years later a second county history printed it again. Neither book was written for the order now known as E Clampus Vitus, and so far as we can find, no historian of that order has ever cited either one.

    A word to the Brethren

    If you wear the red shirt, you know the story: Ephraim Bee and his commission, Joe Zumwalt carrying the ritual to Hangtown, the lodge at Mokelumne Hill by 1851, and Carl Wheat bringing the order back to life in San Francisco in 1931. You also know the line in the order’s own 1966 history, that by 1849 “the East and Middle West were dotted with Clamper Lodges,” not one of them named.

    This page names one. It comes with a hall, a ball and four charter members, in the spring of 1846. And the newspapers of that year, just over the state line in Indiana, show the order alive and growing: a lodge at Connersville, a Tent No. 43 at Indianapolis meeting “in full regalia,” and a bill before the legislature to charter a lodge at Centreville. Credo quia absurdum, the motto runs: I believe because it is absurd. Here you will not need to. Every statement below is marked Documented (the record says so), Reading (our interpretation of it) or Open (not yet known), and every source is listed at the foot of the page.

    At a glance

    What

    Where

    When

    Charter members
    Dr. E. J. Bonine, Laban Harter, J. P. Osborn and Dr. L. Osborn; three of them married sisters of the Beall family
    The ball

    Printed again
    Mathews, History of Cass County, Michigan (1882), p. 174
    Why it matters
    The earliest lodge of the order yet found with its members named, and the first known in Michigan. Indiana newspapers of 1846 confirm the order, and its spelling, in the same year

    New: E Clampus Vitus: the complete record. Every lodge of the order on record before 1905, sixty-one places from Texas to Hawaii, each with its earliest source, plus the words of the old order, the myths beside the record and the founding documents in full.

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • A lodge at Cassopolis, spring 1846, with four charter members named, a ball of “over two hundred badges” at the Union Hotel, a collapse in 1847 and a revival in 1860 (Rogers, 1875; Mathews, 1882).
    • A family affair. Three of the four founders married sisters of one family, the Bealls.
    • The order alive in Indiana in 1846: a lodge at Connersville by February, Tent No. 43 at Indianapolis by July, and a “Jackson Lodge” at Centreville that asked the legislature for a charter.
    • The legislature’s answer was a joke at the order’s expense, printed in the official House Journal of January 1847.

    What it does not show, yet

    • Any Michigan newspaper notice of the lodge or the ball.
    • The names of a single Indiana member.
    • How the order reached Cassopolis. But the founders’ family came from Centreville, the very town whose lodge asked for a charter.

    Who’s who

    Dr. Evan J. Bonine 1821–1892 · charter member
    Quaker-born physician from Richmond, Indiana, who learned medicine at Centreville and married there in 1844; practising at Cassopolis at twenty-four. Went overland to California in 1849 and came back in 1851. Later surgeon of the 2nd Michigan Infantry, state legislator, and mayor and postmaster of Niles.
    Laban Harter 1826–1888 · charter member
    Born at Eaton, Ohio. He married Emily Beall at Cassopolis in 1847, went to California with Bonine, and became a merchant at Niles.
    Jordan P. Osborn born 1823 · charter member
    Of Cassopolis; later a village trustee and, in 1876, President of the Village.
    Dr. Leander Osborn born 1825 · charter member
    Jordan’s nephew. He began to read medicine under Bonine in 1847 and became the doctor at Vandalia.
    The Beall sisters Evaline, Emily P. and Helen M.
    Daughters of Nathaniel Beall, a farmer near Centreville, Indiana, and Sarah Talbot, who married at Centreville in 1826. They married Bonine, Harter and Leander Osborn.
    James M. Shepard 1840 or 1842–1927 · the storyteller
    Cassopolis dentist from 1868, later owner of the Cassopolis Vigilant and state senator. He set the account down in about 1875, from what someone told him, and helped with the county history of 1882.
    Eber Root the innkeeper
    Licensed to keep a tavern at Cassopolis on 24 January 1832. His frame tavern became the Union Hotel, where the ball was held.
    Howard S. Rogers and Alfred Mathews the historians
    Authors of the county histories of 1875 and 1882 that printed Shepard’s account.
    Ephraim Bee 1802–1888 · the founder, by legend
    Innkeeper and blacksmith of West Union, in what is now West Virginia, from whom the order traces its descent.
    Joe Zumwalt the man who carried it west
    Said by the order to have brought the ritual to Hangtown in 1849 and to have founded the lodge at Mokelumne Hill by 1851.
    Carl I. Wheat the reviver
    Revived the order in San Francisco in 1931 and, by the order’s historian, chose the spelling “Clampus.”

    Exhibit A: the page that started it

    This is the passage as Alfred Mathews printed it in 1882, page 174, under the heading “The Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus.” A footnote credits it to “James M. Shepard, in Roger’s ‘History of Cass County.’” It is reproduced in full; the page photographs are below.

    “The first secret society of which there is any record was a lodge of the Ancient and Honorable Order of Eclampsus Vitus, which was instituted in the spring of 1846 with Dr. E. J. Bonine, Laban Harter, J. P. Osborn and Dr. L. Osborn as charter members.

    “The order was in broadest burlesque of legitimate secret organizations, and was afterward merged in the ‘Sons of Malta,’ which died from exposure (by Frank Leslie) in the next decade.

    “The candidates for admission were bound fast, blindfolded and dragged into the hall by halters. They were placed in the most ludicrous positions and required to pledge themselves to performances and courses of conduct which by a cunningly devised double entendre in the wording of the pledges were either impossible or eminently ridiculous.

    “A peculiarity of human nature which renders the victim of a ‘sell’ restless and unhappy until he has inveigled others into the same meshes, insured the rapid growth and financial prosperity of this monstrous hoax. Numerous Neophites were found to assuage the grief and soothe the wounded pride of the earlier victims.

    “A grand ball was given by the lodge in the winter of 1846–47, at the Union Hotel, at which over two hundred badges of the ‘Ancient and Honorable Order’ were displayed, and that, too, by men who stood the highest in popular esteem and respectability.

    “The (dis)order collapsed in 1847, partly from lack of raw material and partly from a growing satiety amounting to disgust on the part of the better portion of the members, but it was successfully resurrected in 1860 under the alias of the ‘Brothers of Charity.’

    “The second edition, although enlarged and improved, was of ‘few days and full of trouble’ to all except the charter members.”

    The original, in Howard S. Rogers’s History of Cass County, from 1825 to 1875 (Cassopolis, 1875), pages 253 and 254, differs in small ways that matter. Rogers prints the order’s name as “the ‘Ancient and Honorable Order of the Eclampsus Vitus,’” the respectable men as those who “stood among the highest,” and the last line as “to all except the charter (?) members.” The question mark is in the 1875 text; Mathews dropped it. The phrase “of few days, and full of trouble” is from the Book of Job (14:1), an old funeral text.

    The storyteller: who wrote it

    Mathews names the author: James M. Shepard. He did not see the lodge. Shepard was a small child in 1846 (born at North Brookfield, Massachusetts, on 24 November; the year is 1840 in the histories of 1882 and 1906, and 1842 in Michigan Biographies of 1924 and in his obituary of 1927, which gives his age as 84) and came to Cassopolis only on 3 September 1868, as the village dentist; he joined the Cassopolis Vigilant in 1876 and became its sole proprietor in 1878, and was elected to the Michigan Senate in 1878 (Glover, 1906, pp. 556–558). When he wrote, in about 1875, all three of the founders we can trace were still living in or near the county. The account is a written record of local memory, set down about thirty years after the events, and it should be read as that, not as a minute book.

    Exhibit B: the newspapers of 1846

    DocumentedShepard wrote his account about thirty years after the fact. The newspapers of 1846 did not wait. Just over the state line, in Indiana, the order was in print that very year, and under the same spelling the Cassopolis account uses.

    Indiana American, Brookville · 27 February 1846

    From a letter written at Connersville: the town’s citizens, it says, think two scoffing editors “naturally qualified members of the ‘Eclampsus Vitus’ society, an order of which, we have in our town.”

    Indiana State Sentinel, Indianapolis · 16 July 1846

    “The members of Tent No. 43 of the order of Eclampsus Vitus are notified to meet in their Hall on Thursday Evening, July 16, at early candle lighting, attired in full regalia. A general attendance is requested. By order.”

    Indiana House of Representatives · winter of 1846–47

    Mr. Julian of Wayne County brought in a bill “to incorporate the ancient order of E-clam-peis Vitus” (Indiana State Sentinel, 7 January 1847); the Richmond Palladium of 5 January says it was for a lodge “at Centreville.” On 14 January the committee on corporations recommended its “indefinite postponement,” and the bill was laid on the table.

    Quotations are from the newspapers’ scanned pages at Hoosier State Chronicles. The scans are worn and their machine-read text is garbled in places (the Sentinel’s type reads “Eclumpees” and “E-clam-peis”), so we quote them as closely as the pages allow.

    ReadingSo when the Cassopolis lodge was “instituted” in the spring of 1846, the order was already a going concern in Indiana: a lodge at Connersville by February, Tents at Indianapolis numbered to at least 43 by July, and a lodge at Centreville that wanted a charter from the state. That last detail is worth a smile: Shepard put a question mark after the Cassopolis “charter,” and the Centreville brethren actually asked the legislature for one. Exhibit C tells how that went.

    DocumentedThe Centreville connection. The road from Centreville to Cassopolis runs straight through the founders’ family. The Beall sisters were born at Centreville, where their father, Nathaniel Beall, farmed 160 acres until he died there in January 1845. Dr. Bonine read medicine at Centreville for three and a half years under Dr. John Pricket (Michigan Legislative Biography), and Dr. Pricket had married the girls’ aunt, Emily Talbot. Bonine married the eldest sister, Evaline, at Centreville on 8 August 1844, and brought her to Cassopolis. Her sister Emily married Laban Harter at Cassopolis in September 1847; the youngest, Helen, married Leander Osborn in 1854 (Cope, Genealogy of the Sharpless Family, 1887). OpenDid the Bealls’ circle bring the order north from Centreville, a few months before Centreville’s own Jackson Lodge asked the legislature for a charter? Nothing yet proves it. It is the likeliest route we know.

    Exhibit C: the Jackson Lodge and the Indiana House

    DocumentedThe official Journal of the House of Representatives of Indiana for 1846–47 gives the Centreville lodge its name. On leave granted, Mr. Julian, one of the four members for Wayne County (the Journal lists them as William S. Addleman, Jacob B. Julian, Solomon Meredith and Robert Gordon), brought in House bill No. 242, “An act to incorporate Jackson Lodge of the ancient order of Eclampses Vitus, of Centreville, Indiana.” It was read twice and sent to the committee on corporations.

    DocumentedThe committee’s report, printed in full in the Journal, is one of the funniest pages a state legislature ever published:

    “The committee on corporations, to which was referred bill of the House No. 242, entitled ‘An act to incorporate the Jackson Lodge of the ancient order of Eclampses Vitus, of Centreville, Indiana,’ have had the same under consideration, and are not able to ascertain from the bill itself what objects are to be attained by the proposed corporation; but if they are correctly informed from other sources, the bill is for the benefit of a species of animals with which the country is now abundantly supplied, and of which it is not desirable either to increase the number or improve the breed. They therefore recommend that it be indefinitely postponed.”

    ReadingThe House concurred, and the bill was laid on the table. The committee never names the animal, and it did not need to: the same session had before it a bill “for the improvement of the breed of horses, jack-asses, and mules.” The joke is that the brethren were asses, and the Clampers of any age will recognise the kind of compliment it is. A page away in the same Journal, the same committee recommended passage of a bill to incorporate the Sons of Temperance.

    DocumentedWe have searched the Indiana House and Senate journals and the session laws from 1842 to 1853 for the order under any of its spellings. This is the only bill.

    Exhibit D: the four founders

    DocumentedDr. Evan J. Bonine. Born at Richmond, Wayne County, Indiana, on 10 September 1821, of Quaker family; read medicine at Centreville, Indiana, and graduated from the Ohio Medical College at Cincinnati in 1843; married Evaline (Eveline) Beall at Centreville on 8 August 1844 and practised at Cassopolis. His own mother was a Talbot of the same Quaker family as his wife’s mother. He was twenty-four in the spring of 1846. In 1849 he went overland to California with his brother-in-law Laban Harter, and returned in 1851 to settle at Vandalia. He was surgeon of the 2nd Michigan Infantry in the Civil War, sat in the Michigan House for Cass County (1853) and Berrien County (1865–68, 1873) and in the Senate (1869–70), and was mayor and postmaster of Niles, where he died in December 1892. (Michigan Legislative Biography; Portrait and Biographical Record of Berrien and Cass Counties, 1893, quoting the Niles Daily Star.)

    DocumentedLaban Harter. Of the four he was the least known, and he is now the best placed. He was born at Eaton, Preble County, Ohio, on 28 April 1826, a son of Joseph Harter and Mary Howell (Cope, 1887), so he was nineteen or twenty in the spring of 1846; he died on 10 July 1888 and is buried at Silverbrook Cemetery, Niles, beside his wife Emily P. Harter (1832–1910) (Friends of Silverbrook Cemetery, grave records, lot City 21). Their only daughter, Martha, was “born on a farm near Niles Nov. 10, 1848” (Niles Daily Sun, 26 October 1914), so the young couple were settled near Niles before he went west with Bonine in 1849. Two later records place him in the town: an “L. Harter, Treasurer,” of the Niles Horse-Thief Association, organized in May 1853 (Ellis and Johnson, 1880), and “the old Loban Harter residence” at Niles, where Bonine’s son Dr. Fred N. Bonine was born on 21 October 1863 (Niles Daily Star, 23 August 1941). He married Emily P. Beall at Cassopolis on 5 September 1847, when she was fifteen, and became a merchant at Niles. OpenWhether he found the order in California is not known.

    DocumentedJ. P. Osborn: Jordan P. Osborn. Born 6 August 1823, a son of the Quaker minister Charles Osborn; named by Mathews as “Jordan P., who is a resident of Cassopolis,” and later a village trustee and, in 1876, President of the Village of Cassopolis. He was twenty-two in the spring of 1846. His elder brother Parker B. Osborn, born 1821, kept a harness shop at Cassopolis but removed to Wilmington, Ohio; the charter’s initials, J. P., point to Jordan.

    DocumentedDr. L. Osborn: Dr. Leander Osborn, of Vandalia. Born 27 December 1825, a son of Josiah Osborn and grandson of Charles Osborn (Mathews, 1882). He began reading medicine with Dr. Bonine in 1847 and was afterward his partner for two years. He married Helen M. Beall of Centreville on 12 November 1854 (Mathews, 1882), and was “a successful physician at Vandalia” (Portrait and Biographical Record, 1893, biography of his brother Jefferson Osborn). He was twenty in the spring of 1846 and not yet a doctor: the title in the 1875 account is the one he held when it was written.

    DocumentedThree sisters. When Nathaniel Beall of Niles died in September 1898, his obituary listed among his survivors “Mesdames E.J. Bonine and Laban Harter,” of Niles, “and Mrs. Osborne, of Vandalia” (Niles Daily Star, 12 September 1898, reprinted in the Niles Weekly Mirror, 14 September 1898). Bonine married Evaline Beall at Centreville in 1844, Harter married Emily at Cassopolis in 1847, and Leander Osborn married Helen in 1854. Three of the lodge’s four charter members married sisters of one family. ReadingSo the lodge was very nearly a family affair: a young doctor, two men who were or would become his brothers-in-law, and Jordan Osborn, the uncle of one of them. In 1846 only Bonine was married; Harter and Osborn married into the family afterward, so the lodge may have helped make the family, as much as the family made the lodge. Two of the four, Bonine and Harter, would be in California within three years.

    Bonine and Harter went overland to California in 1849 and came home in 1851, the very years the order took root in the diggings. These are the diggings as the daguerreotypists saw them then. We have no picture of either man.

    Exhibit E: the room

    DocumentedThe Union Hotel was the frame tavern built by Eber Root, one of the first two men licensed to keep a tavern in Cassopolis, on 24 January 1832 (Rogers, 1875). It stood on the west side of Broadway at the public square (Glover, 1906). The 1860 wall map of Cass, Van Buren and Berrien counties prints one word on that block: HOTEL. Six years after the ball, on 12 June 1852, the county’s Masons held their first meeting “in the Union Hotel” (Mathews, 1882, p. 174): the kind of order the burlesque was made to mock, organizing under the same roof. The corner is still there; the hotel is not, and there is no marker. What became of the building is the subject of Case 2.

    Exhibit F: what became of the lodge

    DocumentedBy the account’s own words, the lodge collapsed in 1847 and was “resurrected in 1860 under the alias of the ‘Brothers of Charity,’” and the order “was afterward merged in the ‘Sons of Malta,’ which died from exposure (by Frank Leslie) in the next decade.”

    ReadingThat merger needs care. No lodge of the Sons of Malta is dated before 1854 (a New Orleans lodge, November 1854), so the lodge of 1846, gone by 1847, cannot have merged into it. The revival of 1860 could have, but only just. The Sons of Malta had reached Michigan by 1859, when a lodge was organized at Grand Rapids “largely for the cultivation of whatever was comic, droll or ridiculous” (Baxter, 1891). Then, in the first weeks of 1860, the order was exposed in print: the “Council of Ten” in Frank Leslie’s Illustrated Newspaper of 11 February 1860, a “Grand Exposure” in Vanity Fair of 25 February, and Thomas Williams’s pamphlet The Sons of Malta Exposed (New York, 1860). Shepard’s “died from exposure” is a pun on exactly that. A Cassopolis revival of 1860 could have joined the Sons of Malta only in that narrow window. No lodge of the Sons of Malta at Cassopolis or Niles has yet been found, and no other record of a burlesque “Brothers of Charity” has turned up.

    Leslie’s artist spared nothing: hooded brethren, knights in armour, a skeleton, a gallows and, at the centre, the candidate. In later numbers came the initiation itself, and the signs and grips that were supposed to be secret.

    The wider trail: where it fits in the order’s history

    E Clampus Vitus is known today as the historical society of the old West, the “Clampers” whose plaques mark forgotten places across California and beyond. Its own history runs like this, and every date below is the order’s or its historians’, not ours.

    DocumentedThe founding legend. The order traces itself to Ephraim Bee (1802–1888), innkeeper and blacksmith of West Union, in what is now West Virginia. The marker at West Union, put up in 2003, says that by legend Bee received his commission “around 1845.” No document from those years has been produced to date it. Jennifer Wilt of the Doddridge County Heritage Guild, working from the county’s court books and the period press, writes that “shortly after 1850” Bee proclaimed himself Grand Hototote, and that she has found newspaper references to the order “as early as 1853.” An 1896 account in the Parkersburg State Journal has its writer meeting Bee in the fall of 1847, and finding, when they met again “some years afterward,” that Bee “had concocted the celebrated secret order” in the meantime.

    DocumentedThe eastern lodges. The order’s historian Dr. Albert Shumate, in a talk of 25 June 1991, wrote: “There is documentation of lodges in Bedford Pennsylvania, (1847); Metropolis, Illinois (1849); and Bowling Green, Missouri (1849).” He cited nothing, but all three can now be traced to print, and all three were first gathered in an ECV pamphlet, Credo quia absurdum (Placerville, 1949).

    DocumentedMore early records. The Alexandria Gazette of 13 March 1852 reported “the new order introduced recently into Virginia, called the ‘E. Clampus Vitas,’” and claimed to expose it as “dog Latin” for “squeezing the life out of you.” The earliest California notice yet found is a call to “THE MEMBERS of the E. C. V.” in the San Joaquin Republican at Stockton, 14 April 1852. And by 1 February 1867 the Wheeling Intelligencer could call Ephraim Bee “the recognized American head” of the order, with the story of Caleb Cushing already attached. All sixty-one places are in the complete record.

    DocumentedBedford, 1847. Judge William M. Hall’s Reminiscences and Sketches (Harrisburg, 1890) gives a whole chapter to “The Ancient and Venerable Order of Ecclampsis Vitis.” In 1847, he writes, “there came to Bedford, from the west, a traveling agent for a patented invention of a cutting-box,” who “instituted a new secret society.” It met in the grand jury room of the court house, by the light of a single tallow candle, under a “grand worthy patriarch” in a brown-paper hat. The candidate swore a blood-curdling oath, learned a sign “to keep the oats from falling out,” and was finally shown “a large picture of two mules,” “amid the uproarious shouts of laughter of the assembled throng.” There was a torch-light procession led by the Bedford band, and the regalia was “a strip of muslin” worn over the shoulder, “ornamented with a star cut out of the heavy purple colored paper that loaf sugar used to come in.” It lasted “some eight or ten weeks,” until a young doctor took offence and gave the secret away.

    DocumentedMetropolis, 1849. The Huntington Library’s manuscript was first cited in 1941 by G. Ezra Dane, one of the men who revived the order, in Ghost Town: a “Division No. 110” record book “for 1849, loaned to us by Miss Nellie Smith of Stockton.” Credo quia absurdum (1949) adds that the book is from “Metropolis City, Illinois” and that “its roster indicates that several of the members had joined theretofore in Missouri, Alabama, Georgia and other Southern States.” We have not found it in the Huntington’s catalogue; it may be among the Dane family papers there.

    DocumentedBowling Green, 1849. Not a lodge at all. The source, a Calaveras Prospect article of 30 May 1896 reprinted in Credo, says the first ritual brought to California “was printed in the office of the Bowling Green Journal in Pike County, Missouri,” where in 1849 Zumwalt “and one W. C. Wright went into the Journal office and each bought a ritual.”

    ReadingSet side by side, the eastern record and Cassopolis tell one story. The order travelled with “drummers” and young professional men. Its officers in Bedford bore the titles of the Sons of Temperance, the great teetotal brotherhood founded in 1842, worthy patriarch and grand scribe; in Indiana it met in “Tents,” and Metropolis kept a “Division” book. It was, as Hall put it, “a burlesque on all secret societies,” and its punchline everywhere was the same animal: two mules in Bedford, the jack-asses of the Indiana committee report. Michigan’s first Grand Division of the Sons of Temperance was chartered on 29 January 1846, a few weeks before the Cassopolis lodge was instituted.

    DocumentedCalifornia. The order’s own 1966 history, Then and Now, says it “is generally accepted” that Joe Zumwalt brought the ritual to Hangtown (Placerville) in 1849, where a lodge “didn’t last,” and that he “did establish the first lodge of ECV by 1851” at Mokelumne Hill. Shumate dates the Hangtown attempt to 1850. Its best-loved story, the initiation of Steamboat Jake, it dates to 1852; the earliest source, Harry Wells’s History of Siskiyou County (1881), sets it at Yreka in 1856.

    DocumentedThe gap the order’s own history leaves. Then and Now says that travelling salesmen were taken into the order, “with or without authority from Bee,” and that by 1849 “the East and Middle West were dotted with Clamper Lodges.” It names none. Its closing postscript hopes that “some day someone will attempt to research and compile a complete list of the number and location of the lodges formed during this earlier period.”

    ReadingRogers names one, and the Indiana papers of 1846 name more: Connersville, the Tents at Indianapolis, Centreville. Cassopolis, spring 1846, is the earliest lodge of the order yet found with its members’ names written down, and the first known in Michigan. With the Indiana lodges it makes the first lines of the list the order asked for in 1966, and proves that the order was alive in the Middle West three years before the gold rush carried it to California.

    The Clampers today

    The order Carl Wheat revived in 1931 is best known now for its plaques: hundreds of them, in bronze and stone, on forgotten places across California and the West. Some of them are pictured here. If the Brethren ever want a plaque in Michigan, we know a good spot on the square at Cassopolis.

    A Clamper’s glossary

    For readers who have never been a Poor Blind Candidate, and for Clampers who will enjoy checking our work.

    Tent
    What the order called a local lodge in Indiana in 1846: “Tent No. 43” at Indianapolis.
    Clamper
    A member of E Clampus Vitus.
    Poor Blind Candidate
    A man not yet initiated. The order’s 1966 history speaks of “a Poor Blind Candidate” led to his initiation.
    Noble Grand Humbug
    “The presiding officer” of a chapter (Then and Now, 1966).
    Hall of Comparative Ovations
    Wherever the Clampers meet, traditionally the back room of a saloon. At Cassopolis the ball, at least, was held at the Union Hotel.
    Hewgag
    “The great tin horn” of the order, which “sounded a horrendous blast” (Then and Now).
    Credo quia absurdum
    The order’s motto: “I believe because it is absurd.”
    Wearing the tin
    Today’s Clampers pin on tin badges with their red shirts. What the “over two hundred badges” of the Cassopolis ball were made of is not recorded; at Bedford in 1847 the regalia was a muslin sash with a star cut from loaf-sugar paper.

    The spelling

    Both Michigan histories spell the name “Eclampsus,” with an S. The order’s historian writes that Carl Wheat chose the present spelling, “Clampus,” in 1931, when the order was revived in San Francisco. The 2003 marker in Ephraim Bee’s own town still reads “E Clampsus Vitus.” The Indiana papers of 1846 spell it the same way: “Eclampsus Vitus.” The Michigan books are not misspelling the name; they spell it as it was spelled before 1931.

    A timeline

    • 24 Jan 1832 Eber Root licensed to keep a tavern at Cassopolis; he builds the frame house that becomes the Union Hotel.
    • c. 1845 The order’s legend: Ephraim Bee’s commission. (Undated in any surviving record.)
    • Feb 1846 A lodge of the “Eclampsus Vitus” at Connersville, Indiana (Indiana American, 27 Feb).
    • Spring 1846 The Cassopolis lodge is instituted: Bonine, Harter, J. P. Osborn and L. Osborn.
    • 16 Jul 1846 Tent No. 43 of the order meets at Indianapolis “in full regalia” (Indiana State Sentinel).
    • Winter 1846–47 The grand ball at the Union Hotel: “over two hundred badges.”
    • Jan 1847 House bill No. 242, to incorporate “Jackson Lodge” of the order at Centreville, Indiana, is reported on with a joke and tabled.
    • 1847 The lodge collapses. Bedford, Pennsylvania: the earliest dated eastern lodge. Leander Osborn begins reading medicine with Bonine.
    • 1847 (fall) The 1896 Parkersburg account’s first meeting with Bee.
    • 1849 Bonine and Harter go overland to California. Metropolis and Bowling Green lodges. Hangtown attempt (1966 history).
    • 1850 Hangtown attempt (Shumate). “Shortly after 1850”: Bee proclaims himself Grand Hototote (Wilt).
    • 1851 Mokelumne Hill: the first lasting lodge in California. Bonine returns to Michigan.
    • Mar 1852 The order “introduced recently into Virginia” (Alexandria Gazette, 13 Mar).
    • 14 Apr 1852 The earliest California notice yet found: the E. C. V. at Stockton (San Joaquin Republican).
    • 12 Jun 1852 The Masons’ first meeting at Cassopolis, in the Union Hotel.
    • 1853 Earliest newspaper references to Bee’s order in West Virginia (Wilt).
    • 1854 Earliest dated lodge of the Sons of Malta.
    • 1860 The Cassopolis lodge revived as “The Brothers of Charity.” Frank Leslie exposes the Sons of Malta.
    • Feb 1867 Bee initiating candidates at Wheeling, “the recognized American head” of the order.
    • 1868 James M. Shepard comes to Cassopolis.
    • 1875 Rogers prints Shepard’s account.
    • 1882 Mathews prints it again, naming Shepard.
    • 1931 The order revived in San Francisco; Carl Wheat fixes the spelling “Clampus.”
    • 1966 Then and Now asks for a list of the early lodges.

    What we are not saying

    • Not that the order was founded at Cassopolis. The account says a lodge of the order was “instituted” there. Rogers uses the same word on the same page for the Odd Fellows lodge instituted at Cassopolis in February 1847 under a dispensation from a Grand Master: a local lodge of an order that already existed.
    • Not that the evidence is like for like. The Cassopolis account is local memory written down about thirty years later by a man who was not there. Wilt’s dates come from contemporary court books and newspapers. Ours is a claim about the surviving record, not about what may have happened unrecorded.
    • Not that Cassopolis was the first lodge. It was not, so far as the newspapers show: there was a lodge at Connersville, Indiana, by February 1846, and Tents at Indianapolis numbered to at least 43 by July. Our claim is narrower and, we think, still remarkable: Cassopolis is the earliest lodge of the order yet found with its members’ names on record, and the first known in Michigan.
    • Not that the question is closed. Seth Slopes’s history of the order (2000) has been read; Lois Rather’s Men Will Be Boys (1980) has not, the Metropolis book of 1849 has not been found, and the Indiana lodges of 1846 have not yet given up their members’ names. Any of them could change this page.
    • Not that Bonine or Harter carried the order west. The dates line up; no document connects them to any California lodge.

    If anyone can show a lodge of the order dated earlier than the spring of 1846, with a place and names, we will say so on this page, with thanks and with their name.

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. Each one says where the answer is most likely to be. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Who were the Clampers of Indiana in 1846? The order was at Connersville by February 1846, at Indianapolis (Tent No. 43) by July, and at Centreville by that winter; it turns up again at Vincennes in 1854 and in the Springville and Mitchell lodges of Lawrence County in 1859. Every notice found is unsigned. A search of Indiana’s digitized newspapers for 1844–1862 under every spelling, and of the Wayne, Fayette, Marion and Lawrence County histories, names no member. The best chance is the original House bill No. 242, which may list the incorporators of Jackson Lodge: the Indiana State Library says bills before 1975 are at the Indiana State Archives (arc@iara.in.gov). Ask for House bill No. 242 of the 31st General Assembly, 1846–47, and the committee on corporations’ report on it of 6 January 1847. Lois Rather’s Men Will Be Boys (1980) has not yet been read; the Huntington Library holds a copy.
    2. Who told Shepard? He came to Cassopolis in 1868 and wrote in about 1875. Neither Rogers’s preface nor Mathews’s names his informant; Mathews thanks Shepard himself among his Cassopolis helpers, and lists Dr. Leander Osborn, a charter member, among his helpers in Penn township. Two charter members were alive and close by: Jordan P. Osborn, President of the Village in 1875, and Leander Osborn, who lived at Vandalia until 1901. The Michigan Pioneer and Historical Collections (volumes 1–40) say nothing of the lodge. Shepard’s obituary, in the Cassopolis Vigilant of August 1927 (microfilm reel A19, Cass District Library), has not yet been read. His question mark after “charter” suggests he was not sure the lodge ever had one.
    3. Did anyone write it down in 1846? Cassopolis had two papers that year, The Literary News and the Cass County Advocate, and few issues survive. Nothing has been found online: Digital Michigan Newspapers holds no Cass or Berrien County paper, and its Michigan papers of 1846–47, like those in Chronicling America, never mention the order. The best chance is the Niles Republican, which survives for every week of 1846–47 but one (31 January 1846). It is not online anywhere; it is on microfilm at the Cass District Library’s Local History Branch and at the Library of Michigan. The issues from November 1846 to March 1847, the season of the ball, are the place to start.
    4. What did the Bealls’ Centreville know? Cope’s genealogy of 1887 gives Harter’s parents as Joseph Harter and Mary Howell, and the Beall marriages to the day. What it cannot tell us is whether any Beall, Talbot or Pricket belonged to Centreville’s Jackson Lodge. The Wayne County histories of 1872 and 1884 say nothing of the order, and the Centreville and Richmond papers of 1845–47 that are online name no members. The bill at the Indiana State Archives is the likeliest place a name survives.
    5. Did Bonine or Harter meet the order in California? Bonine’s typescript “Journey to California” is listed in Will Bagley’s trail bibliography at the Fort St. Joseph Museum, Niles, with a copy in the Merrill J. Mattes Library of the Oregon-California Trails Association; neither has an online catalogue record, so the next step is to ask them. Neither man appears in the 1850 census index for Sacramento County. Has anyone seen their names in any early Clamper record?
    6. Does any badge survive? Two hundred were worn at one ball. Who made them, and of what, is not recorded. If they were like Bedford’s, they were muslin and paper, and very few would have been kept. No badge or regalia of the 1840s or 1850s has been found in any museum catalogue searched. The oldest object of the order yet traced is a printed ritual of about the late 1850s, inscribed “No. 6 Morristown Lodge,” sold privately at auction (Rumsey Auctions, sale 41, lot 31); where its Morristown was is not yet known.
    7. What was “The Brothers of Charity” of 1860, and was there ever a Sons of Malta lodge at Cassopolis? Nothing outside Rogers and Mathews names the Brothers of Charity. The Michigan State Gazetteer for 1860 lists Sons of Malta lodges at Detroit (Cadillac Lodge No. 77) and Grand Rapids (Brahmin Lodge, instituted March 1859), but none at Cassopolis or Niles; across the state line, Plymouth, Indiana, had a lodge and a hall in 1860. The Cass County Republican of Dowagiac, 1858–64, is free on Chronicling America, but its Sons of Malta items are all exchange news and its scans read poorly; the 1860 issues should be read page by page. The Niles Inquirer for 1858–60, on microfilm at the Cass District Library, is the other place to look.
    8. Where is the Metropolis book, and who is on its roster? G. Ezra Dane borrowed the “Division No. 110” record book of 1849 from Miss Nellie Smith of Stockton and wrote in 1941 that it was “now in” the Huntington Library. It is not in the online catalogue. The finding aid for the Dane family papers there (mssDane) has no Metropolis item, but Box 16 holds “E Clampus Vitus. Constitution, rules, roll of members etc., and clippings,” the papers of an 1880s lodge, and Dane’s own notes for a dissertation on the order. The Huntington’s Reader Services Department can say whether the roll in Box 16 is the Metropolis book or a copy of it. Its roster of members who joined “in Missouri, Alabama, Georgia and other Southern States” could map the order’s road before the gold rush.
