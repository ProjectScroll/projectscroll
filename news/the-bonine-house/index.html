    The Underground Railroad

    The Bonine House at Vandalia

    A Quaker family from Indiana, a farm of 1,600 acres, an elk park that answered to its owner’s call and sent elk to the King of Italy, and a house now kept by the Underground Railroad Society of Cass County. What the record says about the James E. Bonine House, and what it has yet to show.

    Vandalia, Mich. At the corner of M-60 and Penn Road, just west of Vandalia, stands a brick house with a tall mansard tower, and across the highway a small Gothic carriage house. They were built, by the society’s account, by James E. Bonine, a Quaker farmer whose family came to Young’s Prairie from Indiana in the 1840s, and since 2010 they have belonged to the Underground Railroad Society of Cass County, which opens them to visitors and tells the story of the freedom seekers who passed through this country before the Civil War.

    The house is now the best-known Underground Railroad site in the county. We went back to the county histories of 1875, 1882 and 1906, and to the first national list of Underground Railroad workers, to see what the record says about the Bonines, the house and the farm around it.

    How to read this page

    Every statement below is marked Documented (a source says so, and you can read it), Reading (our interpretation of the sources) or Open (not yet known); a New finding is something we found in the record that we could not find in any earlier publication. Every source is listed at the foot of the page.

    At a glance

    The place
    The James E. Bonine House and carriage house, M-60 at Penn Road, Penn Township, Cass County, Michigan
    The family
    Isaac Bonine, a Quaker who “could not tolerate slavery,” came from Wayne County, Indiana, about 1842; his son James E. came a year later
    The marriage
    James E. Bonine married Sarah Ann Bogue, daughter of the abolitionist Stephen Bogue
    The farm in 1882
    1,600 acres, “five houses and eight barns,” an “elegant brick house, with fine fountain,” and an elk and deer park
    The builder
    James E. Bonine, born in Wayne County, Indiana, 5 September 1816; died at his home in Niles, 18 February 1897
    The elk park
    Begun about 1862 with a pair of elk from Iowa; 40 acres behind a ten-foot fence by 1885; “more than a score” of elk in 1941; still kept, 12 to 15 head, in 1953, the year the farm left the family
    The house
    Begun as a Greek Revival farmhouse in the 1840s and remade in the Second Empire style after the Civil War, by the society’s account
    Today
    Owned by the Underground Railroad Society of Cass County since December 2010; open for tours in season
    Why it matters
    It keeps the memory of the Quaker farms of Young’s Prairie, where freedom seekers found shelter and work

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • The Bonines were Quakers of the anti-slavery kind. Isaac Bonine “could not tolerate slavery,” says the 1882 history, and the Anti-Slavery Friends met in a log building in a grove that was, by 1906, “the farm of the late James E. Bonine.”
    • James E. Bonine married into the Bogue family. His wife, Sarah Ann, was the daughter of Stephen Bogue, whose farm the Kentucky raiders struck in 1847.
    • The farm was famous for its elk. The 1882 history says seven of its elk were sold to “King Emmanuel, of Italy, who sent a war vessel for them.” A visitor who stood at the park fence in 1885 was told a smaller story: two elk, “to grace the Royal Park,” and no warship.
    • The elk outlived the family who started them. James E. Bonine’s son, grandson and great-grandson kept the herd; it was still there, 12 to 15 head on 40 acres, in 1953, the year the farm passed out of the family.
    • James E. Bonine died on 18 February 1897, at his home in Niles, after an illness of four months. His obituary ran in the Quaker weekly The American Friend.
    • Wilbur Siebert’s national directory of 1898 names a Bonine: Isaac, the father.

    What it does not show, yet

    • Any record of the 1840s or 1850s naming James E. Bonine, or his house, in the work of the Underground Railroad.
    • A document for the story that freedom seekers sheltered in the carriage house. It is told by the society and by local tradition; we have not found it in a written source.
    • When the house and the carriage house were built. No 19th-century source we have read dates them.
    • An Italian record of the Bonine elk. A report from the king’s own park, printed in 1871, tells of his agent buying elk “in America,” but it names no state and no seller.
    • Where James E. Bonine is buried.

    Story or record? Play along

    Here are ten things people say about the Bonine House. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “James E. Bonine’s wife was the daughter of Stephen Bogue.”

    True

    The 1882 history’s sketch of Stephen Bogue names “Mrs. J. E. Bonine” among the children of his first marriage.

    “The 1898 national list of Underground Railroad workers names James E. Bonine.”

    No

    Wilbur Siebert’s list gives, for Cass County, “Bogue, Steven. Bonine, Isaac. Shugart, Zachariah.” The Bonine it names is Isaac, James E.’s father.

    “The tower was a lookout for slave catchers.”

    No

    The mansard tower came with the remodeling after the Civil War, by the society’s own account. It did not exist in the years of the Underground Railroad.

    “Elk from the Bonine farm were sold to the King of Italy.”

    True, as printed, but how many?

    The 1882 history says seven, carried off by a war vessel. In 1885 a visitor heard “two went to Victor Emanual, the King of Italy, … to grace the Royal Park,” with no warship. No Italian record we have found names the Bonines.

    “The Bonine elk came when they were called.”

    True

    In 1885 James E. Bonine took a pail of salt to the park and called “Come Bill! come Bill!” Nine of the ten elk came and licked the salt thrown through the fence. The old bull, with “five feet of branching horns,” did not.

    “The elk park was gone by 1900.”

    No

    It lasted more than half a century longer. A 1953 newspaper photograph shows a herd of 12 to 15 elk on the 40-acre park, still owned by James E. Bonine’s great-grandson.

    “A tunnel ran from the house to the carriage house.”

    No record

    No tunnel has been found, and the society itself says the story “has yet to be verified.”

    “The 1882 county history tells of freedom seekers hiding in the carriage house.”

    No

    It describes the building opposite the house only as “the finely-built storehouse,” and says nothing of fugitives there.

    “The Anti-Slavery Friends worshipped on the Bonine farm.”

    True

    Glover’s history of 1906: their meeting “was held in a log building in the grove on the farm of the late James E. Bonine in Penn township, and known as the Elk Park.”

    “The Bonine House is on the National Register of Historic Places.”

    No record

    We have found no listing. In 1957 the state registered an “Underground Railroad” historic site at “Bonine Elk Park,” which is a marker site, not a listing of the house.

    Quakers from Indiana

    DocumentedIsaac Bonine, the 1882 history says, was “born of Quaker parents, he accepted their religious faith, and could not tolerate slavery.” He came to Young’s Prairie from Wayne County, Indiana, in 1842 (other sources say 1843), and his son James E. Bonine “came in one year subsequent to his father, and purchased eighty acres of land.”

    New findingOne more record disagrees on the order. The roll of the county’s Pioneer Society, printed by Glover in 1906, gives the year each member came to the county: “James E. Bonine … Indiana … 1841,” and, further down the same page, “Isaac Bonine … Indiana … 1842.” By that roll the son came first. Every modern account we have read says 1843.

    New findingThe family genealogies give the dates the modern accounts leave out. James E. Bonine was born in Wayne County, Indiana, on 5 September 1816, and Sarah Ann Bogue in Preble County, Ohio, on 30 April 1825. They were married at Cassopolis on 8 August 1844. Sarah Ann died at Vandalia on 17 September 1909.

    DocumentedYoung’s Prairie, in Penn and Calvin townships, was already a Quaker settlement. James E. Bonine married Sarah Ann Bogue, a daughter of Stephen Bogue, the Quaker whose farm on the prairie the county history calls one of the two Underground Railroad stations in Cass County.

    An elk park

    DocumentedBy 1882, the county history says, James E. Bonine was “the possessor of 1,600 acres, containing five houses and eight barns. Everything around him betokens the man of thrift and cultivated taste, from the elegant brick house, with fine fountain and attractive surroundings to the farthermost field.”

    Documented“On his farm is a beautiful grove, twelve acres of which are included in an elk and deer park, established some twenty years since by procuring one pair of elk from Iowa, and one pair of deer, also from the West. Since that time he has sold $1,000 worth of elk, seven of which were sold to King Emmanuel, of Italy, who sent a war vessel for them.” There were buffalo, too, “which adds a certain picturesqueness to the scene.”

    New findingThe same page describes, “exactly opposite his residence, which is on a corner,” “the finely-built storehouse,” with “two rows of arbor vitæ” running from it. Standing where it does, opposite the house on the corner, this is almost certainly the building now called the carriage house. It is the earliest description of it we have found, and it calls it a storehouse.

    “Come Bill!” A visit in 1885

    New findingIn August 1885 a writer for the Michigan Farmer, signing himself A. C. G., toured the farms of Young’s Prairie and stopped at “the famous farm of James E. Bonine, covering 1,600 acres.” His account is the earliest eyewitness description of the elk park we have found.

    New findingBetween the house and the village, he wrote, lay “a park of 40 acres enclosed by a picket fence ten feet high on two sides,” with rails and stakes of the same height on the other two. “In this enclosure are ten elk and thirteen deer. This is the only private park in Michigan.”

    New findingThen the owner put on a show. “Mr. Bonine procured a pail of salt and went with us to the park,” and called, “Come Bill! come Bill!” “Nine of the elk came at the call, and timidly licked at the salt which was thrown through the fence.” The elk, the visitor thought, were “natural trotters,” circling “like a shy horse.” Only “the old patriarch, with his five feet of branching horns,” would not come. A hound and a bird dog had tried “an excursion through the park” the year before: “they were bound to have a hunt.”

    New findingThe house matched the park. “Mr. Bonine’s residence is in harmony with his possessions. A large fountain ornaments the front yard, and the walks are large square flagging stones, set in a fine lawn.” By then the 1,600 acres were “divided into four farms and occupied by his children.” The fountain, which the 1882 history mentions in passing, and the flagstone walks appear in no modern account of the house we have read.

    Two elk or seven? The King of Italy

    DocumentedThe 1882 history’s story is grand: seven elk, sold to “King Emmanuel, of Italy, who sent a war vessel for them.” The visitor of 1885, who walked to the park with the owner, tells it more modestly: “He has sold several elks to go to other States for city parks, and two went to Victor Emanual, the King of Italy, to grace the Royal Park.” Two elk, a royal park, and no warship.

    ReadingThe name helps date the sale. Victor Emmanuel II, the first king of united Italy, died in January 1878. If he was the buyer, the elk left Vandalia before then, and probably after the park was founded in the 1860s.

    New findingThe Italian side has its own record, and it explains why a king would buy elk from a Michigan farm. In 1871 Benvenuto Comba, the naturalist of the royal park of La Mandria, near Turin, reported to the Acclimatisation Society of Paris on the king’s wapiti. Two bought at Antwerp and four in London in 1863 died within days. Then, he wrote, “M. le comte de Castiglione fut envoyé par Sa Majesté en Amérique pour y acheter des Cerfs wapiti”: the king sent the Count di Castiglione to America to buy elk. “The first shipment was of 60 animals, of which 47 arrived alive in Italy,” and those died too. In 1865 the count sent more.

    DocumentedThe losses went on. A French hunting writer, Henri de la Blanchère, summing up Comba’s figures in 1876, counted eleven wapiti dead at La Mandria in 1871 and six more in the first months of 1873. Comba blamed the animals’ diet; the royal vets blamed disease.

    ReadingA king whose elk kept dying was a king who kept buying them, all through the years when the Bonine park was selling. Comba names no state and no seller, so his report does not prove the Bonine sale. It makes it believable, and it shows where the proof would be: in the royal household’s accounts for the elk Castiglione and others bought in America.

    DocumentedBy 1908 a syndicated newspaper story was back to seven elk and a “man-of-war.” It added that the Bonine elk sold for $100 a head at any age over six months, that others had gone to parks in Detroit and Grand Rapids, “where some were placed in the parks at the Soldiers’ home,” and that the family had given up its buffalo, which “did not do well here except in summer.”

    ReadingTwo in 1885, written by a visitor who spent the afternoon with the owner; seven in print before and after. The smaller number, set down at the farm, is the one we would trust, until an Italian record says otherwise.

    Friends against slavery

    DocumentedSome Quakers of the time went further than their meetings would go. Glover’s county history of 1906 tells of the “Anti-Slavery” Friends: “They would not use cotton cloths, sugar or anything they knew to be made by slaves.”

    New finding“The meeting in Cass county was held in a log building in the grove on the farm of the late James E. Bonine in Penn township, and known as the Elk Park. The pastor was the Rev. Charles Osborn, a renowned minister among Friends.” A school for their children met in the same building. Modern accounts say the Bonines were members of the Anti-Slavery Friends; we have not found one that says the meeting met on their farm.

    DocumentedCharles Osborn, their pastor, was one of the best-known anti-slavery Quakers in the country. In 1879 James E. Bonine and others built the brick Friends’ church at Vandalia, and he and Sarah A. Bonine were among its first elders.

    Station or story?

    New findingThe national directory of Underground Railroad workers in Wilbur H. Siebert’s The Underground Railroad from Slavery to Freedom (1898), names three for Cass County: “Bogue, Steven. Bonine, Isaac. Shugart, Zachariah.” The Bonine it names is Isaac, the father, not James E.

    DocumentedWe have found no letter, newspaper or court paper of the 1840s or 1850s that places freedom seekers at James E. Bonine’s house or carriage house. The society that owns the house says so itself: “To date there is little evidence of the house itself being a UGRR station.” The carriage house, it says, has a stronger tradition.

    ReadingThat is not the same as saying nothing happened there. The Bonines were anti-slavery Quakers, married into the Bogue family, on a prairie where freedom seekers lived and worked in the open by 1847, and where the Kentucky raiders came looking for them. What is missing is a written record that names this house.

    DocumentedSome of what is told can be checked. The tower that is said to have been a lookout was added after the Civil War, and no tunnel has been found; the society’s own volunteers say both stories are unproven.

    The house in the society’s telling

    DocumentedThe Underground Railroad Society’s story of the house is best told in the books of Cindy Yawkey, the Bonine House’s head docent, who writes and illustrates them herself. The society publishes them and says they inform its story.

    DocumentedHer picture book They Have My Shoes, I Have My Freedom: A Story About the Underground Railroad, which won a Michigan State History Award in 2016, follows Lucinda, a woman escaping from Georgia barefoot after her master took her shoes. She crosses the Ohio, is carried by wagon from Levi Coffin’s Richmond to Bristol, and comes at last to Calvin Township, to “a light in the window of the Bonine house.” There James and Sarah Bonine meet her: “We have food, clothing, shoes, and a place to rest on the third floor of the carriage house,” Sarah says.

    ReadingIt is a story, and it says so: Lucinda is a character, not a name from a record. What it preserves is the society’s tradition that the carriage house loft sheltered freedom seekers. That tradition is worth keeping, and worth testing: we have not yet found it in a written source of the 1840s or 1850s.

    DocumentedHer second book, The Kentucky Slave Raid of 1847, Cass County Michigan (the society, about 2019), retells the raid farm by farm, with a hand-drawn map of the prairie on which “James E. Bonine” sits among the farms of the raid’s story. We compare it with the record in our story The Kentucky Raid of 1847.

    The house today

    DocumentedBy the society’s account, the house was begun as a Greek Revival farmhouse in the 1840s and, “soon after the Civil War,” enlarged and remade “in one of the most flamboyant American-Victorian styles, Second Empire,” with its central mansard tower. The Gothic Revival carriage house across the road is dated to about 1850.

    DocumentedIn 1957 the state of Michigan registered an “Underground Railroad” historic site at “Bonine Elk Park, M-60.” The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County bought the house and carriage house in December 2010, and has been restoring them since.

    A man of affairs

    DocumentedJames E. Bonine was more than a farmer. In the 1850s he took premiums at the state fair for Durham heifers, a French and Spanish buck and the best boar; the federal agriculture reports of the 1860s list his Merino sheep; and in 1883 the Michigan Farmer wrote of “a flock of one thousand head belonging to J. E. Bonine of Cass County.”

    New findingIn 1870 he was one of the first directors of the First National Bank of Cassopolis. He was superintendent of the Air Line railroad through Penn Township, to which, the 1882 history says, he gave “three years time, $6,000 right of way, and $6,000 cash.” That was the Michigan Air Line, built from Jackson to Niles and opened through Vandalia in 1871, which gave the village its station. No modern account of the house we have read mentions his part in it.

    DocumentedIn 1879 he and his brother Lot built the brick Friends’ church at Vandalia. By the society’s account it later became the Masonic lodge, and it has since been torn down. In 1887 the Sharpless family genealogy still called him “a banker and farmer, Vandalia”; by 1906 Glover writes of “the late James E. Bonine.” After his death his widow, by the 1908 account, was the largest stockholder of the Cassopolis bank.

    New findingJames E. Bonine died on 18 February 1897. The Quaker weekly The American Friend reported it that April: “Died at his home in Niles, Michigan, Second month 18th, 1897, James E. Bonine, in the eighty-first year of his age.” He had been “for many years an elder,” it said, and had given so freely to the church that he built “principally one house for worship,” very likely the Vandalia church of 1879, and helped to build three others. His last illness lasted four months.

    DocumentedA Berrien County history of 1906 gives a fuller sketch, and a different year. It calls him “the leading banker of Cass county for many years,” says he cleared 800 of his 1,600 acres, and says he moved to Niles in 1866, went back to the farm, and spent his last days in Niles, “where he died in 1898 at the age of eighty-two years.” The obituary, printed weeks after his death, is the one to trust.

    OpenWe have not found where he is buried. His parents lie in Prairie Grove Cemetery in Penn Township, and his grandson in Silverbrook at Niles; either is possible.

    The Elk Park’s later life

    DocumentedThe elk did not end with James E. Bonine. On 14 October 1907 his son Isaac A. Bonine wrote to the federal Biological Survey that he had been breeding elk and deer “for about thirty-five years.” The government printed his letter in a 1910 bulletin on raising game animals.

    Isaac A. Bonine to the Biological Survey · 14 October 1907

    “We find that deer are not so hardy as elk and require more care. Elk winter well on hay and corn fodder with a little grain, and they live and thrive during summer months on blue grass. … The growing of both elk and deer for park purposes may be made profitable.”

    OpenHis “thirty-five years” would start the herd about 1872. The 1882 history’s “some twenty years since” and a 1953 caption both point to about 1862. We cannot yet say which is right.

    New findingIsaac’s son, Dr. J. Gordon Bonine, kept the herd in his turn. When he died in 1934 his obituary called the farm of 1,500 acres “the famous Elk park farm,” where “he kept [a] herd of elk that drew many tourists at all times of the year,” and “a show place in Cass county.” In October 1953 a newspaper photograph showed “a herd of 12 to 15 elk at Bonine Elk Park” on M-60, “an outstanding tourist attraction,” on a forested 40-acre park owned by G. Elwood Bonine, the founder’s great-grandson.

    DocumentedBy 1941 the park was a sight on the highway, and the WPA’s guide to Michigan sent motorists to it.

    Michigan: A Guide to the Wolverine State · 1941

    “On the western border of Vandalia … is Bonine Elk Park (R), a woodland tract of 60 acres. Here more than a score of American elk browse in the open, except during the most severe weather. They are sold to zoos, parks, and private gardens, and sometimes to Elk lodges for ceremonial purposes.”

    New findingThe end came with the farm. When G. Elwood Bonine died in 1976 his obituary said he had “farmed the Elk Park Farms which had been in his family from 1837 to 1953.” He moved to Lansing in 1953, the year of the last photograph of the herd we have found.

    ReadingThat is ninety years of elk on one farm. The state’s 1957 marker went up at “Bonine Elk Park,” but by then the farm had left the family, and by 1963 a writer spoke of the park in the past tense. The last elk most likely went with the farm, about 1953.

    Who’s who

    Isaac Bonine
    The father. A Quaker from Wayne County, Indiana, who “could not tolerate slavery.” Named by Siebert in 1898 as an Underground Railroad worker.
    James E. Bonine (1816–1897)
    The son. Farmer, stock breeder, banker, railroad superintendent and founder of the Elk Park; the builder of the house, by the society’s account.
    Sarah Ann Bogue Bonine (1825–1909)
    His wife, daughter of Stephen Bogue. An elder of the Vandalia Friends’ church in 1879.
    Stephen Bogue
    Her father, whose farm the county history calls one of the two Underground Railroad stations in Cass County, and which the Kentucky raiders struck in 1847.
    Charles Osborn
    Pastor of the Anti-Slavery Friends, who met in the log building in the Bonine grove.
    The children
    Evan E. (1845–1865); Mary E. (born 1847, Mrs. W. T. Dougan); Isaac A. (born 1849), who kept the elk after his father; Elsie E. (born 1851, Mrs. N. B. Hannum).
    The keepers of the elk
    Isaac A. Bonine; his son Dr. J. Gordon Bonine (1874–1934); and G. Elwood Bonine (1896–1976), state senator for Cass County from 1942 to 1952, who farmed the Elk Park until 1953.

    The farm through the years

    • 1816 James E. Bonine born in Wayne County, Indiana, 5 September (Sharpless 1887)
    • 1841–43 Isaac Bonine and his son come to Young’s Prairie; the sources disagree on the year and on who came first
    • 1844 James E. Bonine marries Sarah Ann Bogue at Cassopolis, 8 August
    • 1847 The Kentucky raiders come to Young’s Prairie
    • 1850s State-fair premiums for his cattle, sheep and hogs
    • c. 1862 The elk park begun with a pair of elk from Iowa (1882 history; a 1953 caption). Isaac A. Bonine’s letter points to about 1872
    • 1863–65 The King of Italy sends the Count di Castiglione to America to buy elk; most of the first herds die
    • 1866 The Bonines move to Niles for some years, then go back to the farm
    • 1863–78 Elk go to Victor Emmanuel II, King of Italy: two by the 1885 account, seven by the 1882 history
    • 1870 A first director of the First National Bank of Cassopolis
    • 1871 The Michigan Air Line opens through Vandalia, with Bonine its superintendent in Penn Township
    • 1879 The brick Friends’ church at Vandalia is dedicated
    • 1882 1,600 acres, five houses, eight barns, an elk and deer park and buffalo (county history)
    • 1885 A visitor finds ten elk and thirteen deer in a 40-acre park, and a fountain on the lawn
    • 1897 James E. Bonine dies at his home in Niles, 18 February
    • 1906 Glover writes of “the late James E. Bonine”
    • 1907 Isaac A. Bonine writes to the Biological Survey on raising elk
    • 1908 Elk selling at $100 a head; the buffalo given up
    • 1909 Sarah Ann Bonine dies at Vandalia, 17 September
    • 1934 The Elk Park farm called “a show place in Cass county”
    • 1941 The WPA guide: “more than a score of American elk browse in the open”
    • 1953 12 to 15 elk still on the park; the farm leaves the family
    • 1957 State historic site registered at “Bonine Elk Park, M-60”
    • 2010 The Underground Railroad Society of Cass County buys the house and carriage house

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. Is there a written record of freedom seekers at the Bonine farm? Isaac Bonine’s account book of 1838–1850, at Earlham College, may hold clues.
    2. When were the house and the carriage house built? A deed, a tax roll or a dated letter would settle it.
    3. Two elk or seven, and when? The royal household’s accounts for the elk bought in America, or an American newspaper of the 1860s or 1870s, would settle it.
    4. Where is James E. Bonine buried? Prairie Grove in Penn Township or Silverbrook at Niles.
    5. When did the Elk Park begin, and when did the last elk leave? About 1862 or about 1872; and probably about 1953, when the farm was sold.
