    How the towns got their names

    How Cassopolis got its name

    Everyone knows half of it: Cassopolis is the city of Cass. The other half is stranger. The county seat was first somewhere else, the man who won it for Stone Lake is said to have walked through a night of cold rain, and the village was spelled with an A for nearly forty years.

    By Michael Urbanski · Project Scroll · 22 October 2026 · 10 min read

    Cassopolis, Mich. Ask how Cassopolis got its name and the answer comes quickly: it is the city of Cass, named for Lewis Cass, governor of Michigan Territory, with the Greek word for a city on the end. That much is true. But the county seat was first at another village, the man who won it for Stone Lake is said to have walked through a night of cold rain to do it, and for nearly forty years the post office spelled the name with an A.

    We went back to the county’s three histories, of 1875, 1882 and 1906, to Michigan’s first gazetteer of 1838, and to the hand-coloured county map of 1860, to see what the record shows and what is only a good story.

    How to read this page

    Every statement below is marked Documented (a source says so, and you can read it), Reading (our interpretation of the sources) or Open (not yet known). Every source is listed at the foot of the page.

    At a glance

    The place
    Cassopolis, Michigan: the seat of Cass County, on the east shore of Stone Lake
    The county
    Named Cass by the act of the Legislative Council approved 29 October 1829
    First county seat
    Not Cassopolis: Geneva, on Diamond Lake, chosen in 1830
    The plat
    1831: “a village to be called Cassapolis,” laid out by its proprietors on Stone Lake
    County seat
    By Acting Governor Mason’s proclamation, 19 December 1831
    The spelling
    “Cassapolis” on the plat and at the post office until 1870; “Cassopolis” in print by 1838
    Why it matters
    The name everyone can explain has a vowel nobody can; and the town got its name by winning the county seat

    The case in brief

    What the record shows

    • The county was named Cass in 1829. The act says only “the name thereof shall be Cass.” That it honours Lewis Cass is understood, not written.
    • The county seat came first to Geneva, on Diamond Lake, in 1830. A new commission moved it in 1831.
    • The village was platted in 1831 as “Cassapolis,” with an A, and proclaimed the county seat on 19 December 1831.
    • Three streets on the plat carry the commissioners’ names: Disbrow, Rowland and O’Keefe.
    • The O came slowly. “Cassopolis” is in print by 1838, but the post office kept “Cassapolis” until 1870, and the county map of 1860 still letters the village “Cassapolis.”

    What it does not show, yet

    • The plat and the governor’s proclamation themselves. We have read them only as the county histories report them.
    • Who chose the name, and why “-polis.”
    • Why the A became an O. The 1906 history thought it was for the sound.

    Story or record? Play along

    Here are eight things people say about the name. For each, make your call: true, false, or no record either way. Then see what the paper shows.

    “Cassopolis was Cass County’s first county seat.”

    No

    Rogers’s history of 1875 records that the seat was first located at Geneva, on Diamond Lake, in 1830. It moved to the new plat on Stone Lake by the governor’s proclamation of 19 December 1831.

    “The village was first spelled Cassapolis.”

    True

    Rogers says the plat was “to be called Cassapolis,” and Glover’s history of 1906 says the original spelling, “as found on old letters and the first plat, was ‘Cassapolis.’”

    “The law that created the county says it was named in honour of Lewis Cass.”

    No

    The act of 1829, as the 1882 history prints it, says only “the name thereof shall be Cass.” The honour is understood, not stated.

    “Lewis Cass himself chose the name of the village.”

    No record

    The histories credit the local proprietors with the plat and its name. Nothing we have read connects Cass himself to the choice.

    “Three of the town’s streets were named after the men who picked the county seat.”

    True

    The commissioners were Thomas Rowland, Henry Disbrow and George A. O’Keefe, and Disbrow, Rowland and O’Keefe streets are on the original plat, and on the county map of 1860.

    “The change from Cassapolis to Cassopolis was made official by the post office.”

    True

    Glover says the change was “finally affirmed by the postoffice department.” Postal listings give Cassapolis from 1832 and Cassopolis from 1870.

    “As late as 1860, the county map still spelled it Cassapolis.”

    True

    The plan of the village on Geil, Harley & Siverd’s map of 1860 is headed “CASSAPOLIS. County Seat of Cass Co.”

    “The county seat was won in a race through the night.”

    A good story

    Rogers tells it in 1875, forty-four years after the event: E. B. Sherman heard of rivals’ plans and set out on foot “through the cold rain and dismal darkness in this race for a County seat.” No record of 1831 tells it.

    The county named Cass

    DocumentedCass County was set off by an act of the Legislative Council of Michigan Territory approved on 29 October 1829. The act draws the county’s lines and ends that the land “be, and the same is hereby set off into a separate county and the name thereof shall be Cass.”

    ReadingThe act names no one. That the county honours Lewis Cass, governor of the territory from 1813 to 1831, is the reading everyone has always given, and it is surely right; but it is understood, not written. Henry Gannett’s federal Origin of Certain Place Names (1905) lists the county and the village among the names given “for Gen. Lewis Cass, governor of Michigan in 1820.”

    The county seat that came first

    DocumentedCassopolis was not the first county seat. Howard S. Rogers, in the county’s first history (1875), writes that “sometime in 1830 the County seat of Cass County was located at Geneva, on the bank of Diamond Lake,” by commissioners appointed by the governor. Geneva had been named, Rogers says, after the village of that name in New York.

    DocumentedRogers also passes on a story about how Geneva lost: “it is said that some of the Commissioners ... withheld information of their decision one day, and in the mean-time sent an agent to the Land Office.”

    Reading“It is said” is Rogers’s own warning. The story may be true, but it is a rumour he heard, not a record he read.

    DocumentedOn 4 March 1831, Rogers reports, the Legislative Council passed an act for a new commission to relocate the seats of justice of Cass, St. Joseph and Branch counties. The commissioners for Cass, Glover’s history of 1906 says, were Thomas Rowland, Henry Disbrow and George A. O’Keefe, and they were to meet in the county “on the third Monday in May, 1831.”

    A race through the rain

    DocumentedRogers tells how the new site was won. E. B. Sherman, one of the men who would own it, learned of other men’s plans for the land on Stone Lake, “kept his own counsel,” took his leave, and “started on foot for Edwardsburg”; and on he went, “through the cold rain and dismal darkness in this race for a County seat,” to enter the land first.

    ReadingIt is a fine story, and it may be true in its outline: whoever owned the land the commissioners chose stood to gain a town. But Rogers wrote it down forty-four years later, from what old settlers told him, and we have found no record of 1831 that tells it.

    A village to be called Cassapolis

    DocumentedRogers again: the proprietors “prepared a plat of a village to be called Cassapolis, containing one hundred and sixty acres of land, and each of the four proprietors signed an agreement to donate to the County one-half of his portion of the same, providing the County seat should be located thereon.” Glover, in 1906, counts not four but five proprietors whose names are signed to the plat: E. B. Sherman, A. H. Redfield, Abram Tietsort, Oliver Johnson and Ephraim McCleary.

    DocumentedThe commissioners chose the site, and Acting Governor Stevens T. Mason’s proclamation fixing the seat of justice there was, the 1882 history says, “issued December 19, 1831.”

    DocumentedThe proprietors had made sure of the commissioners’ goodwill. Glover writes that they “announced with effective ostentation the naming of three principal streets after the commissioners,” and he lists Disbrow, Rowland and O’Keefe streets on the plat. Copies of the original plat, he adds, “are still extant.”

    ReadingCass for the governor and the county; polis, Greek for a city, for the town. It was a fashionable ending in the 1820s and 1830s; Indianapolis had been named in 1821. Nobody recorded who chose it.

    The A and the O

    DocumentedThe first post office, the 1882 history says, had Alexander H. Redfield, one of the proprietors, as its postmaster. Postal-history listings give the office as “Cassapolis” from 1832 to 1870 and “Cassopolis” from 1870.

    DocumentedThe O was in print long before the post office took it. John T. Blois’s Gazetteer of the State of Michigan (1838) has “Cassopolis, village, post office, and seat of justice” for Cass County.

    DocumentedEven the town’s own historian used both. Rogers’s book of 1875 was printed at “Cassopolis, Mich.,” as its title page says, but his chapter on the village calls it “Cassapolis” throughout.

    DocumentedGlover, in 1906, gives the only explanation we have: “the change from a to o, which was clearly dictated by euphony, took place gradually in custom and was finally affirmed by the postoffice department.”

    Reading“Euphony” is Glover’s guess. An O also matches the other -opolis towns, from Annapolis to Indianapolis, and that may have pulled the spelling as much as the sound did.

    Stone Lake

    DocumentedThe village grew on the east shore of Stone Lake. The 1882 history says the lake was “so named from the fact that its shores were originally very thickly strewn with stone.” A settler named Abram Tietsort had built a log cabin on the east bank in 1829 or 1830; the histories disagree by a year.

    OpenWho named the lake, and when, we have not found.

    The name on paper

    Play the spellings in order, from the county of 1829 to the town today.

    • 1829 Cass (the county, by act of 29 October)
    • 1831 Cassapolis (the plat)
    • 1832 Cassapolis (the post office)
    • 1838 Cassopolis (Blois’s gazetteer)
    • 1860 Cassapolis (the county map)
    • 1870 Cassopolis (the post office)
    • 1875 Cassopolis (Rogers’s title page, with Cassapolis inside)

    Open questions

    These are the questions we cannot yet answer. If you can help with any of them, please write to us.

    1. What exactly does the plat say? The original is recorded with Cass County; the histories quote it only in part.
    2. Who chose the name? Nothing yet found says which proprietor, or why -polis.
    3. Where is the earliest “Cassopolis”? We have it in 1838. Letters or newspapers of 1832–37 may have it sooner.
    4. Was the race in the rain real? A land-office entry for the plat’s quarter-section, with its date, would test Rogers’s story.
    5. Who named Stone Lake?
