    One Letter, Two Certificates

    One letter. That’s all it was. A single middle initial, and it nearly turned one man into two.

    The case

    A pioneer died in Michigan in 1912. The old county histories give him a plain first and last name. One of them adds “Senior.” County history

    His own death certificate, filed about a month after he died, gives the same plain name. No initial at all. Document

    Then we found his son’s death certificate, from 1935. In the box for the father’s name, whoever answered the questions gave him a middle initial: the same one the son carried. Document

    So which is right? Make your guess now. Ours is further down.

    Then it got stranger

    Once we started comparing every box, the records kept arguing.

    • His birthday: his death certificate and one of the county histories are two days apart.
    • His father’s birthplace: the certificate names one state. That same history names another.

    One man, one family and three disagreements. Nobody lied. Each one is somebody’s memory.

    What this teaches

    Every record was written by someone. A death certificate tells you what the informant believed, on a hard day. Their name is usually on it. The father’s was filed about a month after he died; the son’s was filled in twenty-three years later, by someone remembering a man long gone. County histories have the same weakness: the family usually supplied the facts, often from memory, and nobody checked them.

    One letter can change everything. Fathers and sons with the same name get tangled in the record all the time. One wrong initial can merge two men or split one, and send you chasing somebody else’s family for a month.

    Watch out for “Senior.” In the 1800s it didn’t always mean “father.” Sometimes it only meant the older of two men with the same name in town, related or not. Here a son with the same name does turn up, so the label fits, but always check.

    Disagreement is a clue, not a failure. Every mismatch tells you who was remembering, and when. Our guess, as promised: father and son shared a name, and in 1935 someone gave the father the son’s initial too Reading. But a guess is not proof.

    Four rules for when records disagree

    1. Ask who gave the information, and when. Closer to the event usually weighs more, but not always.
    2. Keep looking. A third, independent record can tip the balance, but records are weighed, not counted.
    3. Write down both versions and where each came from. Never quietly “fix” one.
    4. Call it settled only when the evidence settles it. Until then, it’s an open question.

    Where the case stands

    Open Our next witness: a local newspaper notice from the days or weeks after his death. Then his probate file, and the cemetery’s burial records or a transcription of his stone. Read the stone on paper. Don’t go to it.

    Where to look, for free

    • Archives of Michigan, death certificates 1897–1952. Free; for most years you can open the certificate itself, in the original handwriting.
    • FamilySearch. Birth, marriage and death records from all over the world, free with an account.
    • Michiganology: vital records. The State of Michigan’s own index to its births, marriages and deaths.
    • Michigan County Histories and Atlases. The University of Michigan’s free scans of the county histories, Rogers, Mathews and Glover among them.
    • Find a Grave. Free transcriptions and photographs of gravestones. Read the stone on paper.

    Tip: read every box, including the small print at the bottom. Ages are often given to the day, in years, months and days. Count back from the date of death and you have a birth date to check against everything else. Just remember the informant gave that age too.
