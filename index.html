      What we research

      • A lost ancestorCensus rolls, death certificates, deeds, wills, church books and old newspapers, compared line by line. When the records disagree, we work out who was remembering, and when.
      • Who said it firstA family story, a famous quote or a local legend, traced back to the first time it was printed. Who wrote it down? Were they there? Who told them? What changed each time it was retold?

      Part VI

      How we mark the evidence

      Not every source is worth the same. A death certificate filed the week a man died is not a story his grandson told sixty years later, and neither of them is our own opinion. So in every research report, every statement carries one of nine marks, strongest first. You can see at a glance what the record says, and what we are arguing from it.

      What the record says

      Document
      Made at the time, by someone whose job was to write it down: a death certificate, a deed, a census page, a survey, a map. Example: a 1912 Michigan death certificate.
      Period print
      Printed at or near the time: a newspaper notice, an obituary, a book of the day. Example: a doctor’s obituary printed the year he died.
      County history
      The big county histories of the 1870s to the 1910s. Rich and indispensable, but often written decades later from what families remembered, and rarely checked. Example: the 1882 History of Cass County.
      Compiled
      A later hand gathering earlier records: an index, a bibliography, a transcription, a modern summary. Good for finding things; we go back to the original whenever it survives. Example: a trail-diary bibliography that lists an unpublished typescript.
      Tradition
      What a family or a community says, passed down by word of mouth. Worth recording exactly as told, and worth testing. Example: “Grandpa always said he came over on the last boat before the war.”
      Field
      What we saw or measured ourselves, at first hand, dated, from public ground or with permission. Example: a dated photograph of a marker on a public roadside.

      What we argue from it

      Inference
      A step that follows directly from the records. Example: one certificate was filed a month after he died, the other twenty-three years later, so the first informant was closer to the event.
      Reading
      Our interpretation of what the records point to. Always labeled, always open to correction. Example: “In 1935, someone gave the father his son’s initial.”
      Hypothesis
      An idea worth testing that the records don’t yet support. We say what would prove or disprove it. Example: he never used a middle initial at all; his obituary and gravestone would tell.

      Marks, strongest first: Document Period print County history Compiled Tradition Field (seen at first hand) Inference Reading Hypothesis — the last three marks are our own argument, offered for correction

      How it looks in a report

      A pioneer died in Michigan in 1912. His death certificate gives no middle initial Document. A county history adds “Senior” to his name County history. His son’s death certificate, filled in twenty-three years later, gives the father a middle initial: the son’s own Document. The first informant was closer to the event Inference. Father and son shared a name, and in 1935 someone gave the father the son’s initial too Reading. Or he never used a middle initial at all, which his obituary would show Hypothesis.

      Part VII

      From the field notes

      Lesson No. 1 from the desk: two old records, two different stories. Here is what to do.

      1. Ask who gave the information, and when. Closer to the event usually weighs more, but not always.
      2. Keep looking. A third, independent record can tip the balance, but records are weighed, not counted.
      3. Write down both versions and where each came from. Never quietly “fix” one.
      4. Call it settled only when the evidence settles it. Until then, it’s an open question.

      Person of the month · October

      James M. Shepard, the dentist who wrote it down

      He was three years old in 1846, when the joke lodge was founded. He came to Cassopolis in 1868 and worked there as a dentist. In September 1870 the village’s reading room was organized in his dental office, and a year later he was one of the incorporators of its library association.

      When Howard Rogers wrote the county’s first history in 1875, it was Shepard who wrote down the story of the lodge: the halters, the blindfolds, the ball and the two hundred badges. Seven years later, Alfred Mathews reprinted it and named him in a footnote.

      On the record: in the same winter of 1846–47 that the joke lodge held its ball, the Odd Fellows’ Cass County Lodge No. 21 received its dispensation; Rogers called it “the first legitimate secret society.”

      The open question. He was three in 1846. Who told him?

      Sources: Howard S. Rogers, History of Cass County, from 1825 to 1875 (1875), pp. 253–254 and 258; A. Mathews, History of Cass County, Michigan (1882), p. 174.

      Yes. Every case page has a “Start here” box with the first pages to open, many of them free online. The glossary explains the old words, the guides show you how to read a death certificate and old handwriting, and the hunter’s worksheet keeps your notes straight. All you need is curiosity and, now and then, a library card.

      Can you help with my own family’s mystery?

      Yes. Write to research@projectscroll.com, or send Project Scroll a message on Facebook, with what you know. We’ll tell you honestly what the records are likely to show before you commit to anything.

      Can you write up my whole family’s history?

      Yes. We make family registers (every descendant of one couple), ancestral monographs (every ancestor of one person) and full family histories, built from the records with every statement marked. See a specimen, or download the sample family register.

      Do you also offer imaging, design, photography, 3D or video?

      Yes. Alongside the research, we scan and restore old photographs and documents, bring up faded ink, design family-history books and charts, photograph heirlooms and places, model landscapes in 3D from public LiDAR surveys, make 3D animations and family-history films, and keep living documents that grow with the research. See the studio work, then send us a message with what you have and what you would like made.

      What should I keep notes on?

      Use the hunter’s worksheet: one page to log each record, who made it, when, and where you found it. Print as many as you need.

      Is anything hidden on this website?

      Perhaps. There is a line of code at the foot of every page, a gold diamond that answers if asked, twenty-three tin badges to earn, and every page has a back side. Curious hunters know where to look.

      Who runs Project Scroll?

      An independent researcher and author who writes narrative nonfiction about the history of the Great Lakes country, trained in visual communications. The research behind those books is the same research we offer you, and the imaging and design studio grew from that training. More about Project Scroll.

      How is the hunt paid for?

      By sponsors: friends who give a few dollars a month, patrons who fund a case, local businesses with a card in the sponsors’ strip, and organizations that underwrite a hall. Sponsorship funds the work and never shapes a finding. How it works, and the ledger of what every gift paid for.

      How do I get in touch?

      Email research@projectscroll.com, or message the page on Facebook. Both reach the same desk. The form below lays out what to include. Expect a first reply within a few days; a full read of your records takes longer, and we say how long before we start.

      How long does a research case take?

      It depends on the question and on what survives. Some mysteries open in an afternoon; some take a season. After the first read we tell you what we expect to find, and roughly how long it should take, before you commit to anything.

      Does this website track me?

      Only if you say yes. On your first visit the site asks whether it may count your visit with a small Meta script; until you choose Allow, nothing is loaded and nothing is counted, and you can change your answer on the Privacy page. The things the site remembers, such as your followed cases and your notes, stay in your own browser either way. What this site keeps.

      Is Project Scroll only about Michigan?

      It started in one Michigan county, but the methods work anywhere, and so does our research. Mysteries are welcome from wherever you are. The St. Joseph River does not stop at the state line, and neither did the families: South Bend, Mishawaka and Elkhart mysteries are as welcome as Cassopolis ones.
