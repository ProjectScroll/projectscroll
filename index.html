The rules

  • Read the record. Every answer is in a book, on a map, or on a page.
  • Show your work. A name without the page it came from is only a guess.
  • Stay on paper. Never dig, never go near a grave, and never set foot on anyone’s land.
  • Be kind. These are real people, and their families are still here.

Real cases, every one begun as a single question. Some are answered, some are still open, and we always say which. Now we’re taking on yours.

What we research

  • A lost ancestorCensus rolls, death certificates, deeds, wills, church books and old newspapers, compared line by line. When the records disagree, we work out who was remembering, and when.
  • Who said it firstA family story, a famous quote or a local legend, traced back to the first time it was printed. Who wrote it down? Were they there? Who told them? What changed each time it was retold?
  • A forgotten placeA vanished mill, a schoolhouse, a cabin or a whole village, found again on old plat maps, surveyors’ field notes and county atlases.
  • A legend worth testingGiant bones, underground rivers, buried treasure. We read the story against the records and tell you which parts hold up and which don’t.
  • An heirloom no one can explainAn old photograph, a lodge badge, a deed, a letter or a piece of jewelry, traced through the people who owned it.
  • Lodges, churches and old societiesRosters, proceedings and minute books, where they survive and their keepers allow access, to learn who belonged, when, and what they did.
  • A second pair of eyesA family history, a book chapter or an article checked against its sources before it goes out into the world.

What happens when you bring us one

  1. Tell us what you know. A name, a place, rough dates, and whatever the family says. Our message form makes it easy.
  2. Get an honest first read. We tell you what the records are likely to show, and what they probably can’t, before you commit to anything.
  3. Agree on the question. One clear question, and where we will look to answer it.
  4. We search the record. Certificates, deeds, censuses, old newspapers, county histories and maps, compared line by line.
  5. You get the report. Plain English, every statement marked, every source listed, and what is still open.

Part IV

How we work

Five promises we keep on every case, whether it takes an afternoon or a season.

  1. Every claim comes with its source. You’ll know exactly where each fact came from, so you can check it yourself.
  2. Disagreements are clues, not failures. When two records argue, we show you both and explain why.
  3. Open questions stay open. If something might be true but can’t be proven yet, we say so, and we keep looking instead of throwing it away.
  4. No guarantees, just the record. Some questions have answers on paper and some don’t. We’ll tell you up front which kind yours looks like.
  5. We research the past. No locating living people, no legal or title opinions, no appraisals. No digging, no trespassing, and care with every family’s story.

What you get

A written report in plain English: what we found, where we found it, what is still open, and where to look next. Where the old books and maps are free online, we include the links so you can turn the pages yourself. See a sample report.

Part V

How we mark the evidence

Not every source is worth the same. A death certificate filed the week a man died is not a story his grandson told sixty years later, and neither of them is our own opinion. So in our longer research reports, every statement carries one of nine marks, strongest first. You can see at a glance what the record says, and what we are arguing from it.

What the record says

Document
Made at the time, by someone whose job was to write it down: a death certificate, a deed, a census page, a survey, a map. Example: a 1912 Michigan death certificate.
Period print
Printed at or near the time: a newspaper notice, an obituary, a book of the day. Example: a doctor’s obituary printed the year he died.
County history
The big county histories of the 1870s to the 1910s. Rich and indispensable, but often written decades later from what families remembered, and rarely checked. Example: the 1882 History of Cass County.
Compiled
A later hand gathering earlier records: an index, a bibliography, a transcription, a modern summary. Good for finding things; we go back to the original whenever it survives. Example: a trail-diary bibliography that lists an unpublished typescript.
Tradition
What a family or a community says, passed down by word of mouth. Worth recording exactly as told, and worth testing. Example: “Grandpa always said he came over on the last boat before the war.”
Field
What we saw or measured ourselves, at first hand, dated, from public ground or with permission. Example: a dated photograph of a marker on a public roadside.

What we argue from it

Inference
A step that follows directly from the records. Example: one certificate was filed a month after he died, the other twenty-three years later, so the first informant was closer to the event.
Reading
Our interpretation of what the records point to. Always labeled, always open to correction. Example: “In 1935, someone gave the father his son’s initial.”
Hypothesis
An idea worth testing that the records don’t yet support. We say what would prove or disprove it. Example: he never used a middle initial at all; his obituary and gravestone would tell.

How it looks in a report

A pioneer died in Michigan in 1912. His death certificate gives no middle initial Document. A county history adds “Senior” to his name County history. His son’s death certificate, filled in twenty-three years later, gives the father a middle initial: the son’s own Document. The first informant was closer to the event Inference. Father and son shared a name, and in 1935 someone gave the father the son’s initial too Reading. Or he never used a middle initial at all, which his obituary would show Hypothesis.

Try marking these yourself

Pick the mark you would give each one.

  1. A notice in the local paper, the week after he died.

    Period print. It was printed at the time, but by a newspaper, not by an office whose job was to record the death.

  2. “Great-aunt Ruth always said the family came from Ohio.”

    Tradition. Write it down exactly as she said it, then go and test it.

  3. A website listing every burial in a cemetery, typed up by volunteers.

    Compiled. A later hand gathered it. Wonderful for finding a name; then find the original burial record or the stone itself, on paper.

  4. A biography of a doctor in a county history of the 1890s, quoting his obituary.

    County history. And notice the obituary inside it: if that newspaper survives, it is a period print, and stronger.

  5. “The first certificate was filed a month after he died, so its informant was closer to the event.”

    Inference. It follows directly from the dates on the records themselves.

Part VII

From the field notes

The first lesson from the desk: what to do when two old records tell you two different things.

  1. Ask who gave the information, and when. Closer to the event usually weighs more, but not always.
  2. Keep looking. A third, independent record can tip the balance, but records are weighed, not counted.
  3. Write down both versions and where each came from. Never quietly “fix” one.
  4. Call it settled only when the evidence settles it. Until then, it’s an open question.

Part VIII

Questions

Does the hunt cost anything?

No. The hunt is free to follow and free to play. All you need is curiosity and, now and then, a library card.

Do I have to go anywhere?

No. Every clue is solved on paper: in books, maps and records, many of them free online. The hunt never sends anyone onto private land or near a grave.

I solved one. Now what?

Post your answer in the comments on Facebook, and say where you found it. Solvers are named in the next post.

Can you help with my own family’s mystery?

Yes. Send Project Scroll a message on Facebook with what you know. We’ll tell you honestly what the records are likely to show before you commit to anything.

What should I keep notes on?

Use the hunter’s worksheet: one page to log each record, who made it, when, and where you found it. Print as many as you need.

Is anything hidden on this website?

Perhaps. Every page has a back side, and curious hunters know where to look.

Is Project Scroll only about Michigan?

It started in one Michigan county, but the methods work anywhere, and so does our research. Mysteries are welcome from wherever you are.
