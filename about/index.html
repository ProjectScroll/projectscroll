    About Project Scroll

    Project Scroll is independent historical research: a history hunt anyone can play, a research desk anyone can hire, and a studio for digital imaging, design, photography, 3D LiDAR, animation and video. All of it runs on one rule. Every clue is true, and every answer is in the record.

    Who we are

    Project Scroll is run by an independent researcher and author who writes narrative nonfiction about the history of the Great Lakes country, and who trained in visual communications. The research behind those books is the same research we offer you: old certificates, deeds, surveys, county histories and newspapers, read line by line and compared until they agree, or until we can say exactly why they don’t.

    What we offer

    Professional research. Lost ancestors, family histories and registers, family legends tested against the record, forgotten places, maps and land surveys, heirlooms, lodges and old societies, historical research for authors, films and local groups, and a second pair of eyes on anything you mean to publish. Research services.

    Imaging, design and video. Digital imaging of photographs, documents, maps and heirlooms; imaging analysis of faded ink, damaged pages and the details in old photographs; image creation and restoration; graphic design for family books, charts, covers and keepsakes; photography; and video production, from family-history films to recorded interviews.

    3D and living documents. Landscapes modeled in three dimensions from public LiDAR elevation surveys, 3D animations and fly-throughs, every reconstruction labeled as one, and living documents: research papers, timelines and family records that keep growing as new evidence turns up, with every change dated. The studio.

    Every commission starts with an honest first read: what can be done, and what it will take, before you commit to anything.

    What we believe

    • The record decides. Not the family tree online, not the story everybody tells, not what we hoped to find.
    • Every statement is marked. You can always see whether a fact comes from a document, a book, a family story or our own reading. How we mark the evidence.
    • Gaps are shown, not filled. An honest “not yet known” is worth more than a confident guess.
    • Corrections are welcome. If you know better, tell us. The record is bigger than any one of us.
    • Places and people are respected. The hunt never asks anyone to dig, to disturb a grave or a mound, or to go looking for anyone’s home.

    When we get something wrong

    We will. When a reader or a new document shows that a page of this site is mistaken, we change the page, date the change on it, and say what was wrong, rather than quietly replacing the words. A case that was called solved on a bad reading is reopened and says so. If you have found an error, tell us; corrections are credited to the person who sent them, by name or not, as they prefer.

    The record behind this site

    The dates on the scroll, the person of the month and the then-and-now maps are built from the same sources the hunt asks you to open: Rogers (1875), Mathews (1882), Glover (1906), the 1860 wall map at the Library of Congress, and Michigan death certificates at the Archives of Michigan. Where a page of this site makes a claim, the source is on the page or one click away. If it is not, that is a correction we owe.

    Where it started

    Project Scroll began in Cassopolis, Michigan, with a few questions the county history couldn’t answer. It doesn’t stop there. The hunt and the desk now take on questions from anywhere the records reach.
